# Study 0053 — Genesis 14:1–24 (KJV)
## Kings, conflict, captivity, rescue, Melchizedek, blessing, and refusal of Sodom's reward

**Primary passage:** Genesis 14:1–24 (KJV)  
**Previous study:** [Study 0052 — Genesis 13:1–18](0052-GENESIS-13-1-18.md)  
**Next passage:** Genesis 15:1–21 (KJV)

**Central question:** Who receives the credit for victory, and who has the authority to make Abram rich?

## 1. Passage structure

- Verses 1–12: Four eastern kings wage war against five kings of the Jordan plain; Lot is captured.
- Verses 13–16: Abram hears of Lot's capture, gathers his trained household men and allies, pursues the captors, and rescues Lot and the others.
- Verses 17–20: Abram meets the king of Sodom and Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of the most high God. Melchizedek blesses Abram and God; Abram gives him tithes.
- Verses 21–24: The king of Sodom offers Abram the goods while asking for the people. Abram refuses to enrich himself from Sodom, except for provisions consumed and the portions due to his allies.

## 2. Immediate observations

The chapter follows directly from Genesis 13. Lot had chosen the Jordan district and pitched his tent toward Sodom (13:10–13). Now war reaches the cities of the plain, and Lot is carried away with their possessions (14:11–12).

Important sequence:
1. Political subjection and rebellion.
2. Military campaign and defeat of the local kings.
3. Lot's capture.
4. A survivor reports the event to Abram.
5. Abram mobilizes his household and allies.
6. Abram rescues the captives and recovers goods.
7. Melchizedek interprets the victory as God's deliverance.
8. Abram refuses the king of Sodom's offer.

Do not confuse the narrator's description of Abram's military action with Melchizedek's theological attribution of the outcome to God. Both are present in the text.

## 3. The kings and the conflict — verses 1–12

The text names four kings in the eastern coalition: Amraphel of Shinar, Arioch of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer of Elam, and Tidal of nations. They fight five kings associated with Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim, and Bela/Zoar.

The five kings had served Chedorlaomer for twelve years and rebelled in the thirteenth. In the fourteenth year Chedorlaomer and his allies campaign through several territories and defeat peoples named in verses 5–7. The five local kings then meet the eastern coalition in the vale of Siddim.

The KJV identifies the vale of Siddim with the Salt Sea in verse 3. The text describes slimepits in the valley (v. 10); the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah flee, some fall there, and the survivors flee to the mountain. The victors take the goods and food of Sodom and Gomorrah and depart. Lot, Abram's brother's son, is taken with his goods because he dwells in Sodom (vv. 11–12).

**Text:** The chapter names the rulers, their alliances, the rebellion, the campaign, the defeat, the capture, and the plunder.

**Unresolved:** The exact identities of most kings, the precise locations of several ancient place names, the size of the armies, the date of the campaign, and the detailed topography of the vale are not established by the passage alone.

The names should not be confidently equated with famous rulers solely on resemblance. In particular, the older identification of Amraphel with Hammurabi is not established.

## 4. Lot's capture and Abram's response — verses 12–16

Verse 12 identifies Lot as Abram's brother's son and says he dwelt in Sodom. This follows the trajectory in Genesis 13:12, where Lot pitched his tent toward Sodom. The later narrative now says he dwelt in the city.

A man who escaped comes and tells Abram, who is dwelling by the oaks of Mamre the Amorite, brother of Eshcol and brother of Aner; these are confederates with Abram (v. 13). The narrator calls Abram “the Hebrew.”

Abram hears that his brother is taken captive. He arms or brings out 318 trained servants, born in his own house, and pursues the captors unto Dan (v. 14). He divides his forces against them by night, strikes them, pursues them unto Hobah, north of Damascus, and brings back all the goods, Lot, the women, and the people (vv. 15–16).

Several distinctions matter:
- Abram acts in response to news that Lot has been taken.
- The text gives a specific number, 318, for trained household men.
- Abram has named allies, Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre; verse 24 later acknowledges their portions.
- The narrative credits Abram's planning and action, while Melchizedek will identify God as the one who delivered the enemies into Abram's hand.

The term translated “trained” is rare; the text does not provide a full description of the household's military organization. The passage does not tell us the exact number of opposing troops or casualties.

## 5. Melchizedek appears — verses 17–20

After Abram returns from defeating Chedorlaomer and the kings with him, the king of Sodom goes out to meet him in the valley of Shaveh, the king's dale (v. 17).

Then Melchizedek, king of Salem, brings forth bread and wine. He is identified as “the priest of the most high God” (v. 18). He blesses Abram and says:

> Blessed be Abram of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth:

He continues:

> And blessed be the most high God, which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand.

Abram gives Melchizedek tithes of all (v. 20).

### What the text establishes

- Melchizedek is king of Salem.
- He is priest of the most high God.
- He brings bread and wine.
- He blesses Abram in the name of the most high God.
- He praises the most high God as possessor of heaven and earth.
- He attributes the deliverance of Abram's enemies to God.
- Abram gives Melchizedek tithes of all.

### What the text does not establish

Genesis 14 does not give Melchizedek's genealogy, parents, birth, death, or account of how he became priest or king. It does not explicitly say he is Shem, a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ, or any other named individual. Those identifications have appeared in later interpretation, but Genesis itself does not identify him that way.

Salem is traditionally identified with Jerusalem, and Psalm 76:2 associates Salem with God's tabernacle and Zion. The identification is a strong traditional reading, but the immediate verse names Salem without spelling out the modern location.

The name Melchizedek is commonly understood as “king of righteousness” or “my king is righteousness.” Hebrews 7:2 explicitly gives the interpretation “King of righteousness” and “King of Salem” as “King of peace.”

### Bread, wine, blessing, and tithe

The text says Melchizedek brought bread and wine; it does not specify a sacramental ceremony. The later Christian comparison with the Lord's Supper should not be substituted for the immediate narrative without explaining the later interpretive step.

The tithe is given by Abram to Melchizedek after the blessing. The verse says “tithes of all”; the exact accounting base is debated in later interpretation, with the context associating it with the recovered goods. Do not turn this one event into a complete law of tithing or assume every later tithe practice is defined here.

## 6. Abram refuses Sodom's goods — verses 21–24

The king of Sodom tells Abram to give him the persons and take the goods for himself (v. 21). Abram replies that he has lifted his hand unto the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth, and has sworn that he will take nothing belonging to the king of Sodom—not even a thread or a shoe-latchet—lest the king say, “I have made Abram rich” (vv. 22–23).

Abram makes an exception for what the young men have eaten and for the portion of the men who went with him: Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre. He says they may take their portion (v. 24).

This is a clear statement of attribution and independence:
- Abram acknowledges the LORD as most high God and possessor of heaven and earth.
- Abram refuses the king of Sodom's goods.
- Abram does not want Sodom's king to claim credit for making him rich.
- Abram does not deny his allies their agreed or appropriate share.

The text does not say that Abram rejects every possible form of property or that the rescued goods were all his personal property. His refusal is specifically framed in relation to the king of Sodom's offer and the claim the king might make.

## 8. Historical and geographical context

Genesis 14 has an unusually detailed list of kings, places, peoples, and military movements. Scholars debate the chapter's historical setting and the identification of its rulers and locations.

The broad ancient Near Eastern setting of city-states, regional coalitions, tribute, rebellion, and campaigns is historically intelligible. But no secure identification of all named kings or a matching campaign has been established. The chapter should not be presented as independently verified by an inscription that has not been identified.

Some place names are uncertain. “Dan” in verse 14 is often discussed as a later place-name update, since Judges 18:29 says the Danites renamed Laish as Dan. This raises a textual/geographical question about the form of the place name in the narrative; it does not erase the passage's report of Abram's pursuit.

The vale of Siddim is identified by the KJV with the Salt Sea. The exact ancient landscape and the relation of the slimepits to the geology of the Dead Sea region require separate geological and archaeological work. No precise geological mechanism is asserted here.

Melchizedek's Salem is traditionally identified with Jerusalem, especially in light of Psalm 76:2. The passage itself does not provide an archaeological identification of his palace, sanctuary, or person.

**Historical classification:** Contextual plausibility exists for regional kings, alliances, tribute, and military campaigns. The named rulers, exact chronology, route, and archaeological correlation remain uncertain. Historical uncertainty must not be converted into either a claim of proof or a claim that the narrative is disproved.

## 9. The Four GUA-W Questions

The four questions are recursive and mutually informative, not sequential stages.

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- How do we distinguish the human instrument of action from the ultimate source of deliverance?
- How do we record Abram's military action without ignoring Melchizedek's attribution to God?
- What does the passage actually say about Melchizedek, and what is supplied by later interpretation?
- How do we distinguish blessing, priesthood, kingship, and the giving of a tithe?
- Why does Abram refuse the king of Sodom's goods, and what does he explicitly say he wants to prevent?
- How do we preserve the distinction between an event and the later theological interpretation of that event?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The Source-to-Witness order helps distinguish:
- the LORD as possessor of heaven and earth;
- Abram as the human actor who mobilizes and pursues;
- Melchizedek as the priest-king who blesses and interprets the victory;
- the king of Sodom as the one who offers material goods;
- Abram as the one who refuses a claim that Sodom made him rich.

The map does not replace the text or decide Melchizedek's identity beyond what Scripture reveals.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

- Lot's choice in Genesis 13 places him in Sodom, and Genesis 14 records his capture.
- Abram responds with trained household men and allied support.
- Abram rescues Lot and the other captives and recovers goods.
- Melchizedek identifies himself as priest of the most high God and blesses Abram.
- Melchizedek attributes the deliverance to God.
- Abram gives tithes to Melchizedek.
- Abram refuses the king of Sodom's goods so the king cannot claim to have made Abram rich.
- Abram protects the portions of his allies.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A truthful Witness must report both the action and the attribution. It would be incomplete to report only Abram's skill and omit Melchizedek's declaration that God delivered the enemies into his hand. It would also be incomplete to report only divine deliverance while erasing Abram's actual mobilization, pursuit, and rescue.

The Witness must not invent Melchizedek's genealogy or identity. Later Scripture, especially Hebrews 7, gives further interpretation; it must be distinguished from what Genesis 14 itself states.

### How the questions inform one another

The passage requires a distinction between action, agency, and attribution. The human participant acts; the priest-king blesses and attributes the victory to God; Abram refuses a material arrangement that would misattribute the source of his wealth. GUA-W's governing order helps preserve these distinctions without diminishing the narrated human actions.

## 12. Triple Constraint review

### Biblical compliance — Pass

The study follows Genesis 14 in sequence and preserves the explicit distinctions among the kings' conflict, Lot's captivity, Abram's rescue, Melchizedek's blessing, and Abram's refusal. It distinguishes Genesis' account from later interpretation in Psalm 110 and Hebrews 5–7. It does not identify Melchizedek as Shem or as Christ as though Genesis explicitly said so.

### Scientific coherence — Contextual claims limited

The political and military setting is broadly intelligible in the ancient Near East, but the precise identities, dates, and locations are not independently established here. The study does not invent an archaeological inscription, campaign record, army size, or geological mechanism.

### GUA-W consistency — Pass

God is identified as possessor of heaven and earth and the one who delivers Abram's enemies. Abram's human action is retained without making him the ultimate Source. Abram refuses Sodom's claim to have made him rich. No architecture is redesigned.

**Result:** Admissible for this study. The identity of the kings, exact historical setting, geography of several sites, Melchizedek's genealogy, and the precise relationship of Genesis 14 to later priestly interpretation remain open where Scripture or evidence does not settle them.

## 13. Findings and unresolved questions

### Established by the KJV text

1. Four kings make war against five kings associated with the Jordan plain.
2. The five kings had served Chedorlaomer and rebelled.
3. The eastern coalition campaigns through named territories and defeats several peoples.
4. The kings of Sodom and Gomorrah flee; their goods and provisions are taken.
5. Lot is captured with his goods.
6. Abram hears of the capture and mobilizes 318 trained men born in his household.
7. Abram pursues the captors and rescues Lot, the people, and the goods.
8. Abram's allies are named.
9. Melchizedek is king of Salem and priest of the most high God.
10. Melchizedek brings bread and wine, blesses Abram, and blesses the most high God.
11. Melchizedek attributes the deliverance to God.
12. Abram gives Melchizedek tithes of all.
13. The king of Sodom offers Abram the goods.
14. Abram refuses the goods so the king of Sodom cannot claim to have made him rich.
15. Abram allows the young men’s consumed provisions and the portions of his allies.

### Supported observations

- Genesis 14 develops the consequence of Lot's move toward and into Sodom.
- Abram's rescue is an act of family intervention and involves his household and allies.
- The chapter records both human action and divine attribution; neither should be erased.
- Melchizedek's role is simultaneously royal and priestly in the text.
- Abram receives a blessing before giving the tithe in the narrative sequence.
- Abram's refusal is explicitly concerned with the source of his wealth and the claim the king of Sodom might make.
- Abram does not use his refusal to deprive his allies of their portion.
- Hebrews 7 is essential for the New Testament's interpretation of Melchizedek, but its claims must be identified as later canonical interpretation rather than inserted as unspoken details in Genesis.

### Unresolved questions carried forward

1. Who were Amraphel, Arioch, Chedorlaomer, and Tidal historically?
2. What are the secure geographical identifications of Ellasar, Ashteroth Karnaim, Ham, Shaveh Kiriathaim, El-paran, En-mishpat, Hazezon-tamar, and Hobah?
3. What is the date and historical setting of the campaign?
4. How should the reference to Dan in verse 14 be understood in relation to Judges 18:29?
5. What was the scale and organization of Abram's 318 trained household men?
6. What was the exact nature of the alliance between Abram and Mamre, Eshcol, and Aner?
7. What is the precise relationship between the king of Sodom's offer and the recovered goods?
8. What was the exact accounting basis of the “tithes of all”?
9. Is Salem securely identified with Jerusalem in this passage's original setting?
10. What does Genesis leave unstated about Melchizedek's origin, genealogy, priesthood, and death?
11. How do Genesis 14, Psalm 110, and Hebrews 5–7 develop the Melchizedek theme?
12. How should the bread and wine be understood in the immediate setting without importing later sacramental claims into the text?
13. How does the blessing of Melchizedek relate to the blessing promised to Abram in Genesis 12:2–3?
14. What is the textual relationship between “delivered” in Genesis 14:20 and “shield” in Genesis 15:1?

### What must not be claimed

- That the named kings have all been identified with specific known rulers.
- That Amraphel is certainly Hammurabi.
- That the exact campaign has been archaeologically verified.
- That the text supplies the size of the enemy armies or the number of casualties.
- That Melchizedek is explicitly Shem.
- That Melchizedek is explicitly identified as Jesus in Genesis 14.
- That the bread and wine are explicitly a sacrament in this passage.
- That Genesis provides Melchizedek's genealogy or death.
- That Abram's victory is attributed only to military skill; Melchizedek explicitly attributes deliverance to God.
- That Abram did nothing; the text records his mobilization and rescue.
- That Abram rejects his allies' portions; verse 24 expressly preserves them.
- That this one passage defines all later practices of tithing.
- That the exact historical geography or chronology is settled.

## 14. Continuity record

- **Current conclusion:** Genesis 14:1–24 records a regional war, Lot's capture, Abram's rescue with 318 trained household men and allied support, Melchizedek's appearance as king of Salem and priest of the most high God, Melchizedek's blessing and attribution of deliverance to God, Abram's tithe, and Abram's refusal of the king of Sodom's goods.
- **Central GUA-W distinction:** The human Witness/agent must not be confused with the Original Cause. Abram acts in the rescue, but Melchizedek declares that God delivered the enemies into Abram's hand. Abram refuses to let Sodom claim the source of his wealth.
- **Important textual caution:** Melchizedek's identity beyond the details given in Genesis is not settled by this passage; historical identifications of the kings and places remain uncertain; later interpretation in Psalm 110 and Hebrews 5–7 must be distinguished from Genesis' immediate wording.
