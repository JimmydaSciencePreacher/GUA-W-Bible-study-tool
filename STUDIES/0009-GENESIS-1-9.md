## 1. Passage text — KJV

> And God said, Let the waters under the heaven be gathered together unto one place, and let the dry land appear: and it was so.

The verse records:
1. God speaks.
2. Waters under the heaven are commanded to be gathered together unto one place.
3. Dry land is commanded to appear.
4. The account closes, “and it was so.”

This is a command followed in the same verse by the affirmation that it was so. Verse 10 will name the dry land and the gathered waters. Keep command, reported fulfillment, and subsequent naming distinct.

### A. “Let the waters be gathered” — קָוָה (*qavah*)

The form *yiqqavu* comes from the Hebrew root *qavah*. In this construction it is a Niphal imperfect, third-person masculine plural, functioning as a jussive/command in context: “let the waters be gathered” or “let the waters collect.”

The grammatical form presents the waters as the subject of the gathering command. It does not say that the waters independently decide or author the change. The speaker and initiating authority in the sentence is God.

The root *qavah* has a range of uses. Its meaning here is governed by the sentence: the waters are to be collected together. Do not build a physical mechanism from the root's wider dictionary range.

### B. “Under the heaven” — מִתַּחַת הַשָּׁמַיִם (*mittachat hashamayim*)

The verse specifies which waters are in view: those beneath the heaven. This links directly to Genesis 1:7–8, where waters are distinguished as beneath and above the expanse, and the expanse is called Heaven.

The command concerns the lower waters already distinguished in the preceding account. It does not say in this verse that the waters above the heaven are also gathered or removed.

### C. “Unto one place” — אֶל־מָקוֹם אֶחָד (*el-maqom echad*)

The phrase is composed of:
- *el* — to/toward/unto;
- *maqom* — place or location;
- *echad* — one.

The phrase explicitly describes the destination or resulting arrangement as “one place.” It does not, by itself, provide a modern map, coordinates, or a complete account of how the water bodies are distributed.

A question is created by the relation between this singular phrase in verse 9 and the plural “Seas” in verse 10. The singular may describe the collective domain or gathered destination, while the plural name may encompass multiple seas or divisions within that domain. That is a contextual possibility, not a conclusion to force before studying verse 10.

### D. “Let the dry land appear” — וְתֵרָאֶה הַיַּבָּשָׁה (*vetera'eh hayabbashah*)

The verb comes from *ra'ah*, “to see.” Here it is a Niphal imperfect, third-person feminine singular, functioning as a jussive: “let the dry land appear/be seen.”

The noun *yabbashah* means dry ground or dry land. The contrast is with the waters. The verse commands that the dry land become visible; it does not describe the detailed geological mechanism by which the land and water arrangement is established.

The text's wording emphasizes appearance/visibility of dry land, not a technical account of uplift, subsidence, drainage, crustal formation, or ocean-basin development.

### E. “And it was so” — וַיְהִי־כֵן (*wayhi-ken*)

The phrase closes the command with the account's affirmation that it was so. Verse 10 then reports God's naming of the dry land and gathered waters. The narrative sequence distinguishes the command and its fulfillment from the naming that follows.

## 3. Immediate literary sequence: Genesis 1:7–10

| Verse | Textual movement | What is stated |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 1:7 | Making and separation | God makes the expanse and divides waters beneath from waters above. |
| Genesis 1:8 | Naming and day marker | God calls the expanse Heaven; the second day closes. |
| Genesis 1:9 | Command and fulfillment | Lower waters are gathered unto one place; dry land appears; “it was so.” |
| Genesis 1:10 | Naming and evaluation | God calls the dry land Earth and the gathered waters Seas; God sees that it is good. |

### Observations

1. Genesis 1:9 continues the arrangement of waters begun in verses 6–7. It specifies the waters beneath the heaven.
2. The command has two coordinated outcomes: the waters are gathered and dry land appears.
3. Verse 9 uses “one place”; verse 10 calls the gathered waters “Seas” (plural).
4. Verse 10 supplies the names “Earth” and “Seas”; those names should not be inserted into verse 9 as if already stated there.
5. The “and it was so” formula appears at the end of verse 9. Verse 10 separately records God's naming and evaluation.

### Open question: “one place” and “Seas”

The singular “one place” and plural “Seas” need to be read together. They are not necessarily contradictory: a collective gathered region can contain distinct seas, and a plural name can describe the waters collectively. But the text's precise geographical conception should not be settled from grammar alone.

The NET Bible note interprets the phrase as the waters being restricted to an area to form the ocean. Other commentators have proposed a single surrounding sea or a collective water domain. These are interpretations of the picture, not additional words in the Hebrew.

## 4. Scripture cross-references

### Genesis 1:6–8
The waters beneath the heaven are distinguished from the waters above the expanse. Genesis 1:9 now addresses the lower waters specifically. It does not say that the upper waters are gathered into this one place.

### Genesis 1:10
God calls the dry land Earth and the gathering of waters Seas. The plural “Seas” is essential context for understanding “one place” in verse 9. Study the naming and evaluation in verse 10 as a separate passage.

### Genesis 1:11–13
Vegetation appears on the dry land in the next part of the third-day account. This establishes the narrative importance of the dry land as the setting for the next commanded development. Do not import the seed/vegetation content into verse 9 before examining its own wording.

### Psalm 33:6–9
The psalm connects the heavens and their host with the word of the LORD, and speaks of waters being gathered as a heap and the depths being laid up in storehouses. This is a canonical poetic witness to God's command over waters. Its poetic language should not be converted uncritically into a technical geological mechanism.

### Psalm 104:5–9
The psalm describes the earth, waters covering it, mountains rising, valleys descending, and boundaries set so that waters do not return to cover the earth. This is a particularly relevant canonical parallel to Genesis 1:9–10. Examine the poem's sequence and imagery; it may illuminate the biblical witness to boundaries, but it does not settle the exact physical mechanism in Genesis 1:9 by itself.

### Job 38:8–11
The sea is pictured as emerging from the womb, being bounded by doors, and receiving a limit. This passage emphasizes God's authority over the sea and its boundary. Its poetic personification must be respected.

### Proverbs 8:27–29
Wisdom's speech describes God's ordering of the heavens, the deep, the sea, and the limits of the waters. This is a related canonical reflection on ordered boundaries, but it is poetic personification and should not be treated as a geological report.

### Jeremiah 5:22
The LORD sets the sand as a boundary for the sea by a perpetual decree. This later passage supports the biblical theme of God's authority over the sea's limits.

### Exodus 14:21–22; Joshua 3:14–17
These passages describe dry ground appearing or being traversed amid waters during the Exodus and Jordan crossing. They are later historical narratives of extraordinary water/land separation. They provide a useful lexical and thematic comparison for *yabbashah* (“dry ground”), but the events are not identical to Genesis 1:9.

## 5. Creation investigation: water domains, topography, and exposed land

### 5.1 What present Earth science observes

The present Earth has a differentiated solid surface with continental land and ocean basins. The ocean covers more than 70 percent of Earth's surface. The seafloor is not a featureless container: it includes mountains, canyons, plateaus, continental shelves, slopes, abyssal plains, and other varied structures.

The U.S. Geological Survey explains that bathymetry measures the elevation/depth of the Earth's surface beneath water, especially the ocean. Seafloor mapping uses acoustic methods, samples, video, photographs, and other observations to characterize underwater topography and geology. The USGS also notes that less than 10 percent of the seafloor has been mapped globally at high resolution. Thus the broad existence of ocean basins is well established, while detailed knowledge of the seafloor remains incomplete.

- USGS, “Bathymetry and Elevation”: https://cmgds.marine.usgs.gov/catalog/science.php?term=22&thcode=23
- USGS, “Marine Geology”: https://www.usgs.gov/science/science-explorer/ocean/marine-geology
- USGS, “Ocean” FAQs: https://www.usgs.gov/science/faqs/ocean

### 5.2 What the evidence supports

Present observations support these statements:
- Water occupies large connected ocean basins as well as lakes, rivers, groundwater systems, ice, and atmospheric reservoirs.
- Land and seafloor have measurable topography; elevations and depths vary substantially.
- Bathymetry allows scientists to map underwater relief and investigate the structure and composition of the seafloor.
- Water and land boundaries are dynamic at coastlines and in river, lake, sedimentary, and tectonic environments.

These observations make the relationship between gathered waters and exposed dry land a legitimate subject for comparison with Genesis 1:9. They do not demonstrate that the present global arrangement is identical to the initial arrangement narrated in Genesis.

### 5.3 What the science does not establish about this verse

Present-day bathymetry and geology do not directly observe the event described in Genesis 1:9. They do not, by themselves, establish:
- The exact initial distribution of waters in the narrated event.
- Whether “one place” refers to one continuous oceanic domain, a collective basin arrangement, or another ancient geographical picture.
- Whether land appeared primarily by uplift, water recession, subsidence of basins, or a combination of processes in the event described.
- The timing or rate of the narrated change.
- Whether the present continents and ocean basins correspond directly to the land/water arrangement at that point in the Genesis sequence.

The text gives the command and outcome; it does not specify the physical mechanism. Modern geology investigates Earth's observable structure and history using evidence and models. Neither should be made to answer what it cannot establish.

### 5.4 Comparison classification

**Relationship:** Comparable relationship, with important unresolved historical and physical details.

Genesis 1:9 describes waters gathered and dry land appearing. Present Earth science observes large water domains, exposed continental land, and topographic basins. Both concern a differentiated land/water arrangement. This is a legitimate subject-level comparison, not proof that the present geography is a direct snapshot of the Genesis event or that one modern mechanism is the one described in the verse.
