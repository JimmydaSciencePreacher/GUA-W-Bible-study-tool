# Study 0022 — Genesis 2:15–17
## The man's vocation, God's provision, the command, and the boundary

**Study status:** Genesis 2:15–17 investigated and recorded as a unified Scripture-and-Creation study. The LORD God takes the man and places him in the Garden of Eden to dress and keep it. God gives a broad permission concerning the trees of the Garden, then gives a specific prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, with the stated consequence: “thou shalt surely die.” The passage establishes provision, vocation, command, boundary, and consequence. It does not by itself settle every question about the timing or full nature of death.

## 1. Passage text — KJV

> **Genesis 2:15**  
> And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.
>
> **Genesis 2:16**  
> And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:
>
> **Genesis 2:17**  
> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

### Immediate observations

1. The LORD God is the acting subject: He takes the man and puts him in the Garden.
2. The man receives an assignment: “to dress it and to keep it.”
3. God speaks a command to the man.
4. The permission is broad: “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat.”
5. The prohibition is specific: the man is not to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
6. The command includes a stated reason or consequence: “in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.”
7. Provision and prohibition appear together. The prohibition does not erase the breadth of the permission.
8. The man is placed in an environment he did not originate, given work within it, and addressed by the One who placed him there.

The text distinguishes the man's work from God's originating action. The man is entrusted with a task in the Garden; he is not described as the Garden's author or owner in the ultimate sense.

## 2. Literary and canonical context

### 2.1 The sequence from Genesis 2:8–17

- Genesis 2:8: God plants the Garden and places the man there.
- Genesis 2:9: God causes trees to grow, including the two specially named trees.
- Genesis 2:10–14: the river waters the Garden; four heads and their names are described.
- Genesis 2:15: God puts the man in the Garden to dress and keep it.
- Genesis 2:16–17: God gives the command, broad permission, specific prohibition, and consequence.

The man receives a prepared place, provision, vocation, and instruction. These are not interchangeable. The text presents them in a meaningful narrative sequence.

### 2.2 Genesis 1:26–30 — dominion, fruitfulness, and provision

Genesis 1:26–28 describes humanity's image-bearing role and dominion. Genesis 1:29–30 records God's provision of food. Genesis 2:15–17 focuses on the man in the Garden, his work there, and the specific command.

The Garden assignment can be read in relation to humanity's broader commission, but the two passages should not be collapsed into identical wording. Genesis 2 adds a particular place, task, permission, prohibition, and consequence.

### 2.3 “Dress” and “keep” — vocation before transgression

The verbs translated “dress” and “keep” are important. They describe work and care within the Garden before Genesis 3. Work itself is therefore not introduced as a punishment after the Fall. The character, conditions, and difficulty of human labor change in the later account (Genesis 3:17–19), but Genesis 2:15 already gives the man a meaningful assignment.

Do not automatically read every later association of labor, toil, or guarding back into these two verbs. Their range and force must be evaluated in context.

### 2.4 Permission and prohibition

Genesis 2:16 begins with a broad permission: the man may freely eat of every tree of the Garden. Genesis 2:17 introduces a specific exception. The text does not present the man as receiving no provision or as being surrounded only by restrictions.

The command is not merely information about a tree. It is an instruction from God to the man, with a stated boundary and consequence. Genesis 3 will show the serpent questioning God's word, reframing the prohibition, and drawing the woman and man into transgression. That later account must be studied in its own words and sequence.

### 2.5 The consequence: “thou shalt surely die”

The Hebrew construction commonly transliterated *mot tamut* combines an infinitive absolute with a finite verb from the same root, intensifying or emphasizing the certainty of the statement: “you shall surely die.” The wording makes the consequence emphatic.

The phrase “in the day that thou eatest thereof” must be considered with the whole canonical account. Genesis 2:17 states the consequence; Genesis 3 narrates the transgression and its consequences, including alienation, curse, mortality, and exclusion from the tree of life. The expression “in the day” should not be casually reduced to a modern 24-hour clock claim without examining Hebrew usage and the narrative context. Nor should the warning be weakened: the text emphatically states that death follows eating.

The precise relationship among the stated day, the onset of death, spiritual separation, and eventual physical death is a theological question requiring careful canonical examination. This study records the question rather than pretending Genesis 2:17 alone supplies every detail.

### A. “Took” — *laqach*

The verb *laqach* means to take. Genesis 2:15 says the LORD God took the man and put him in the Garden. The verse emphasizes God's initiative and placement. It does not describe the man independently choosing or establishing his own location.

### B. “Put” — *nuach*

The verb is associated with placing, setting down, or causing to rest, depending on form and context. The KJV renders it “put.” The point in this sentence is that the LORD God places the man in the Garden for the stated assignment.

Do not build a complete doctrine of rest from the root alone. The immediate syntax and context govern the meaning here.

### C. “Dress” — *avad*

The Hebrew verb *avad* has a broad range including work, serve, and labor; in an agricultural context it can mean cultivate or work the ground. “Dress” in the KJV is older English for tending or cultivating in this setting, not clothing the Garden.

This verb is also used in contexts of service. That wider range invites careful study of the relationship between human work and service, but it does not mean that every occurrence should be translated identically.

### D. “Keep” — *shamar*

The verb *shamar* can mean keep, guard, watch, preserve, or observe. In Genesis 2:15 it is paired with *avad*. The pair can be rendered in sense as working/cultivating and keeping/guarding the Garden.

The text gives the man responsibility within the Garden. The precise scope of what he is to guard, and how that responsibility relates to the later serpent encounter, is a question for the full narrative, not a settled conclusion from the verb alone.

### E. “Commanded” — *tsavah*

The verse explicitly says the LORD God commanded the man. This is not merely the man's observation of a natural regularity. It is a spoken directive from the Author to the human recipient.

This distinction matters: a physical constraint describes what happens under conditions; a command addresses an agent with an instruction and responsibility. These categories may be compared, but they must not be confused.

### F. “Thou mayest freely eat” — *akol tokel*

The Hebrew construction uses the infinitive absolute with the finite verb *akal*, intensifying the permission. The KJV's “freely eat” captures this emphatic permission. The man is given access to the trees of the Garden broadly, subject to the specific exception that follows.

The verse's generosity must be kept visible when interpreting the prohibition in verse 17.

### G. “Thou shalt not eat” — prohibition

The prohibition is directed to the man and concerns the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The command is specific. The text does not say that the man is prohibited from eating all fruit or all trees.

### H. “Thou shalt surely die” — *mot tamut*

The construction is emphatic. It should not be weakened into “you might die” or treated as an uncertain possibility. The exact relation between the phrase “in the day,” the immediate consequence of transgression, and the later unfolding of death must be examined across Genesis 3 and the rest of Scripture.

## 4. Creation investigation

### 4.1 Investigation question

What can the Garden assignment teach us to investigate about cultivation, ecological care, responsibility, boundaries, and the relationship between an instruction and the conditions of a living environment? How can these Creation observations be considered without reducing God's command or the stated consequence to a merely physical mechanism?

### 4.2 Cultivation and plant systems

Agriculture, horticulture, soil science, and plant biology study how plants grow and how humans cultivate them. Relevant factors include:
- Soil structure, texture, organic matter, and nutrient availability.
- Water infiltration, retention, drainage, and irrigation.
- Light, temperature, and atmospheric conditions.
- Plant reproduction, growth, disease, and interactions with other organisms.
- The effects of cultivation practices on soil and surrounding ecosystems.

Genesis 2:15 assigns the man work in the Garden before the Fall. Present-day cultivation provides observable examples of work within a living environment. It does not establish the precise agricultural methods used in Eden or the complete ecological condition of the original Garden.

### 4.3 Keeping, guarding, and stewardship

Ecology and conservation science investigate how environments are maintained, how disturbances affect systems, and how human activity changes habitats. Soil erosion, nutrient depletion, invasive species, water contamination, habitat fragmentation, and loss of biodiversity are measurable concerns in present environments.

These observations can help clarify what responsible care of a place may involve. They do not, by themselves, define the full theological meaning of *shamar* or prove that Genesis 2:15 is a modern conservation policy. The text's own assignment remains primary.

### 4.4 Boundaries in living systems

Living systems have conditions and limits. A plant's growth depends on suitable ranges of water, temperature, light, nutrients, and other factors. Ecosystems also exhibit thresholds, feedback, and consequences when conditions change.

These are descriptive features of Creation. Genesis 2:17, however, is a moral and personal command from God. The prohibition is not merely a physical threshold discovered by observing a tree. A scientific description of toxicity, nutrition, or biological response would not explain away the command's authority or the stated consequence.

A useful comparison must therefore distinguish:
- **Physical condition:** a measurable condition affecting a system.
- **Natural regularity:** a repeatable pattern described by scientific methods.
- **Personal command:** an instruction issued by an authority to an agent.
- **Moral boundary:** a distinction between what is permitted and what is prohibited.
- **Consequence:** what the text states follows transgression.

They may be compared as categories of order and constraint, but they are not interchangeable.

### 4.5 Causality and consequence

Science investigates causal relationships through observation, measurement, controlled tests where possible, and explanatory models. Genesis 2:17 presents a divine warning with an emphatic consequence. The two kinds of inquiry must not be collapsed.

Scientific methods may investigate biological aspects of mortality and the processes by which organisms die. They cannot, by themselves, determine the full theological meaning of the death announced in God's command. Conversely, theological interpretation must not misstate biological evidence when making claims about physical processes.

### 4.6 Work before the Fall

Genesis 2:15 is prior in the narrative to Genesis 3. Therefore, work and responsibility in the Garden are not introduced as a punishment for sin. Genesis 3:17–19 later describes the ground being cursed and human labor becoming painful and difficult. A careful comparison should distinguish assigned work in Genesis 2 from toil under the conditions described after transgression.
