## 2. Structure and movement

The passage contains two connected movements.

### Verses 1–4: A troubling account before the flood
- Humanity multiplies.
- Daughters are born.
- The “sons of God” see the daughters of men and take wives.
- The LORD speaks concerning His Spirit, humanity as flesh, and 120 years.
- The Nephilim are mentioned alongside the mighty men of old and men of renown.

### Verses 5–8: God's assessment, grief, judgment, and grace
- God sees the great wickedness of humanity.
- The thoughts and intentions of the human heart are described as continually evil.
- The LORD is grieved and declares judgment.
- Noah finds grace in the LORD's eyes.

**Narrative movement:** Human multiplication is not itself identified as wickedness. The decisive assessment appears in verse 5: the great wickedness of humanity and the continual evil of its thoughts and intentions. Verse 8 introduces grace before the flood account proceeds.

Do not assume that every detail in verses 1–4 is fully explained by verses 5–8. The connection is strong in the final narrative, but the identity of the “sons of God” and the precise role of the Nephilim remain disputed.

## 3. Verse-by-verse investigation

### 3.1 Humanity multiplies — verse 1

The verse begins with the multiplication of *ha-adam* — humanity/the human population — on the face of the ground/earth. Daughters are born.

The wording recalls the Creation blessing and commission to be fruitful and multiply (Genesis 1:28). Human multiplication is not condemned in verse 1. The verse establishes the setting for what follows.

The text does not give a population number, geographic distribution, or a chronology for the births. It does not say that the birth of daughters is itself a problem.

**Observation:** Human population growth and the birth of daughters provide the narrative setting. The moral assessment comes later.

### 3.2 “The sons of God” and “the daughters of men” — verse 2

The Hebrew expression translated “sons of God” is *bene ha-Elohim* (בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים). The same or closely related expression appears in Job 1:6, 2:1, and 38:7, where it is commonly understood as referring to heavenly/divine beings. That usage is an important argument for the divine-being interpretation in Genesis 6, though interpreters dispute whether the expression must have the same referent in every context.

The verse says these “sons of God” saw that the daughters of men were fair/good and took wives from among those they chose.

The Hebrew verb *laqach* means “take.” The phrase “they took them wives” is ordinary marriage language elsewhere in the Old Testament, but the phrase “of all which they chose” raises the question of power, desire, and unrestricted choice in this context. The text does not explicitly state that the women consented or describe the social circumstances.

Three major interpretive families must be preserved:

**A. Divine-being / heavenly-being interpretation**

- “Sons of God” refers to heavenly beings, with Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7 providing the strongest direct lexical comparison.
- The narrative describes these beings taking human women.
- This reading has ancient Jewish interpretive support, notably the Watchers tradition reflected in 1 Enoch 6–7.
- Difficulties include explaining the nature of the union, how the beings act in the human realm, and the relationship of the passage to later biblical statements about angels.

**B. Sethite interpretation**

- “Sons of God” refers to men from the line of Seth, associated with the godly line in Genesis 4:25–5:32.
- “Daughters of men” is interpreted as women from Cain's line or humanity considered in its fallen condition.
- This reading places the passage within the two genealogies immediately preceding it.
- Difficulties include the fact that Genesis 6 does not explicitly identify the men as descendants of Seth or the women as descendants of Cain. The phrase “sons of God” is also used for heavenly beings in Job.

**C. Royal-ruler / tyrant interpretation**

- “Sons of God” refers to powerful rulers or kings who claimed divine status, divine sonship, or sacral authority.
- Their taking of women “of all which they chose” is read as royal appropriation, coercion, or the abuse of power.
- This interpretation can connect the passage to ancient royal ideology and the taking of women by powerful men.
- Difficulties include the absence of an explicit word for kings or rulers in the passage and the need to explain the specific expression “sons of God.”

**Disposition:** The divine-being reading has a strong lexical parallel in Job; the Sethite and royal-ruler readings offer different contextual and social explanations. The passage's identity question remains disputed. Do not report any one interpretation as an uncontested fact or use later traditions as if they were the wording of Genesis.

### 3.3 The LORD's Spirit and 120 years — verse 3

The KJV reads:

> My spirit shall not always strive with man, for that he also is flesh: yet his days shall be an hundred and twenty years.

Two Hebrew expressions are especially difficult.

The verb translated “strive” is *yadon* (יָדוֹן), a rare form occurring only here in the Hebrew Bible. Its derivation and precise meaning are disputed. English versions and commentators have proposed ideas such as strive, contend, remain, abide, or continue. Because the word is rare, confident claims about its exact nuance should be avoided.

The phrase translated “for that he also is flesh” identifies humanity as *basar* — flesh. The verse contrasts God's Spirit with human creatureliness/frailty. The text does not say that being flesh is itself a moral crime. The moral indictment is stated in verse 5.

**Two main readings of the 120 years:**

1. **A period of reprieve before the flood:** God declares that a limited period remains before judgment. This fits the immediate movement toward the flood and the subsequent narrative.
2. **A limit on human lifespan:** The statement establishes a maximum or general limit for human life. This is often inferred from the number, though Genesis itself does not explicitly call it a universal maximum. Later biblical figures, including Moses at 120 years (Deuteronomy 34:7), are sometimes discussed in relation to this reading.

The immediate context favors reading the statement as a countdown or period of divine patience before the flood, but the interpretation is not beyond dispute. Genesis 11 and later biblical genealogies also require care before claiming that 120 became an immediate and absolute biological ceiling.

**Disposition:** Preserve the number 120. Mark its referent as disputed. Do not present it as a scientifically established lifespan limit or as a precisely dated countdown unless the interpretation is explicitly identified as such.

### 3.4 The Nephilim and mighty men — verse 4

The Hebrew word *Nephilim* (נְפִילִים) is retained by transliteration because its precise derivation and meaning are uncertain. The KJV translates it “giants,” following an interpretive tradition. The word is also used in Numbers 13:33, in the report of the spies concerning the Anakim.

Genesis 6:4 states:
- the Nephilim were on the earth in those days;
- and also afterward;
- the sons of God came in to the daughters of men;
- the women bore children;
- “the same” became mighty men of old, men of renown.

The Hebrew *gibborim* means mighty men, warriors, or powerful ones. “Men of name” is a literal rendering of *anshe hashem*, commonly understood as famous or renowned men.

**Important grammatical caution:** The verse places the Nephilim and the unions in the same time setting, then describes the children as mighty men of old and men of renown. The syntax is debated. It does not unambiguously state in a simple sentence that “the Nephilim were the offspring” of the unions. Avoid turning a possible implication into an explicit quotation.

The verse does not provide measurements, biological descriptions, or a scientific classification for the Nephilim. “Giants” is a traditional translation, but the Hebrew term itself remains uncertain.

The phrase “and also after that” has generated questions because the flood narrative later describes the destruction of life outside Noah's household. Numbers 13:33 uses the term again in the spies' report. The relationship between the pre-flood Nephilim and the later report is not explained in Genesis 6:4. Do not invent a survival mechanism or assume a continuous biological lineage without evidence.

**Disposition:** The Nephilim were present in the period described; the text associates the episode with mighty men of old and men of renown. Their exact identity, physical characteristics, origin, and relation to later Nephilim references remain unresolved.

### 3.5 God's assessment of human wickedness — verse 5

The passage now states the moral condition directly:

> And GOD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually.

The Hebrew wording intensifies the statement:
- *ra'ah* — evil or wickedness;
- *kol-yetser machshevot libbo* — every formation, inclination, or intention of the thoughts of the heart;
- *raq ra' kol-hayom* — only evil continually/all the day.

The verse is not merely describing isolated acts. It describes the pervasive orientation of human thought and intention.

Compare:
- Genesis 3:6 — the woman's evaluation and desire in the temptation.
- Genesis 4:7 — sin at the door and the question of rule.
- Genesis 4:8 — Cain's murder of Abel.
- Genesis 4:23–24 — Lamech's violent boast.
- Genesis 5 — the repeated death formula.
- Genesis 6:11–13 — the earth filled with violence, which continues the assessment in the next passage.
- Matthew 15:19 — evil thoughts and actions proceeding from the heart.
- Romans 1:21–32 — human thought and conduct in rebellion against God.

**Textual distinction:** Verse 1's multiplication is not the stated cause of judgment. Verse 5 gives God's explicit assessment of human wickedness. The text does not say that population growth, marriage, sexual reproduction, or the existence of mighty men alone explains the judgment.

### 3.6 The LORD repents and is grieved — verse 6

The KJV says:

> And it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart.

The Hebrew verb translated “repented” is from the root *nacham* (נחם), here in a form commonly associated with being sorry, regretting, relenting, or being moved to grief. The second clause says it grieved Him at His heart.

The language is deeply anthropopathic: it communicates God's response in language humans can understand. The text presents God's response as real and morally serious. It does not invite the reader to treat God as indifferent to evil.

A necessary canonical comparison:
- Numbers 23:19 says God is not a man, that He should lie, nor a son of man, that He should repent.
- 1 Samuel 15:11 says the LORD regrets/mourns that He made Saul king; 1 Samuel 15:29 says the Glory of Israel will not lie or repent as a human does.
- 1 Samuel 15:35 again uses the language of the LORD's regret concerning Saul.
- Jonah 3:10 describes God relenting from announced judgment when the Ninevites turn from evil.
- Jeremiah 18:7–10 describes God's announced judgment and relenting in relation to a nation's conduct.

These texts show that Scripture uses *nacham* language in more than one setting. The word cannot be forced into a single English gloss in every passage.

Genesis 6:6 does not say God discovered information He did not know, made an error in creating humanity, or ceased to be God. Nor should the emotional force be erased by saying the verse means nothing more than a change in human circumstances. It says God was grieved by what humanity had become.

**Disposition:** The text genuinely portrays divine grief and regret in response to human wickedness. The manner in which this language relates to God's unchanging knowledge and purpose must be read canonically, not resolved by denying either the grief language or the other passages.

### 3.7 Judgment declared — verse 7

The LORD declares that He will destroy humanity from the face of the earth, and also names beasts, creeping things, and birds.

The language returns to the created categories of Genesis 1. The same Creator who made living creatures now announces a judgment affecting them.

The verse says “for it repenteth me that I have made them,” repeating the *nacham* root and extending the statement to the living creatures named.

The passage does not yet describe the flood's mechanics, its extent, or the precise sequence of events. Those details belong to the following narrative.

The judgment is not presented as arbitrary in the immediate context: verse 5 has just stated God's assessment of pervasive human wickedness. Verse 7 is the announced response.

### 3.8 Noah finds grace — verse 8

The verse is short:

> But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD.

The Hebrew word translated “grace” is *chen* (חֵן), favor or gracious acceptance.

The opening word “But” (Hebrew *we-noach*) sharply contrasts Noah with the preceding description of humanity. The narrative does not say Noah earned God's favor through a system of merit. It says he found grace in the LORD's eyes.

The next verse, Genesis 6:9, will describe Noah as just, perfect/blameless in his generations, and one who walked with God. The order matters: verse 8 states grace; verse 9 describes Noah. Do not reverse the order and turn grace into a wage earned by the description in verse 9.

Compare:
- Genesis 6:9 — Noah is described as just and walking with God.
- Exodus 33:12–17 — Moses finds grace in God's sight.
- Ephesians 2:8–10 — salvation by grace through faith, not of works, with good works following.
- Hebrews 11:7 — Noah acts by faith in response to God's warning.

**Disposition:** Grace is explicitly present before the flood narrative unfolds. The text does not define it as a human achievement or a reward purchased by Noah.

## 5. Creation and scientific investigation

This passage raises claims about heavenly beings, human reproduction, extraordinary figures, population, moral conduct, and divine action. We must distinguish what science can investigate from what is a theological or textual claim.

### 5.1 Human multiplication and population

Population science can investigate demographic growth, fertility, mortality, age structure, and migration in populations for which evidence exists. Archaeology can investigate settlement density, food systems, material remains, and changes in human communities.

Genesis 6:1 does not provide a count, date, region, or demographic series. No specific population-growth rate can be calculated from the verse.

**Disposition:** Population growth is a scientifically investigable phenomenon. Genesis 6:1 gives no numerical demographic data to test.

### 5.2 The sons of God and the Nephilim

The identity of heavenly beings, their possible interaction with humans, and the identity of the Nephilim are primarily questions of textual interpretation and theology in this passage. No independently established scientific dataset identifies the “sons of God” or the Nephilim of Genesis 6.

Archaeological evidence for ancient stories about divine beings, heroes, rulers, or giants can illuminate the cultural world in which ancient texts were read. It cannot by itself prove that a particular interpretation of Genesis is true, identify the biblical figures, or establish the biological mechanics of the episode.

Modern genetics and human biology can investigate human reproduction and biological variation. They do not supply evidence for a human–angel hybrid category, and the biblical text does not give a biological description that could be tested as such.

**Disposition:** Do not claim scientific confirmation or scientific disproof of the supernatural interpretation from evidence that does not address the specific textual claim. Keep textual interpretation, ancient cultural comparison, and biological observation distinct.

### 5.3 Human violence and social power

Anthropology, history, criminology, and archaeology can investigate violence, coercive power, warfare, inequality, and the social consequences of domination. Evidence may include skeletal trauma, weapons, settlement destruction, burial patterns, and written records.

Such evidence can illuminate the reality and consequences of human violence in particular populations and periods. It cannot independently measure the claim that “every imagination” of the human heart was continually evil across the entire population described in Genesis 6:5.

The following passage, Genesis 6:11–13, explicitly says the earth was filled with violence. That will provide a closer textual focus for investigating violence and its material traces.

**Disposition:** Scientific and historical disciplines can study human violence in documented contexts. They cannot turn the theological scope of Genesis 6:5 into a statistical conclusion without evidence the passage does not supply.

### 5.4 Divine grief, judgment, and grace

Science can investigate human emotional experience and the biological processes associated with grief, but Genesis 6:6 is a theological statement about God's response. It is not a claim about human neurobiology.

Likewise, divine judgment and grace are theological claims, not laboratory variables. Scientific method neither supplies a physical mechanism for God's grief nor measures grace.

**Disposition:** Do not force a scientific analogy onto the divine actions described. Preserve the text's theological claims and examine their language and canonical context.

## 7. The Four Questions

- **Distinctions:** Multiplication versus wickedness; seeing versus choosing; human desire versus divine assessment; text versus interpretation; divine grief versus human ignorance; judgment versus arbitrary action; grace versus earned reward.
- **Boundary:** GUA-W does not decide the sons-of-God question by inventing an architecture, and it does not convert the Nephilim into a scientific category. No GUA-W structural rule is added or changed.
- **Continuity:** Genesis 1:28 supplies the blessing of multiplication; Genesis 3–5 records the spread of human disobedience, violence, toil, and death; Genesis 6:9–22 will introduce Noah's generations and the preparation for the flood.

The central hierarchy remains: God alone is Original Cause. The derivative cannot alter the Source. The Witness reports what is given and does not become the Author.

5. What is the derivation and precise meaning of *Nephilim*?

7. What does “and also after that” mean in the verse's literary and chronological setting?
8. How should Genesis 6:6's divine grief be related to Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29?
9. What precise relationship does Genesis 6 establish between the unions in verses 1–4 and the pervasive wickedness in verse 5?
10. How does grace in verse 8 relate to Noah's description and faith in Genesis 6:9 and Hebrews 11:7?

### Do not claim yet
- That the sons of God are certainly angels, Sethites, or kings.
- That the daughters of men are certainly Cain's descendants.
- That all the women consented or that the passage explicitly narrates assault.
- That 120 years is certainly a universal lifespan ceiling.
- That 120 years is certainly a precisely measured countdown.
- That the Nephilim are a scientifically verified species or biological category.
- That the Nephilim are explicitly and unambiguously the offspring of the unions.
- That a post-flood survival mechanism for Nephilim has been established.
- That God's grief means God was surprised, ignorant, or mistaken.
- That God's grief is merely figurative and carries no real meaning.
- That Noah earned grace.
- That archaeology or genetics has identified the beings or individuals in the passage.
