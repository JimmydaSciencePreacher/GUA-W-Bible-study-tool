## 2. First observations from the text

### Genesis 1:1

**Text**
- “In the beginning” establishes the opening temporal frame.
- “God” is the grammatical subject.
- “Created” is the action attributed to God.
- “The heaven and the earth” are the objects of the action.

**Observation**
- The sentence places God as the acting subject and heaven and earth as that which God created.
- The verse does not name another creator, source, or co-equal originating cause.

**Initial inference**
- The account begins by attributing the created order to God. This is consistent with the GUA-W governing distinction, **Author → Word → Creation → Witness**.
- The verse alone does not spell out every operation or mechanism by which the created order is brought forth. Those questions must be investigated as the account proceeds.

### Genesis 1:2

**Text**
The verse describes:
1. The earth as “without form, and void.”
2. Darkness upon the face of the deep.
3. The Spirit of God moving upon the face of the waters.

**Observations**
- The earth is already the named subject from verse 1 and is now described by its condition.
- The verse names the deep and the waters and relates darkness and the Spirit's movement to their “face.”
- The verse does not say that the earth is evil, sinful, defective, or the result of a prior judgment.
- The verse does not yet explain the physical nature, origin, or scientific composition of the deep or the waters.

**Initial inference**
- Genesis 1:2 describes a condition within the opening creation account and introduces the Spirit of God before the first recorded command, “Let there be light” (1:3).
- Whether verse 2 is a parenthetical description of the initial created condition, a temporal circumstance, or part of a different grammatical construction is a question of Hebrew syntax and literary structure. It must not be settled by assumption.

## 3. Hebrew language investigation

The Masoretic Hebrew text and transliteration are available in the University of Toronto's research guide for Genesis 1:1–2. The guide also places the KJV beside a materially different JPS rendering, illustrating that the opening syntax has been translated in more than one way.

### Genesis 1:1 terms

- **בְּרֵאשִׁית — bəreshit:** “In the beginning” in the KJV. The construction and the status of *reshit* have been debated. A 2025 *Journal of Biblical Literature* study argues that the lack of a definite article does not, by itself, require a construct/relative reading; it can support an adverbial “in the beginning” reading under attested Hebrew reading traditions.
- **בָּרָא — bara:** “created.” The verse uses this verb with God as its subject. The verb's full semantic range and theological implications should not be reduced to a simplistic English gloss.
- **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim:** “God” in this sentence; it is the subject performing the action.
- **הַשָּׁמַיִם — ha-shamayim:** “the heaven” in the KJV.
- **הָאָרֶץ — ha-aretz:** “the earth” in the KJV.

### Genesis 1:2 terms

- **תֹהוּ — tohu:** translated “without form” here; its usage elsewhere can carry senses such as waste, emptiness, futility, or an unproductive/desolate condition. Context matters.
- **בֹּהוּ — bohu:** translated “void.” Lexical references associate it with emptiness or voidness. It occurs with *tohu* in Genesis 1:2 and Jeremiah 4:23.
- **חֹשֶׁךְ — choshekh:** “darkness.”
- **תְּהוֹם — tehom:** “the deep.” The term can refer to the deep or abyss/waters; the exact physical referent in this verse is not fully specified by the word alone.
- **רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים — ruach Elohim:** “the Spirit of God” in the KJV. *Ruach* has a semantic range that includes spirit, wind, or breath; the KJV's “Spirit of God” is retained as the text under study.
- **מְרַחֶפֶת — merachephet:** translated “moved.” The verb is rare. Scholarly discussion recognizes a range that includes moving, hovering, or fluttering; the precise nuance in Genesis 1:2 should remain open pending fuller contextual study.

**Language caution:** A lexical range does not mean every possible gloss is equally appropriate in this sentence. Grammar, context, and canonical usage must determine which sense is most defensible.

## 4. Grammar and literary structure: a live question

A significant interpretive question concerns the relationship between Genesis 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3.

One published study in the *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* (Jeremy D. Lyon, 2019) argues that:
- Genesis 1:1 is an independent clause describing God's initial creative act;
- Genesis 1:2 describes the condition of the earth as initially created;
- Genesis 1:3 advances the narrative with God's command concerning light.

Other scholarly readings construe Genesis 1:1 as an introductory heading or read it in a dependent construction (“When God began to create…”). The University of Toronto guide displays the JPS rendering as one example of the latter translation tradition.

**Current record:** The KJV is our primary wording. The independent-clause reading has a published grammatical defense, but the competing readings must be examined rather than dismissed. No “gap,” pre-existent matter, or elapsed interval is to be inserted merely because one interpretation assumes it. Nor should an alternative be excluded before its grammatical case is examined.

## 6. Scripture-and-Creation investigation

This study is not restricted to Bible-text observations alone. Creation is also within the agreed investigation scope. However, the physical investigation must be tied to a clear question and use evidence appropriate to the relevant discipline.

**What can be responsibly said at this stage:**
- Genesis 1:1–2 names earth, darkness, the deep, and waters. These are terms in the text, not yet scientific measurements or technical definitions.
- The passage does not provide units, material composition, temperature, pressure, chronology beyond its own narrative framing, or a modern physical model.
- No cosmological or geological model is being asserted by this record.
- A scientific investigation should identify the exact observable or claim under examination, cite primary or authoritative evidence, distinguish observation from model and inference, and report uncertainty and competing explanations.
- Scientific findings may help us investigate the created things and relationships being witnessed. They do not become the Author, and they do not dictate what the biblical text says.

**Research question to carry forward:** What, if anything, can disciplined observation of the physical world contribute to our understanding of the named realities and relationships in Genesis 1:1–2, without assuming that modern scientific terminology is identical to the ancient Hebrew terms?

No scientific conclusion is claimed at this stage.

## 7. GUA-W relationship

**Governing order:** Author → Word → Creation → Witness.

- **Author / Source:** Genesis 1:1 explicitly names God as the one who created.
- **Word:** These two verses do not yet record the spoken command “Let there be…”; that appears in verse 3. Do not collapse the act of creating in verse 1 and the speech recorded in verse 3 into an undifferentiated statement.
- **Creation:** Heaven and earth are named in verse 1; verse 2 describes the earth's condition and names the deep and waters.
- **Witness:** The present study is a derivative report of the text and the created order. It must not be confused with the Author or treated as authority over the Source.

**Architectural restraint:** The distinction between originating Cause and created reality is directly relevant. It is too early to assign every later GUA-W layer—Constraint, Structure, Framework, Pattern, Runtime, or State—to these two verses as though each were explicitly named here. Those mappings require evidence from the unfolding account.

## 10. Findings and unresolved questions

### Established from the text
- Genesis 1:1 attributes the creation of heaven and earth to God.
- Genesis 1:2 describes the earth as “without form, and void,” darkness upon the face of the deep, and the Spirit of God moving upon the face of the waters.

### Supported observations
- God is the acting subject in Genesis 1:1.
- Genesis 1:2 introduces a described condition and several relationships before the first recorded “And God said” in Genesis 1:3.
- The Hebrew terms *tohu*, *bohu*, *tehom*, *ruach*, and *merachephet* require contextual treatment rather than one-word assumptions.

### Unresolved
1. Is Genesis 1:1 an independent opening clause, a heading, or part of a dependent construction?
2. How exactly does verse 2 relate grammatically and temporally to verse 1?
3. What range of meaning best fits *tohu va-bohu* in this context?
4. What is the referent of *tehom* here, and how does the text relate it to “the waters”?
5. What nuance of *merachephet* best fits the Spirit of God in this sentence?
6. What do the canonical cross-references clarify, and where do they differ in context?
7. Which concrete observations of Creation are relevant to these verses, and what can those observations legitimately establish?

### What must not be claimed yet
- That “without form and void” necessarily means evil, sin, disorder, or a prior judgment.
- That Genesis 1:2 describes a destruction and recreation, or a time gap, without establishing the grammatical and textual case.
- That *ruach* must be translated “wind” merely because the word can carry that sense elsewhere.
- That *merachephet* describes a specific physical mechanism.
- That modern cosmological or geological models are directly encoded in these two verses.
- That GUA-W's later architectural layers are all explicitly present in these two verses.
