## 1. Passage text — KJV

**Genesis 3:6**

> And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.

**Genesis 3:7**

> And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.

## 2. Immediate context

Genesis 2:16–17 records God's command to the man: broad permission to eat from the Garden's trees, one specific prohibition, and the stated consequence. Genesis 3:1–5 records the serpent questioning the command, the woman's reply, the serpent denying the warning, and the serpent promising opened eyes and knowledge of good and evil.

Genesis 3:6–7 now records what the woman and man do and what immediately follows. The passage does not merely repeat the serpent's words. It narrates actions and an observed change in the pair's awareness.

The study must keep distinct:
- what the serpent promised in verse 5;
- what the woman saw and evaluated in verse 6;
- what both people did;
- what the narrator says happened in verse 7;
- and what remains to be explained by the following verses.

## 3. Observe the sequence before interpreting it

### 3.1 The woman's seeing and evaluation — verse 6

The verse gives three descriptions of the tree:

1. **“Good for food.”**
2. **“Pleasant to the eyes.”**
3. **“To be desired to make one wise.”**

The order is worth preserving. The narrator reports what the woman saw about the tree and its fruit before recording her action. The three descriptions concern provision/food, visual appeal, and the desired prospect of wisdom.

There is a notable textual relationship with Genesis 2:9. There the trees are described as “pleasant to the sight, and good for food.” Genesis 3:6 again uses the categories of food and visual appeal, now in the woman's evaluation of the prohibited tree, and adds that it is desired to make one wise.

This is a comparison within the text, not proof that the tree's actual nature changed. The tree was already present in the Garden and had already been named in the command. What changes in this scene is the narrated evaluation and action of the human pair.

**Observation:** The woman evaluates the tree in relation to food, sight, and the desired result of wisdom. The narrator then records taking and eating.

**Interpretive caution:** The text reports the woman's evaluation; it does not say that her evaluation changed God's command or made the prohibited fruit permissible. Nor does the passage state that food itself, beauty itself, or wisdom itself is evil. The boundary given in Genesis 2:17 remains the relevant command.

### 3.2 Taking and eating — verse 6

The actions are stated plainly:
- “she took of the fruit thereof”;
- “and did eat.”

The narrative connects the action to the tree already identified by the preceding conversation. The text does not record a new permission, a new command, or a withdrawal of the earlier boundary.

The woman's taking and eating are followed by her giving fruit to her husband. The verse then states that he ate.

**Observation:** The text attributes eating to both the woman and the man. It does not describe the man as refusing, being physically forced, or being absent from the event.

### 3.3 “Unto her husband with her” — verse 6

The KJV says she gave the fruit “unto her husband with her.” The wording places the husband with her in the scene when she gives him the fruit.

This matters, but the wording must not be made to say more than it says. It does not specify exactly when the husband arrived, whether he heard the serpent's entire conversation, or what he understood at each point. Those details are not narrated here.

The text does explicitly say that she gave and that he ate. The man's action is not omitted from the account.

**Unresolved:** The exact circumstances of the husband's presence during the serpent's conversation are not established by this phrase alone. “With her” is clear in the giving/eating scene; a complete reconstruction of his location and hearing throughout verses 1–6 is not supplied.

### 3.4 The eyes of both are opened — verse 7

The narrator says:

> “And the eyes of them both were opened…”

This directly echoes the serpent's statement in Genesis 3:5:

> “then your eyes shall be opened…”

The correspondence is explicit at the level of wording: the serpent said their eyes would be opened; the next account says the eyes of both were opened.

But the narrator immediately identifies what they came to know:

> “and they knew that they were naked…”

The text does not say in verse 7 that they became omniscient, became equal to God, or acquired unlimited wisdom. It records a particular recognition: they knew they were naked.

**Important distinction:** The serpent's promise and the narrator's report must be compared carefully. The promised “opened eyes” corresponds to the wording of the reported event. The meaning, scope, and full consequence of “knowing good and evil” are not exhausted by the phrase “they knew that they were naked.” Further examination is required.

### 3.5 Their response — verse 7

After recognizing their nakedness:
- they sewed fig leaves together;
- they made themselves aprons.

This is the first response to the newly stated recognition. The text records an act of covering. It does not yet tell us what they will say about their action, whether the covering succeeds in addressing the deeper condition, or how God responds. Those matters continue in Genesis 3:8–13 and beyond.

The narrator's order is important: opened eyes, knowledge of nakedness, then humanly made covering.

**Observation:** The pair respond to what they now recognize by making a covering from fig leaves.

**Limit:** Do not import later shame, fear, hiding, or divine judgment into verse 7 before reading the next passage. Those developments are narrated subsequently.

## 4. Language and textual observations

### 4.1 “Saw” and “good”

The Hebrew verb commonly transliterated *ra'ah* means “to see” and can also describe perceiving or considering, depending on context. The KJV's “saw that” introduces the woman's stated evaluation of the tree.

The word translated “good” is from the Hebrew *tov*. In this sentence it describes the tree as good for food from the woman's perspective. It is not the narrator repeating God's evaluation of the whole Creation in Genesis 1:31.

**Distinction:** God's declaration that His completed work was “very good” in Genesis 1:31 and the woman's assessment that this tree was “good for food” in Genesis 3:6 are different statements by different speakers/narrative voices in different contexts. Do not treat the shared English word as making the evaluations identical.

### 4.2 “Pleasant to the eyes”

The phrase reports visual appeal. Genesis 2:9 had already described the Garden's trees as pleasant to sight. Visual appeal is not itself condemned in the text. The issue in this passage is the woman's evaluation and action in relation to the particular tree that God had prohibited.

### 4.3 “Desired to make one wise”

The phrase describes the tree as desirable for the prospect of wisdom. The verse does not say that wisdom is inherently wrong. It places the desire for this result in the same sentence as taking and eating the fruit of the prohibited tree.

The precise nature of the knowledge of good and evil, and its relation to wisdom, remains a larger canonical question. Do not define it solely by a modern idea of intelligence or information.

### 4.4 “Opened” and “knew”

The Hebrew wording uses a form of *paqach* for the opening of their eyes and *yada* for their knowing. The narrative links opened eyes with a new recognition of nakedness. The text's own object of knowing in verse 7 must be retained when interpreting the result.

The wording does not by itself settle every theological question about what kind of knowledge was acquired. It gives the immediate narrated result and leaves the wider implications to the rest of Scripture.

## 11. Findings and questions carried forward

### Established by the text
1. The woman evaluates the tree in three stated ways: good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable to make one wise.
2. She takes and eats the fruit.
3. She gives fruit to her husband, who is described as with her in that scene.
4. The man eats.
5. The eyes of both are opened.
6. Both know that they are naked.
7. They sew fig leaves together and make themselves aprons.

### Supported textual observations
- Genesis 3:6 echoes the Garden's descriptions of food and visual appeal in Genesis 2:9.
- The serpent's promise that their eyes would be opened in Genesis 3:5 is followed by the narrator's statement that both people's eyes were opened.
- The narrator immediately specifies the recognition that follows: they know they are naked.
- The narrative moves from human action to recognition and then to self-made covering.

### Unresolved
1. What is the full meaning of “knowing good and evil” in the immediate and canonical contexts?
2. What is the exact relationship between the serpent's promise and the knowledge reported in verse 7?
3. Was the man present during the serpent's entire conversation, or only at the point the verse explicitly places him with the woman? The passage does not settle this.
4. What is the significance of the fig leaves and the act of making coverings, beyond the immediate fact that they cover themselves?
5. How will the pair respond when they hear the voice of the LORD God in the Garden?
6. How do later canonical passages distinguish the woman's deception and the man's participation without erasing either person's recorded action?

### Do not claim yet
- That the husband heard the whole serpent conversation.
- That the phrase “their eyes were opened” means they acquired unlimited knowledge or became equal to God.
- That the immediate recognition of nakedness exhausts the meaning of “knowing good and evil.”
- That food, visual appeal, or wisdom is inherently evil.
- That the fig-leaf coverings resolved the condition.
- That the full consequences of the act have been narrated by the end of verse 7.
