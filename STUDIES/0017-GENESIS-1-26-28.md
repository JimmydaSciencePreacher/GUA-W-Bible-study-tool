# Study 0017 — Genesis 1:26–28
## Humanity in God's image, male and female, and the given human vocation

**Study status:** Initial unified Scripture-and-Creation investigation recorded. ## 1. Passage text — KJV

> **Genesis 1:26**  
> And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth.
>
> **Genesis 1:27**  
> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.
>
> **Genesis 1:28**  
> And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that moveth upon the earth.

The passage presents a sequence that must be kept in view:

1. God speaks: “Let us make man” (v. 26).
2. Humanity is described in relation to God's image and likeness (v. 26).
3. A dominion vocation is stated before the fulfillment report (v. 26).
4. God creates humanity in His image; the text repeats the image statement and identifies male and female (v. 27).
5. God blesses them (v. 28).
6. God addresses them with commands concerning fruitfulness, multiplication, filling the earth, subduing it, and dominion (v. 28).

The text does not say that humanity creates itself, grants itself the image, or originates its own authority. God is the speaking, creating, and blessing subject. Humanity is the recipient of creation, image-language, blessing, and vocation.

## 2. Immediate literary context

### 2.1 The sixth day has two distinguishable movements

Genesis 1:24–25 records God's command concerning land creatures and the statement that God made them according to their kinds. Genesis 1:26–28 then turns to humanity. Both are on the sixth day, but the narrative does not present them as interchangeable.

The preceding animal account names cattle, creeping things, and beasts of the earth. The human account adds the explicit image/likeness statement, male and female, a direct blessing, and a stated vocation over the other creatures and the earth.

Do not erase either continuity or distinction:
- Humanity belongs within the created order; the text does not present humanity as the Author.
- Humanity is distinguished within the account by the image/likeness language and its stated vocation.
- The shared language of living creatures, fruitfulness, and multiplication does not make the human and animal accounts identical.

### 2.2 The larger creation sequence

The passage follows a repeated pattern of divine speech and realized result. In earlier passages God speaks, domains are distinguished, creatures appear, and God evaluates what He has made. Here the text gives an especially explicit deliberative statement (“Let us make”), followed by the creation report (“So God created”), then blessing and command.

The narrative order matters. The dominion vocation is spoken in verse 26; verse 27 reports the creation; verse 28 reports blessing and the commands. Do not collapse creation, blessing, and vocation into one undifferentiated event.

### 2.3 Literary observations

- “God said” begins verse 26.
- “Let us make” is first-person plural wording.
- “Our image” and “our likeness” are parallel expressions.
- “Let them have dominion” uses plural reference to humanity.
- Verse 27 repeats “in the image of God” and uses the verb “created” three times in the verse's compact statement.
- “Male and female created he them” explicitly includes both sexes in the creation statement.
- “God blessed them” precedes the commands in verse 28.
- The commands use plural address: “unto them.”
- Dominion is stated in both verses 26 and 28, while verse 28 adds the command to subdue the earth.

### A. “Man” — אָדָם (*adam*)

In Genesis 1:26–27, *adam* is used in a collective or representative human sense: the passage moves between the singular “him” and the plural “them,” then identifies “male and female.” The wording does not limit the image statement to a single male individual. The text's own movement from singular collective language to plural humanity is important.

Genesis 2 will narrate the formation of the man in a more particular sequence. Do not import every detail of Genesis 2 into Genesis 1 before examining the next account in its own order and context.

### B. “Image” — צֶלֶם (*tselem*) and “likeness” — דְּמוּת (*demuth*)

Verse 26 says “in our image, after our likeness.” Verse 27 repeats “in his own image” and “in the image of God.” Both terms are important. The text does not provide a definition in these verses that reduces image to one human feature, such as intelligence, language, physical form, moral capacity, or dominion.

The words are related in meaning, but this passage alone does not establish whether they describe two distinct aspects, overlapping expressions, or a reinforcing parallel. Any detailed doctrine must be examined through the wider canonical context rather than asserted from the dictionary gloss alone.

**Textual finding:** The image statement is explicit, repeated, and attached to God's act of creating humanity.  
**Not established by these verses alone:** A complete inventory of what constitutes the image, whether it is primarily physical, relational, moral, representative, functional, or some combination.

### C. “Let us make” — נַעֲשֶׂה (*na'aseh*)

The wording is first-person plural: “Let us make.” The next verse says, “So God created,” using singular reference to God. The passage therefore gives plural deliberative speech followed by a singular divine creative act.

Interpretations of the plural have included divine deliberation, address to a heavenly court, and other explanations. The grammar identifies the plural form; grammar alone does not settle the theological referent of “us.” Do not treat one proposed explanation as explicit wording in the verse. The canonical study should compare relevant passages while preserving what Genesis 1 itself states.

### D. “Have dominion” — רָדָה (*radah*)

The verb concerns ruling or exercising dominion. Its objects in verse 26 are named: fish of the sea, fowl of the air, cattle, all the earth, and every creeping thing that moves upon the earth. Verse 28 repeats dominion over fish, fowl, and every living thing moving upon the earth.

The text grants a vocation; it does not say humanity becomes the Creator or receives authority to change the Source. Nor do these verses alone define every practical limit, method, or accountability involved in exercising dominion. Those questions require the rest of Scripture and careful investigation of the created world.

### E. “Male and female” — זָכָר (*zakar*) and נְקֵבָה (*neqebah*)

Verse 27 explicitly states: “male and female created he them.” Both are included in the image statement that frames the verse. The following verse says God blessed “them” and addressed “them.”

The immediate text therefore does not assign the image of God to one sex while excluding the other. Nor does it state in these three verses a hierarchy of human worth based on sex. Questions about bodily differentiation, reproduction, family, and social roles require careful reading of subsequent passages; they must not be smuggled into this verse as if already stated here.

### F. “Blessed” — בָּרַךְ (*barak*)

Verse 28 says God blessed them and then gives the commands. The blessing is an action attributed to God; the fruitfulness and dominion commands are the words addressed to humanity.

The text links blessing and vocation in sequence. It does not define blessing as a biological mechanism, a gene, a psychological state, or a human achievement. Those would be additional claims requiring separate evidence.

### G. “Be fruitful, and multiply” — פָּרָה (*parah*) and רָבָה (*rabah*)

These verbs repeat language used in the blessing of the aquatic and flying creatures in Genesis 1:22. The human command shares fruitfulness and multiplication language with the animal blessing, while the human passage adds the image/likeness statement and the distinct commands concerning the earth and dominion.

The shared words establish a textual connection. They do not erase the distinct recipients and vocations in the two passages.

### H. “Replenish the earth” — מָלֵא (*male*)

The Hebrew verb means “fill” or “make full.” The KJV renders it “replenish.” The English word can suggest “fill again” to a modern reader, but that implication should not be imposed on the Hebrew verb here. Genesis 1:28 itself does not state that the earth had previously been emptied of humanity or that this command necessarily means repopulate after a prior human population.

The safest immediate rendering for lexical comparison is “fill the earth,” while preserving the KJV wording in the quoted text.

### I. “Subdue it” — כָּבַשׁ (*kabash*)

The verb carries the sense of bringing under subjection or subduing. Its object here is the earth (“subdue it”). It is stronger than merely observing or inhabiting the earth, but the verse does not provide a complete policy for how this command is to be carried out.

The force of the verb should not be softened away; neither should it be expanded into an unlimited license for destruction. The limits and responsibilities of the vocation must be studied with the rest of Scripture, especially the Garden assignment and later passages concerning care, provision, and accountability.

### J. “Created” — בָּרָא (*bara*)

Verse 27 uses *bara* three times in a compact poetic or highly structured statement: God created man in His image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them. The repeated verb places the creative act with God. Humanity is the result, not the originating cause.

This repetition is a strong textual emphasis. It should not be reduced to a generic statement that humans merely appeared or self-authored their status.

## 4. Creation investigation: human biology, cognition, social life, and ecological effects

### 4.1 Investigation question

What can present-day scientific disciplines observe about human beings as biological organisms, cognitively capable agents, social participants, and ecological actors? Which observations are relevant to the human vocation described in Genesis 1:26–28, and which questions remain theological rather than empirically measurable?

The comparison is deliberately bounded. Biology can investigate human bodies, development, reproduction, behavior, and relationships with ecosystems. It cannot measure whether a person bears the image of God as a theological reality. Scientific descriptions must not be made to define or exhaust the biblical claim.

### 4.2 Human beings are embodied organisms

Humans are living organisms with cellular bodies, inherited genetic material, metabolism, development, reproduction, sensory systems, and dependence on air, water, food, and environmental conditions. Human physiology is studied through anatomy, genetics, developmental biology, neuroscience, endocrinology, and related disciplines.

Humans are classified biologically as *Homo sapiens*, within the primate order. Comparative anatomy and genetics document both shared biological features with other primates and traits that characterize human populations. These classifications describe biological relationships; they do not settle the theological meaning of *adam*, *tselem*, or *demuth*.

Relevant observations include:
- Bipedal locomotion and anatomical adaptations associated with habitual upright walking.
- Large and developmentally complex brains, with capacities for learning, memory, planning, and flexible behavior.
- Long childhood and extended dependence, with substantial learning occurring through development and social interaction.
- Human reproduction and sexual development, including typical male and female reproductive organization and biological variation.
- Dependence on cooperative care, food systems, shelter, and social environments.

These observations do not identify a measurable biological feature as “the image of God.” A brain measurement, genetic sequence, reproductive capacity, or cognitive score cannot be substituted for the passage's theological statement.

### 4.3 Human cognition and communication

Research in cognitive science and neuroscience investigates perception, memory, attention, learning, reasoning, language, planning, and decision-making. Human beings also transmit information through speech, gesture, writing, symbolic systems, teaching, and cumulative cultural practices.

Human cognition is not uniform or unlimited. Individuals and populations vary; development, health, education, environment, and social conditions affect measured performance. A scientific account of cognitive capacities must not turn ability, intelligence, disability, age, or educational attainment into a ranking of human worth.

**Comparison boundary:** These capacities may be relevant to questions about human vocation and responsibility, but Genesis 1:26–28 does not say that the image of God is identical to intelligence, language, or abstract reasoning.

### 4.4 Human social life and cooperation

Humans live in social groups and develop kinship systems, communities, institutions, shared practices, and forms of cooperation. Human infants require prolonged care; teaching and imitation enable knowledge and skills to pass between generations. Cooperation can support food acquisition, child-rearing, construction, defense, exchange, and collective problem-solving.

Human social life also includes conflict, coercion, unequal power, and harm. Scientific description of cooperation does not establish that every human institution is good or that social success determines moral standing.

**Comparison boundary:** Genesis addresses humanity collectively (“them”) and gives the vocation to the human recipients. The scientific fact of social life can inform study of how humans act together, but it cannot determine the divine meaning of the blessing.

### 4.5 Humans alter ecosystems

Humans are part of Earth's ecosystems and also exert unusually extensive effects through agriculture, settlement, extraction, transport, energy use, species movement, and land-use change. These activities can alter habitats, nutrient cycles, water systems, biodiversity, and climate. Effects differ by place, time, technology, scale, and practice; human activity can also restore habitats, protect species, and reduce ecological damage.

This evidence makes the dominion/subduing language a serious subject for investigation rather than a decorative phrase. However, observed human power to alter ecosystems does not prove that every alteration is justified. Capability is not identical to authority, and observed consequences are not by themselves a complete moral rule.

**Comparison boundary:** Ecology can describe consequences and constraints of human action. The biblical passage supplies a theological vocation. The practical meaning and limits of that vocation must be examined through the full biblical context rather than inferred from ecological power alone.

### 4.6 What science can and cannot establish here

| Question | Scientific contribution | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| What kind of organism is a human? | Anatomy, physiology, genetics, development, and biological classification | Does not define the theological meaning of humanity |
| How do humans perceive, learn, reason, and communicate? | Cognitive science, neuroscience, psychology, linguistics | Does not demonstrate that the image of God is identical to a cognitive trait |
| How do humans reproduce and develop? | Reproductive biology, genetics, embryology, developmental science | Does not explain divine blessing as a biological mechanism |
| How do humans cooperate and form societies? | Anthropology, sociology, psychology, history, and behavioral research | Does not make social status a measure of human worth |
| What effects do human activities have on ecosystems? | Ecology, conservation science, Earth-system science, and environmental monitoring | Does not by itself define the moral or theological limits of dominion |
| Can science measure the image of God? | It can investigate observable human traits and behaviors | The image claim in Genesis is theological and is not operationalized as a scientific variable |

### 4.7 Evidence status and sourcing discipline

This is a qualitative synthesis of established disciplinary domains, not a new systematic review or a claim that particular measurements have been collected for this study. No numerical estimates are asserted here. A later evidence pass should attach specific, current sources for each empirical claim—preferably peer-reviewed reviews and primary research, major scientific assessments, and authoritative institutional datasets—and record publication dates, methods, sample limits, and uncertainty.

Do not cite a scientific source as proving the image of God. Do not cite Genesis as though it were a modern biological classification manual. Keep the two evidence streams distinct and compare only where the question warrants comparison.

## 5. Scripture cross-references

These are pathways for examination. Each related passage must be read in its own context; a shared word does not make two passages interchangeable.

### Genesis 1:22
God blesses aquatic and flying creatures and commands fruitfulness, multiplication, and filling. Compare the shared language in Genesis 1:28 while preserving the additional human image and dominion statements.

### Genesis 1:24–25
The land animals are made before the human creation statement. Compare the order and wording without collapsing the two movements of the sixth day.

### Genesis 2:7
The man is formed from the dust of the ground, and God breathes into his nostrils the breath of life; man becomes a living soul. This gives a more particular account of the man's formation. It must be read in its own narrative sequence and compared with Genesis 1:26–27 without assuming that every detail is already explained by the word “image.”

### Genesis 2:15
The man is put into the Garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it. This is an important next canonical passage for examining human work, care, and responsibility. It may qualify how the dominion and subduing language is understood, but it should not be imported into Genesis 1:28 as if the words were identical.

### Genesis 2:18–25
The account of the woman, the man's recognition, and the joining of man and woman develops the human relationship theme. Read the complete passage before drawing conclusions about sex, marriage, or social order.

### Genesis 5:1–2
The text again connects the creation of man in God's likeness with male and female and God's blessing. This is a direct canonical return to Genesis 1 language.

### Genesis 9:1–7
After the Flood, God blesses Noah and his sons with fruitfulness and multiplication, gives living creatures into their hand, and speaks about the value of human life in relation to God's image. This passage is essential for studying dominion, human life, and accountability; read its full context.

### Psalm 8
The psalm reflects on humanity's place and the creatures placed under human feet. It is a major canonical witness for examining dominion and human creatureliness before God.

### Psalm 104
The psalm praises God's wisdom and provision throughout Creation. It helps prevent the human vocation from being studied as though other creatures exist only as instruments of human use.

### Hebrews 2:5–9
The passage quotes Psalm 8 and discusses humanity's dominion in relation to Jesus Christ. It must be studied in its own argument; it should not be used to bypass the immediate wording of Genesis.

### Colossians 1:15–20 and Hebrews 1:1–3
These passages speak of the Son in relation to the invisible God, Creation, and the sustaining of all things. They are relevant to the Author–Word–Creation relationship, but their precise claims must be handled in their own contexts.

## 6. GUA-W relationship

Governing order:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

### A. Author and originating act

Genesis 1:26 begins with God speaking. Genesis 1:27 repeatedly names God as the one who creates. Humanity does not originate the image statement or its own existence.

This is consistent with the governing principle:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

### B. Image and representation

The passage explicitly relates humanity to God's image and likeness. GUA-W may record this as a foundational distinction between the Author and the human creature who bears the stated image. It must not turn the map into the image, or define the image beyond what Scripture establishes.

No new GUA-W layer or mechanism is proposed. The image/likeness language is a textual claim whose full theological meaning remains under canonical investigation.

### C. Male and female are both included

The text says “male and female created he them” within the repeated image statement. Both are included in the plural recipients of God's blessing and command. A GUA-W description must preserve this textual inclusion and must not assign the image to only one sex.

### D. Blessing precedes the commands

The narrative states that God blessed them, then records the commands. The human recipients do not create the blessing by their own achievement. This is a textual sequence worth preserving.

A comparison may be made with the blessing and fruitfulness of Genesis 1:22. The relationship is a repeated textual pattern, not proof that the same biological or architectural mechanism applies identically to every creature.

### E. Dominion is a received vocation, not authorship

Human dominion is granted within the created order. The human Witness does not become the Author. Dominion cannot be interpreted as permission to rewrite the Source or treat the creation as ownerless.

The practical meaning, limits, and accountability of dominion remain to be examined with Genesis 2:15 and later canonical passages. The science of human ecological effects can reveal consequences of human actions; it does not independently settle the theological meaning of the vocation.

### F. Human Witness and the limits of registration

Human beings can observe, name, investigate, cultivate, build, communicate, and transmit knowledge. These capacities are relevant to the role of a Witness, but Genesis 1:26–28 does not state that human observation is infallible or that human interpretation is identical to the Author's knowledge.

The distinction remains:
- Author: God, Original Cause.
- Word: the originating Word, not a derivative human construction.
- Creation: what is brought forth.
- Witness: the derivative receiver, observer, and reporter.

A human Witness may describe what is present, but the report remains derivative and must not be confused with the Source.

## 7. The Four Questions

The questions work recursively and together. They are not four sequential stages.

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- What does GUA-W already establish about the distinction between Author and human Witness?
- What is the precise relationship between “image” and “likeness” in the biblical text?
- Does the plural “Let us” identify its addressee in this passage, or does that require canonical comparison?
- What does the passage itself assign to humanity, and what must be learned from later passages?
- How can scientific descriptions of human capacities be kept distinct from theological claims about the image?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The governing order prevents AI from treating human intelligence, technology, or interpretive power as originating authority. The passage presents humanity as created by God, bearing the stated image/likeness, male and female, blessed, and given a vocation. Dominion is received; it is not self-originated authorship.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The text explicitly states God's creation of humanity in His image, male and female, and the blessing and commands given to them. Scientific disciplines observe human embodiment, development, cognition, social life, reproduction, and ecological effects. These observations illuminate the creaturely conditions in which human vocation is exercised, but they do not define or measure the image of God.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness must distinguish observable capacities from theological identity, capability from authority, and dominion from unlimited permission. Scientific measurements can describe particular human traits, but they cannot rank the worth of image-bearers or determine who bears God's image. The Witness must also recognize its own limited comprehension and avoid making one interpretation of “image” or “Let us” appear settled when the passage has not settled it.

**How the four questions inform one another:**
- The text establishes the Author's creative act and humanity's received status and vocation.
- Hebrew wording clarifies the image/likeness pair, the male/female statement, and the commands.
- Cross-references open the next questions about the Garden assignment, the value of human life, and dominion.
- Scientific investigation describes human biology and ecological action without claiming to measure the theological image.
- GUA-W preserves the distinction between Source, created human, received vocation, and derivative witness.

## 10. Findings and unresolved questions

### Established from the text
- God speaks the words “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness.”
- God is the subject of the creation report in verse 27.
- Humanity is created in God's image; the statement is repeated.
- The text says “male and female created he them.”
- God blesses them.
- God commands them to be fruitful, multiply, and fill the earth.
- God commands them to subdue the earth.
- God gives dominion over the creatures named in verses 26 and 28.
- The commands and blessing are addressed to the human recipients in plural form.

### Supported language observations
- *Adam* can refer to humanity collectively in this context.
- *Tselem* and *demuth* concern image and likeness; their exact relation in this passage remains interpretive.
- *Radah* expresses rule or dominion.
- *Zakar* and *neqebah* are the words for male and female.
- *Barak*, *parah*, *rabah*, *male*, and *kabash* distinguish blessing, fruitfulness, multiplication, filling, and subduing.
- The Hebrew verb *male* means fill; the KJV “replenish” should not automatically be read as “refill.”

### Supported Creation observations
- Humans are embodied organisms with biological development, reproduction, and environmental dependencies.
- Human cognition and communication are studied through several scientific disciplines.
- Human social life includes cooperation, learning, care, institutions, and conflict.
- Human activity can significantly alter ecosystems, with effects that vary by practice, location, and scale.
- These observations do not scientifically define the image of God.

### Unresolved
1. What is the precise relationship between *tselem* (“image”) and *demuth* (“likeness”) in Genesis 1:26?
2. What is the referent of “us” in “Let us make”? How do the immediate literary context and wider canonical passages inform the question?
3. Does the image language describe a status, relationship, representative vocation, capacity, or several dimensions together? What can be established from the full canon?
4. How does Genesis 1:27's image statement relate to Genesis 2:7's formation of man and the breath of life?
5. What does the text establish about the inclusion of male and female in the image and blessing, and what questions are developed only in Genesis 2 and later passages?
6. What is the proper scope and practice of *radah* (dominion) and *kabash* (subdue)?
7. How does Genesis 2:15's command to dress and keep the Garden inform the human vocation in Genesis 1:28?
8. How do Psalm 8 and Hebrews 2 develop the theme of human dominion and creaturely dependence?
9. How does Genesis 9:1–7 connect dominion, fruitfulness, and the value of human life made in God's image?
10. What does “fill the earth” mean in the narrative, and how does the command relate to later human history? Do not infer a prior emptied population from the English word “replenish.”
11. What empirical sources best document human biological, cognitive, social, and ecological characteristics without turning any one trait into a definition of the image?
12. How should ecological evidence inform the study of dominion without allowing scientific descriptions alone to settle a theological or moral command?
13. What is the relationship between human witness, knowledge, and responsibility, while preserving the distinction between the human Witness and the Author?

### Claims not admitted
- That “Let us” is conclusively explained by one interpretation from this verse alone.
- That image and likeness are two fully defined, separate GUA-W mechanisms.
- That the image of God is reducible to intelligence, language, physical appearance, reproduction, or dominion.
- That one sex alone bears God's image.
- That “replenish” proves the earth had a previous human population that was destroyed.
- That dominion is unlimited permission for exploitation.
- That scientific measurements can establish or deny the image of God.
- That human power or knowledge makes humanity the Author.
- That this passage alone supplies a complete doctrine of human nature, sex, marriage, social hierarchy, ecology, or salvation.
