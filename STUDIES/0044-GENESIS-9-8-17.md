## 2. Immediate observations

The passage has six movements:

1. **God speaks (v. 8):** God initiates the communication.
2. **God establishes (vv. 9–11):** God identifies Himself as the One establishing the covenant and identifies its recipients.
3. **God appoints a token (vv. 12–13):** The bow in the cloud is identified as the covenant token.
4. **God describes its appearance (v. 14):** The bow will be seen when a cloud is brought over the earth.
5. **God declares remembrance (vv. 15–16):** God says He will remember His covenant.
6. **God confirms the covenant (v. 17):** God repeats that the covenant is established between Himself and all flesh upon the earth.

The repetition is significant: God establishes, appoints, declares, remembers, and confirms. The covenant does not originate with Noah. Noah receives God's declaration.

## 3. Hebrew investigation

### 3.1 Covenant — *berit* (בְּרִית)

The principal Hebrew word is *berit*, translated covenant. It occurs repeatedly in this passage.

Genesis 9:9: “I establish my covenant with you.”  
Genesis 9:11: “I will establish my covenant with you.”  
Genesis 9:17: “which I have established between me and all flesh.”

God is the speaker and covenant-establishing party. The covenant is not presented as a human invention, negotiation, or arrangement initiated by Noah.

**Disposition:** God is the initiating and establishing party. Noah and the other named recipients receive the covenant declaration.

### 3.2 Establish — *qum* (קוּם)

The verb comes from *qum*, meaning rise, stand, or establish, according to context. In the covenant construction it communicates establishing or confirming the covenant.

Compare Genesis 6:18, where God says, “with thee will I establish my covenant.” Genesis 9 develops that earlier declaration by identifying recipients, scope, promise, and sign.

**Disposition:** The covenant in Genesis 9 continues a commitment announced earlier in the Flood narrative. It is not introduced as a human arrangement after Noah leaves the ark.

### 3.3 Seed — *zera* (זֶרַע)

Genesis 9:9 includes “your seed after you.” *Zera* means seed or offspring. The covenant is not limited to Noah's immediate household; his descendants are expressly included.

This connects with the multiplication commands in Genesis 8:17 and 9:1–7. The word here does not itself establish a complete biological theory of heredity; its immediate sense is Noah's offspring or descendants.

### 3.4 Every living creature — *kol nephesh chayyah* (כָּל־נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה)

The covenant includes more than humanity. Genesis 9:10 identifies birds, cattle, beasts of the earth, and every living creature associated with Noah and the ark. Verses 12, 15, and 16 repeat the inclusion of every living creature or all flesh.

This connects with Creation vocabulary in Genesis 1. The covenant's stated scope is broader than a covenant exclusively between God and Noah's human descendants.

**Finding:** Nonhuman living creatures are expressly included as covenant recipients. This is stated in the passage, not inferred from a modern environmental framework.

### 3.5 Token/sign — *'ot* (אוֹת)

The KJV uses “token.” The Hebrew *'ot* means sign, token, or distinguishing mark. It appears in Genesis 1:14, where the heavenly lights are appointed for signs, and in Genesis 9:12–17 for the covenant sign.

God identifies the bow as the sign of His covenant. The physical appearance and the covenant meaning must be distinguished: the optical phenomenon is observable, while its appointment as this covenant sign comes from God's declaration.

### 3.6 Bow — *qeshet* (קֶשֶׁת)

The Hebrew *qeshet* means bow, including an archery weapon, and is the word used here for the visible bow in the cloud. The text says, “I do set my bow in the cloud.”

Some interpreters understand the bow imagery in relation to a weapon no longer directed against the earth. That is a possible theological interpretation, but the passage does not explicitly explain the symbolism as God laying down a weapon.

**Disposition:** The text identifies the bow as God's covenant token. The weapon imagery is suggestive, but a specific “weapon laid aside” interpretation remains an interpretation rather than an explicit explanation.

### 3.7 Remember — *zakar* (זָכַר)

Genesis 9:15 says, “And I will remember my covenant.” *Zakar* means remember, call to mind, or bring to remembrance.

The statement should not be reduced to human forgetfulness projected onto God. In biblical covenant contexts, remembrance is associated with God's action in relation to His declared commitment.

Compare Genesis 8:1, where God remembered Noah, and Exodus 2:24, where God remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

**Disposition:** Divine remembrance is covenant language. It does not imply that God has forgotten and needs the rainbow to refresh His memory.

## 4. What exactly does God promise?

Genesis 9:11 gives the central promise:

> neither shall all flesh be cut off any more by the waters of a flood; neither shall there any more be a flood to destroy the earth.

Two related declarations are made:

1. All flesh will not again be cut off by the waters of a flood.
2. There will not again be a flood to destroy the earth.

The promise should not be expanded beyond its wording. It does not say that no local flood will ever occur, no person will ever drown, no animal will ever die in a flood, or that the earth will never experience another natural disaster.

The stated promise concerns another destruction of all flesh by a flood that destroys the earth.

### 4.1 Scope of the covenant

The named recipients include:
- Noah and his sons, addressed in verse 8;
- Noah's seed/descendants, verse 9;
- every living creature associated with the ark, verse 10;
- every living creature of all flesh, verses 12, 15, and 16;
- the earth, expressly named in verse 13.

This covenant is not restricted to Israel. It precedes Abraham, Moses, and the nation of Israel. Its stated scope reaches all flesh upon the earth.

### 4.2 Is this covenant conditional?

The passage does not attach a human performance condition to the promise that another world-destroying flood will not occur. God repeatedly says, “I establish,” “I will establish,” “I will remember,” and “I have established.”

The emphasis is on God's commitment. It is reasonable to describe this as a divine covenant promise without a stated human performance condition in this passage. Do not infer that every biblical covenant has identical terms; each covenant must be investigated according to its own text.

## 5. The rainbow: physical phenomenon and covenant sign

### 5.1 What causes a rainbow?

A primary rainbow is produced when sunlight interacts with water droplets in the atmosphere. The principal optical processes are:

1. **Refraction:** Light changes direction as it enters a water droplet.
2. **Dispersion:** Different wavelengths of visible light are refracted by slightly different amounts.
3. **Internal reflection:** Light reflects inside the droplet.
4. **Refraction on exit:** Light leaves the droplet and travels toward the observer.

Different wavelengths reach the observer at different angles, producing the visible spectrum. A secondary rainbow involves two internal reflections and appears outside the primary rainbow, usually with reversed color order.

A rainbow is not a physical object suspended in the atmosphere. It is an optical phenomenon produced by sunlight, water droplets, and viewing geometry. The observer's position matters; there is no single fixed location that every observer can approach.

### 5.2 Does the Bible explain the optical mechanism?

No. Genesis 9 does not explain refraction, dispersion, wavelengths, internal reflection, viewing angles, or optical geometry.

The passage identifies the bow as the sign of God's covenant. Science investigates the physical phenomenon through which the sign is seen.

These are different levels of explanation. Understanding the optical mechanism does not erase the covenant significance given by Scripture. Conversely, the covenant statement should not be presented as a technical optics description.

### 5.3 Did rainbows exist before the Flood?

This remains unresolved by the passage.

Genesis 9:13 says, “I do set my bow in the cloud.” It identifies the bow as God's covenant sign, but does not explicitly say:
- rainbows had never existed before;
- the optical mechanism was created at that moment;
- atmospheric conditions changed at that moment;
- the physical phenomenon was newly created;
- or that the sign was previously unknown to humanity.

Possible interpretations include:
- the rainbow was physically introduced after the Flood;
- the optical phenomenon already existed but was now appointed as a covenant sign;
- the phenomenon or atmospheric conditions changed and God appointed the resulting bow as the sign.

The passage itself does not settle these alternatives. Scientific knowledge of how rainbows form does not establish when the phenomenon first appeared in biblical history.

**Disposition:** The physical history of the rainbow before Genesis 9 remains open. Do not promote one interpretation to established fact without additional evidence.

### 5.4 Three distinct categories

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Physical phenomenon | The optical appearance of a bow in the atmosphere. |
| Divine appointment | God identifies the bow as His covenant token. |
| Witness registration | Noah and subsequent generations can see the sign. |

The physical phenomenon does not appoint itself as a covenant. The observer does not create the covenant meaning. God identifies the sign.

## 6. “Between me and the earth”

Genesis 9:13 says the bow is a token of a covenant “between me and the earth.” The Hebrew noun is *erets* (אֶרֶץ), earth or land according to context.

Earlier verses identify Noah, his descendants, and every living creature. Verse 13 expressly names the earth. Verses 15–17 repeat the inclusion of every living creature and all flesh upon the earth.

This repetition emphasizes the covenant's broad scope.

It does not mean that the earth is a human person, or that every component of matter is independently described as a covenant party. Preserve the language without extending it beyond what is stated.

**Finding:** Genesis 9 presents God's covenant commitment in relation to humanity, nonhuman living creatures, and the earth.

## 11. Triple Constraint review

### Biblical compliance
**Pass.** The study preserves the repeated divine establishment of the covenant, its named recipients, its promise, the bow as sign, and the language of remembrance. It does not claim that the text explicitly describes the first physical appearance of rainbows.

### Scientific coherence
**Pass within stated limits.** The optical explanation of rainbows is consistent with established atmospheric optics. The study distinguishes the physical phenomenon from its covenant meaning and does not use science to assert an undocumented chronology for the first rainbow.

### GUA-W consistency
**Pass.** God remains the Author and establishing Source. The covenant recipients do not originate it. The physical bow is Creation; its covenant significance is appointed by God; the Witness registers the sign.

## 12. Findings and open questions

### Established by the text
1. God speaks to Noah and his sons.
2. God establishes His covenant with Noah and his descendants.
3. Every living creature associated with the ark is included.
4. God promises that all flesh will not again be cut off by a flood that destroys the earth.
5. God appoints the bow in the cloud as the covenant token.
6. The covenant is stated for perpetual generations and called everlasting.
7. God declares that He will remember His covenant.
8. The covenant is described as between God and every living creature of all flesh upon the earth.

### Supported interpretations
- God is the initiating and establishing party.
- The covenant has a scope beyond humanity.
- The bow is a sign by divine appointment, not by human invention.
- Covenant remembrance describes God's commitment and action, not forgetfulness.
- The physical optics of a rainbow and its theological covenant significance are distinct categories.
- The promise should not be expanded to mean no local flooding or environmental disaster will ever occur.

### Unresolved questions
1. Did rainbows exist as an optical phenomenon before the Flood?
2. Does “I do set my bow” describe a new physical phenomenon, a new appointment, or both?
3. What is the full symbolic force of *qeshet* as bow?
4. Does the bow imagery intentionally evoke a weapon laid aside, or is that a later interpretive association?
5. What is the precise scope of “between me and the earth”?
6. How should “perpetual generations” and “everlasting covenant” be understood in relation to the duration of the present earth?
7. How does the Noahic covenant relate to later biblical covenants without collapsing their distinct terms?
8. What does divine remembrance reveal about covenant action across Scripture?
9. How should the physical mechanism of the rainbow be described without confusing it with the divine appointment of its signification?

### What must not be claimed
- That the Bible explicitly says rainbows never existed before the Flood.
- That the Bible explicitly says the optical mechanism was created in Genesis 9.
- That modern optics establishes the historical date of the first rainbow.
- That the rainbow creates or causes the covenant.
- That God forgets and needs a visible reminder.
- That the promise prohibits every local flood.
- That every biblical covenant has the same terms.
- That every component of matter is independently described as a covenant party.
- That the “weapon laid aside” interpretation is explicitly explained by the passage.
