## 2. Structure and immediate sequence

The passage contains two genealogical movements.

### Cain's line — Genesis 4:17–24

Cain → Enoch → Irad → Mehujael → Methusael → Lamech → Jabal, Jubal, Tubal-cain, and Naamah.

The account records:
- Cain's wife, son, and building of a city;
- a genealogy extending to Lamech;
- Lamech taking two wives;
- pastoral or tent-dwelling life associated with Jabal;
- musical instruments associated with Jubal;
- metalworking associated with Tubal-cain;
- Lamech's violent declaration and appeal to Cain's sevenfold vengeance.

### Seth's line — Genesis 4:25–26

Adam and Eve → Seth → Enos → people calling upon the name of the LORD.

The narrative returns to Adam and Eve after the account of Cain's descendants. Eve describes Seth as another seed appointed in place of Abel. Enos is born, and the passage closes with the statement that people began to call upon the name of the LORD.

The passage is not simply a list of names. It records family continuity, settlement, occupations, technologies, music, violence, and religious invocation.

## 3. Verse-by-verse investigation

### 3.1 Cain's wife, Enoch, and the city — verse 17

The verse says Cain knew his wife, she conceived and bore Enoch, and Cain built a city named after his son.

The Hebrew verb *yadaʿ* (“knew”) is the same broad verb used for marital intimacy in Genesis 4:1 and 4:25. The context makes its marital/sexual sense clear.

**What the text states:** Cain had a wife; she bore Enoch; Cain built a city and named it after Enoch.

**What the text does not state:** It does not name Cain's wife, identify her parents, explain where Cain met her, give the city's location, describe its size or construction, or state how many people lived there.

The existence of Cain's wife is not, by itself, an explanation of human population origins. Genesis 4 does not supply her identity or a separate account of her ancestry. Genesis 5 later presents Adam's continuing family and says he had other sons and daughters (Genesis 5:4), but it does not identify Cain's wife. Do not fill the silence with certainty.

The Hebrew *ʿir* means city or town. “Built a city” indicates organized settlement in the narrative, but the verse gives no architectural plan, walls, population, political institutions, or technological inventory. A modern definition of a city should not be imposed on the word without evidence.

The naming of the city after Enoch is explicit. The text does not explain Cain's motive beyond the naming itself.

### 3.2 The genealogy to Lamech — verse 18

The verse lists:
Enoch → Irad → Mehujael → Methusael → Lamech.

The repeated *yalad* (“begat”) language marks descent. Biblical genealogies can be selective and structured; the text does not provide ages, dates, generation intervals, or a complete account of every person in the family.

Names in this genealogy resemble names appearing in the later genealogy of Genesis 5, including Enoch and Lamech. Similar or identical names do not make the people identical. Genesis 4's Enoch is Cain's descendant; Genesis 5's Enoch is in the line of Seth through Jared. Their stated genealogical positions differ.

The passage does not explain the meanings of every name. Proposed etymologies should not be treated as established meanings merely from resemblance to a Hebrew root.

### 3.3 Lamech takes two wives — verse 19

Lamech takes two wives, Adah and Zillah.

This is the first explicit account in Genesis of one man having two wives. The text reports the practice; it does not present a divine command instituting it or a statement that it is the original marriage pattern.

Compare Genesis 2:24, which describes a man leaving father and mother, cleaving unto his wife, and the two becoming one flesh. Jesus later cites this creation account in Matthew 19:4–6 and Mark 10:6–9 when addressing marriage.

The distinction is important:
- Genesis 2:24 gives the foundational creation pattern.
- Genesis 4:19 reports Lamech's taking of two wives.
- Reporting a practice is not the same as commanding or approving it.

The text does not describe Adah's or Zillah's consent, feelings, or household arrangements. Do not invent those details.

### 3.4 Jabal — tents and cattle — verse 20

Adah bears Jabal, described as “the father of such as dwell in tents, and of such as have cattle.”

The description associates Jabal with a way of life involving tents and livestock. The Hebrew *miqneh* commonly refers to livestock or possessions, and in this context “cattle” is a reasonable KJV rendering. It need not be restricted to modern cattle species.

The verse does not say Jabal invented every tent or domesticated every animal. “Father of” can identify an originator, founder, or representative ancestor of a practice or group. The exact historical scope is not supplied.

Tent-dwelling and livestock keeping involve mobility, animal management, shelter, and adaptation to environmental conditions. These are real relationships with the created world, but Genesis does not give a technical account of tent design, herd size, species, or migration routes.

### 3.5 Jubal — harp and organ — verse 21

Jubal is described as “the father of all such as handle the harp and organ.”

The KJV's “harp” and “organ” are older English instrument terms. The Hebrew words are *kinnor* and *ʿugab*. The *kinnor* is generally understood as a stringed instrument, often described as a lyre. The *ʿugab* is commonly understood as a pipe or wind instrument. “Organ” here does not mean a modern keyboard organ with pipes, keys, and mechanical action.

The verse associates Jubal with people who play these instruments. It does not provide their construction, number of strings or pipes, tuning, musical scale, notation, repertoire, or the social and religious settings in which they were played.

Music is a human capability involving sound, timing, pattern, bodily action, materials, memory, and social communication. The text names an early ancestral association; it does not say music itself is morally good or evil. The moral character of a particular use of music must be evaluated from its context, not inferred from the existence of instruments.

### 3.6 Tubal-cain and metalworking — verse 22

Zillah bears Tubal-cain, described in the KJV as “an instructor of every artificer in brass and iron.” His sister is Naamah.

The Hebrew phrase is difficult to render precisely. It is commonly understood as describing a forger, sharpener, or instructor of every craftsman in copper/bronze and iron. The Hebrew *neḥoshet* can refer to copper or bronze; the KJV's “brass” should not automatically be read as the modern copper-zinc alloy called brass. The verse names two metal categories in the KJV, but the ancient material terminology does not map perfectly onto modern metallurgical categories.

The wording can be read as Tubal-cain being a forger/sharpener of every tool or implement of copper/bronze and iron, or as a teacher/master of metalworkers. The precise syntax and the force of the rare word *loteš* have been discussed by translators. The safe finding is that the verse associates Tubal-cain with metalworking in these materials.

The text does not state:
- which ores were used;
- whether the metals were native, smelted, cast, hammered, or forged;
- which tools or objects were made;
- whether he discovered metallurgy;
- the exact date or duration of the work;
- or whether “every artificer” means every metalworker in a universal historical sense.

Naamah is named as Tubal-cain's sister, but no occupation or further action is assigned to her in this passage. Her inclusion should be preserved without inventing a role.

### 3.7 Lamech's speech — verse 23

Lamech addresses both wives and calls them to hear his speech. He says he has slain a man to his wounding and a young man to his hurt.

The Hebrew wording is compact and difficult. The terms translated “wounding” and “hurt” can be associated with injury or bruising. The statement has been read in more than one way:
- as a boast about killing a man who wounded him and a young man who injured him;
- as a declaration of retaliatory violence;
- or, in some interpretations, as a lament or statement about a killing and the injury it caused.

The KJV's wording naturally presents Lamech as saying he has slain a man and a young man in relation to his own wounds. However, the exact relationship between the clauses and whether two victims or parallel descriptions are intended require care.

The text does not explain the circumstances of the killing, identify the victims, establish whether the two descriptions refer to one or two people, or tell us whether Lamech acted in self-defence. Those matters remain unresolved.

The speech is important because Lamech invokes Cain's protection in the next verse. His words place violence and retaliation in the foreground.

### 3.8 “Seventy and sevenfold” — verse 24

Lamech says:

> If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold.

The phrase is commonly rendered “seventy-sevenfold” or “seventy times seven,” depending on translation and interpretation. The expression is intensified in comparison with the sevenfold vengeance declared for Cain in Genesis 4:15.

The text records Lamech's assertion. It does not say God granted Lamech this protection, nor does it repeat God's mark upon Cain for Lamech. Lamech appears to appropriate or amplify the earlier declaration as a claim about his own vengeance.

The contrast is significant:
- God declared a boundary to prevent Cain from being killed by another person.
- Lamech speaks of greatly magnified vengeance in the context of his own killing.

The two statements must not be treated as equivalent. Cain's mark is a divine protective act in the text. Lamech's statement is Lamech's own speech.

Later, Jesus tells Peter to forgive “seventy times seven” (Matthew 18:21–22). The numerical expression invites comparison, but the contexts and purposes differ: Lamech's speech concerns vengeance; Jesus' instruction concerns forgiveness. Do not claim that Jesus is merely repeating Lamech's statement with the same meaning.

### 3.9 Seth — another seed appointed — verse 25

The narrative returns to Adam and Eve. Eve bears another son and names him Seth, explaining:

> For God, said she, hath appointed me another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew.

The Hebrew name *Shet* (Seth) is associated in the verse with the verb *shath* (“appointed” or “set”). The wordplay is explicit in the narrative: God has appointed another seed in place of Abel.

Eve's statement identifies God as the One who appoints this seed. Seth is not described as a replacement that erases Abel's death; he is another seed given after Abel was killed.

The text does not say Seth is Abel resurrected, nor that every aspect of Abel's role is transferred to Seth. It says another seed is appointed in his place.

This verse also returns the reader to Adam and Eve after the account of Cain's line. The family story continues despite Abel's death and Cain's departure.

### 3.10 Enos and calling upon the name of the LORD — verse 26

Seth has a son named Enos. The verse concludes:

> then began men to call upon the name of the LORD.

The Hebrew expression is *qaraʾ beshem YHWH*: to call upon, proclaim, or invoke the name of the LORD. The verb *qaraʾ* can mean call, call out, proclaim, or name, depending on context. “Name” (*shem*) in biblical usage can refer not merely to a spoken label but to the person, identity, or reputation represented by the name.

The KJV gives the traditional understanding that people began to call upon the name of the LORD. This is commonly understood as the beginning or public emergence of worshipful invocation in this line.

There is a translation and interpretive discussion about whether the phrase means people began to call upon the LORD, or whether the name of the LORD began to be called/proclaimed in a particular way. The immediate wording and later biblical usage strongly support the traditional sense of invoking the LORD, but the study should preserve the fact that translations express the clause differently.

Do not conclude that no one had ever spoken to God or worshipped before Enos. Genesis has already recorded God's dealings with Adam, Eve, Cain, and Abel, and the offerings in Genesis 4:3–5. Verse 26 may mark a public or communal development in calling upon the LORD; it does not erase earlier encounters or offerings.

This final statement provides a deliberate contrast with Lamech's speech. The Cainite section closes with an intensified claim of vengeance; the Sethite section closes with people calling upon the name of the LORD. The contrast is literary and theological, though it should not be turned into a claim that every individual in one genealogy had the same character.

## 6. Creation and scientific investigation

The passage names settlement, livestock keeping, music, and metalworking. These are concrete human activities that can be investigated through archaeology, materials science, zooarchaeology, anthropology, and music archaeology. The evidence must be used carefully: archaeology studies surviving traces in particular places and periods; it cannot identify Cain, Enoch, Jabal, Jubal, or Tubal-cain without independent evidence.

### 6.1 City-building and settlement

**Question:** What can archaeology say about the development of settled communities and cities, and what does Genesis 4:17 itself establish?

Archaeology documents a long, varied transition from mobile or seasonally mobile lifeways to permanent settlements and eventually urbanized societies in the ancient Near East. Augusta McMahon's chapter “From Sedentism to States, 10,000–3000 BCE” describes early sedentary communities around 10,000 BCE and increasingly complex urbanized societies by about 3000 BCE. The chapter also emphasizes that the relationship between sedentism and agriculture is not a simple one-way equation.

This evidence shows that settlement and urbanization are processes with multiple interacting conditions, not a single universal event. It does not identify Cain's city, date it, or establish that the archaeological sequence maps directly onto the Genesis genealogy.

Genesis 4:17 says Cain built a city and named it after Enoch. That is the textual claim. The verse gives no archaeological coordinates or dating data.

**Disposition:** Archaeology can describe settlement and urbanization in the ancient Near East. It cannot independently identify Cain's city or use modern chronological frameworks to rewrite the passage. [McMahon, “From Sedentism to States”](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119362500.ch2).

### 6.2 Pastoralism, tents, and livestock

**Question:** What kinds of human-creation relationships are involved in the way of life associated with Jabal?

Pastoral lifeways require knowledge of animals, reproduction, grazing, water, shelter, seasonal movement, and protection from predators and disease. Tent-dwelling can support mobility and adaptation to different environments. These practices are not simply “primitive” versions of settled farming; they can be sophisticated responses to ecological and social conditions.

The archaeological and anthropological record shows that herding, cultivation, and mobility have often coexisted in different combinations. A family or community may use both settled and mobile strategies.

Genesis 4:20 associates Jabal with tents and livestock. It does not give species, herd sizes, migration routes, or the date at which these practices began.

**Disposition:** The verse records an ancestral association with tent-dwelling and livestock keeping. Modern pastoral studies can illuminate the practical complexity of such lifeways but cannot identify Jabal archaeologically.

### 6.3 Music, instruments, and material culture

**Question:** What does Jubal's association with instruments reveal, and what can surviving instruments tell us?

The verse names *kinnor* and *ʿugab*, usually understood as a stringed instrument and a pipe/wind instrument. Instruments are material objects shaped to produce sound, but music also involves performance, rhythm, learned skill, memory, and shared cultural practice.

Archaeological evidence confirms that ancient Near Eastern societies had diverse musical instruments and musical performance. The lyres from the royal tombs of Ur, dating to the mid-third millennium BCE, are among the best-known surviving or reconstructable examples. Images and surviving remains provide evidence for string, wind, and percussion instruments. A study of ancient Mesopotamian music describes the Ur lyres and the range of instruments represented in art and archaeology.

This is evidence that ancient music had a substantial material and cultural history. It does not prove that the instruments of Genesis 4 were identical to later excavated instruments, nor does it date Jubal.

Music is not inherently aligned or misaligned merely because it is music. Scripture later depicts instruments used in praise (Psalm 150), while the moral significance of music depends on its purpose, words, conduct, and context.

**Disposition:** Music is a human capacity expressed through created materials and learned practices. Archaeology supplies examples of ancient instruments, not an identification of Jubal's specific instruments. [The Ancient Near East Today, “Music in Ancient Mesopotamia”](https://anetoday.org/music-ancient-mesopotamia/); [CNRS, “The Music of Antiquity”](https://news.cnrs.fr/articles/the-music-of-antiquity).

### 6.4 Metallurgy: copper, bronze, and iron

**Question:** What does the text mean by Tubal-cain's metalworking, and what does archaeology show about the technologies?

Metalworking includes several distinct operations: obtaining ore or native metal, heating, smelting, refining, alloying, casting, hammering, annealing, and forging. These are not interchangeable processes. A text that mentions a metalworker does not specify which of these operations were performed.

Archaeological research indicates that human use of native copper and copper minerals predates large-scale extractive smelting. The origins and spread of copper smelting are studied through artifacts, slag, furnaces, and compositional analysis. Research on early metallurgy in Eurasia describes native copper use and the development of smelting in the sixth millennium BCE, while other studies emphasize regional variation and debate over the earliest secure evidence.

Iron has a different and more complex history. A 2019 review by N. Erb-Satullo reports limited extractive iron metallurgy in Anatolia in the early second millennium BCE, with wider adoption later. A 2026 Cambridge University Press study by Robert Downes further examines how iron production may have developed in relation to earlier copper-smelting traditions. These findings show why a simple, universal chronology should be avoided.

The KJV's “brass” should not be silently converted into modern brass. The Hebrew *neḥoshet* is generally copper/bronze terminology. The verse does not provide enough information to identify a particular alloy or process.

Most importantly, archaeological chronologies do not by themselves settle the historical identity or date of Tubal-cain. The biblical genealogy and archaeological sequences are different evidence types. Their relationship requires a defined chronology and independent evidence; it must not be assumed.

**Disposition:** The text associates Tubal-cain with metalworking in copper/bronze and iron terminology. Archaeology shows complex, regionally variable histories for copper and iron technologies. It neither identifies Tubal-cain nor gives a basis for forcing the Genesis genealogy into one archaeological date. [Roberts, Thornton & Pigott, “Development of Metallurgy in Eurasia”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X001141ಕಾರ); [Erb-Satullo, “The Innovation and Adoption of Iron in the Ancient Near East”](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:8b406295-a086-48b1-aa7a-f66c4c035b07); [Downes, “The Advent of Iron”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/abs/advent-of-iron/D1A04995292E0E5164C8A79A264564D3).

### 6.5 Human skill and moral direction

**Question:** Does greater capability establish greater alignment with the Author?

The passage places useful human capabilities alongside Lamech's violent speech. Jabal's pastoral association, Jubal's music, and Tubal-cain's metalworking are not condemned in the text. Yet the same family account includes Lamech's killing and amplified vengeance.

This juxtaposition prevents a simplistic equation:
- technological development does not prove righteousness;
- musical skill does not prove worship;
- pastoral knowledge does not prove moral alignment;
- metalworking does not prove violence;
- and the existence of a city does not, by itself, prove rebellion.

Capabilities are real; their moral direction must be examined separately.

Scientific and historical investigation can describe what humans make and how skills develop. It cannot infer spiritual alignment from technological complexity alone.

**Disposition:** The passage supports a distinction between human capability and moral/spiritual direction. Do not label the named technologies evil or righteous merely by association with Cain's genealogy.

## 7. The Four Questions

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

How does GUA-W distinguish human capability from alignment with the Author? How can a society develop settlement, animal husbandry, music, and metalworking while the same narrative records violence and vengeance? What is the difference between a capacity, its use, and the moral purpose governing that use?

How do we compare the biblical genealogy with archaeological evidence without pretending that names, dates, and material remains identify one another automatically?

What is the textual difference between Lamech's appeal to vengeance and the later calling upon the name of the LORD?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The passage records the development of human activities and skills without declaring that technical capability itself establishes spiritual alignment.

The Cainite line includes:
- settlement and city-building;
- pastoral and tent-dwelling life;
- music and instruments;
- metalworking;
- and Lamech's declaration of intensified vengeance.

The Sethite line concludes with people calling upon the name of the LORD.

The contrast is in the text's arrangement. It does not authorize a blanket judgment that every descendant in one line was identical in character, or that every human skill is corrupt.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Human society can develop multiple forms of practical knowledge:
- organizing settlement;
- managing livestock and shelter;
- shaping materials into instruments;
- working metals;
- transmitting skills through generations.

These are expressions of human capability within Creation. They are not the original source of Creation or independent standards of truth.

The passage also records that capability and violence can coexist. Lamech's speech prevents us from treating cultural development as proof of moral progress.

Seth's line closes with calling upon the LORD. The passage therefore distinguishes the development of human culture from the explicit act of invoking the Author.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must:
- report the city without inventing its location or population;
- distinguish Cain's Enoch from Seth's Enoch;
- distinguish the two Lamechs;
- report Lamech's two wives without treating the practice as God's creation command;
- identify the likely instrument categories without equating “organ” with a modern keyboard organ;
- distinguish ancient copper/bronze terminology from modern brass;
- report metalworking without inventing the exact process;
- preserve the ambiguity in Lamech's speech;
- distinguish Lamech's self-declared vengeance from God's protection of Cain;
- and preserve the final statement about calling upon the LORD without claiming that no one previously worshipped or spoke to God.

**How the four questions inform one another:** Human capability, cultural transmission, and moral direction are distinct. The text records the growth of practical skills and the presence of violence, then closes with the calling upon the LORD. GUA-W's Author–Word–Creation–Witness distinction prevents human achievement from being mistaken for Original Cause or spiritual authority.

## 8. GUA-W relationship

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

- **Author / Source:** God is named by Eve as the One who appointed Seth; the passage closes with people calling upon the name of the LORD.
- **Word:** The genealogy and narrative record the family lines, human developments, Lamech's speech, and the calling upon the LORD.
- **Creation:** Human reproduction, settlement, livestock, tents, sound, instruments, metals, and craftsmanship are named.
- **Witness:** The narrator records what is given. Archaeology can witness surviving material traces of settlement, instruments, and metalworking, but cannot identify the named Genesis individuals without independent evidence.
- **Distinctions:** Capability versus alignment; description versus command; genealogy versus archaeological chronology; ancient material terms versus modern categories; vengeance versus protection; invocation versus technological achievement.
- **Boundary:** Genesis 4:17–26 does not date Cain's city, identify his wife, explain every name, specify the exact instruments or metal processes, or settle all of Lamech's wording.
- **Continuity:** Genesis 2:24 provides the creation marriage pattern; Genesis 4:15 provides the sevenfold statement Lamech invokes; Genesis 5 will continue the line of Adam through Seth.

## 10. Triple Constraint review

**Biblical compliance:** The study follows Genesis 4:17–26 in order, distinguishes the two genealogical lines, preserves the text's explicit statements, and does not turn descriptions into commands. It records uncertainty in Lamech's speech, the instrument terms, and the metalworking phrase. It does not claim that Seth is Abel restored or that no worship existed before Enos.

**Scientific coherence:** Archaeological evidence is used to describe settlement, pastoral lifeways, musical instruments, and metallurgy. Chronologies are regional and debated; material remains do not identify the biblical individuals. Ancient copper/bronze terminology is not confused with modern brass. No unsupported scientific date is assigned to Cain, Enoch, Jubal, or Tubal-cain.

**GUA-W consistency:** God remains Original Cause. Human skills and cultural products are derivative capabilities within Creation. Their existence does not make them the Source or prove alignment. The Witness distinguishes what the text states, what archaeology observes, and what remains inference.

**Disposition:** The textual findings are admissible. The identity and location of Cain's city, Cain's wife's ancestry, the exact instrument forms, the precise metalworking process, the interpretation of Lamech's speech, and the nuance of Genesis 4:26 remain unresolved. Archaeological comparisons are contextual, not identifications.

## 11. Findings and questions carried forward

### Established by the text
1. Cain's wife conceived and bore Enoch.
2. Cain built a city and named it after Enoch.
3. The genealogy from Enoch to Lamech is recorded.
4. Lamech took two wives, Adah and Zillah.
5. Jabal is associated with tents and livestock.
6. Jubal is associated with the harp and organ in the KJV.
7. Tubal-cain is associated with artificers in brass and iron; Naamah is named as his sister.
8. Lamech speaks to his wives about killing and invokes a seventy-sevenfold or seventy-times-seven vengeance expression.
9. Adam and Eve have another son, Seth, whom Eve describes as appointed by God in place of Abel.
10. Seth has a son named Enos.
11. The passage says people began to call upon the name of the LORD.

### Supported observations
- The Cainite genealogy records settlement and several forms of specialized human activity.
- The KJV “organ” does not refer to a modern keyboard instrument; *ʿugab* is commonly understood as a pipe/wind instrument.
- Hebrew *neḥoshet* is copper/bronze terminology; modern brass should not be assumed.
- Archaeology documents long and regionally varied histories of settlement, music, and metallurgy.
- Lamech's vengeance statement is not the same as God's protective declaration concerning Cain.
- The final clause creates a strong narrative contrast between Lamech's vengeance and calling upon the LORD.
- Genesis 5's Enoch and Lamech belong to Seth's line and are not the same individuals as the names in Cain's genealogy.

### Unresolved
1. Who was Cain's wife, and what does the wider Genesis genealogy allow us to say without speculation?
2. Where was Cain's city, and what kind of settlement did the text mean by *ʿir*?
3. Is the genealogy in verses 17–18 selective, and what generational information is omitted?
4. What are the precise meanings and derivations of the names in Cain's genealogy?
5. What is the exact force of “father of” in Jabal and Jubal's descriptions?
6. What were the physical forms of the *kinnor* and *ʿugab* in this early setting?
7. What is the precise syntax and meaning of *loteš* in verse 22?
8. Which metal categories and working processes are intended by *neḥoshet* and *barzel* in this context?
9. Does Lamech describe one victim or two, and is the speech a boast, lament, or another form of declaration?
10. What is the precise force of “seventy and sevenfold”?
11. What is the full nuance of *qaraʾ beshem YHWH* in verse 26?
12. Does verse 26 describe the beginning of public invocation or a new form of calling upon the LORD? The verse does not say no one had previously worshipped.
13. How should the genealogical and chronological information in Genesis 4 be compared with Genesis 5 without conflating the two lines?

### Do not claim yet
- That Cain's wife is identified by name or explicitly identified as his sister in this passage.
- That Cain's city has been archaeologically located.
- That the Enoch or Lamech in Genesis 4 is the same person as the one in Genesis 5.
- That Jabal, Jubal, or Tubal-cain invented all pastoralism, music, or metallurgy in a modern universal sense.
- That “organ” means a modern keyboard organ.
- That “brass” necessarily means a modern copper-zinc alloy.
- That Tubal-cain's precise metalworking method is known.
- That Lamech's statement is God's authorization of vengeance.
- That the mark of Cain is extended to Lamech.
- That no worship or calling upon God occurred before Enos.
- That technological development proves spiritual progress or alignment.
