## 1. Passage text — KJV

**Genesis 3:1**  
> Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?

**Genesis 3:2**  
> And the woman said unto the serpent, We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden:

**Genesis 3:3**  
> But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the garden, God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die.

**Genesis 3:4**  
> And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die:

**Genesis 3:5**  
> For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.

## 2. Immediate context — what was actually given?

Before interpreting the conversation, return to the command in Genesis 2:16–17:

> And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:
>
> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

The command contains:
- **A speaker:** the LORD God.
- **A recipient:** the man.
- **A broad permission:** freely eat of every tree of the Garden.
- **A specific prohibition:** do not eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
- **A stated consequence:** “thou shalt surely die.”

Genesis 2:25 immediately precedes the new chapter's encounter: the man and woman were both naked and were not ashamed.

Genesis 3:1 introduces the serpent speaking to the woman. The narrative now records a conversation about what God said.

## 3. Observe the conversation before interpreting it

### 3.1 The serpent's description and opening question — verse 1

The serpent is described as “more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made.”

The KJV word *subtil* means crafty or shrewd in this context. The verse identifies the serpent as a creature the LORD God made. Later Scripture gives further canonical identification; Genesis 3:1 itself should first be read for what it states.

The serpent asks:

> “Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?”

Compare the framing:

- **God's command:** “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat.”
- **The serpent's question:** “Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?”

The serpent's wording places the restriction in the foreground and does not repeat the broad permission that God gave.

**Observation:** The conversation begins with a question about God's words, but the question frames the command differently from its original presentation.

Do not yet claim that every question is deception. The evidence for the serpent's direction becomes clearer in the following verses, especially when he directly contradicts the stated consequence.

### 3.2 The woman's reply — verses 2–3

The woman answers that they may eat of the fruit of the trees of the Garden. She then identifies a prohibition concerning the tree in the midst of the Garden.

Compare the wording carefully:

| God's command — Genesis 2:16–17 | Woman's reply — Genesis 3:2–3 |
|---|---|
| “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat” | “We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden” |
| “Thou shalt not eat of it” | “Ye shall not eat of it” |
| “For in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die” | “Neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die” |

Three details deserve attention:

1. The woman preserves the broad permission to eat from the Garden's trees.
2. She includes “neither shall ye touch it,” which is not included in the command as recorded in Genesis 2:16–17.
3. She says “lest ye die,” rather than repeating God's emphatic wording “thou shalt surely die.”

**Unresolved:** Genesis 3 does not tell us here why the phrase about touching is included. It may reflect an instruction not recorded earlier, the woman's own restatement, or another aspect of the conversation. We must not declare her motive or accuse her of deliberately changing God's word without evidence.

There is also a detail worth preserving: Genesis 2:9 describes both the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil in the midst of the Garden. The woman's phrase “the tree which is in the midst” does not name the tree in the same full wording as Genesis 2:17. The immediate conversation identifies which tree is under discussion, but the wording should still be noticed.

### 3.3 The serpent directly contradicts the consequence — verse 4

> “Ye shall not surely die.”

This is no longer simply a question about what God said. The serpent now states the opposite of God's warning.

- God: “thou shalt surely die.”
- Serpent: “Ye shall not surely die.”

The repeated Hebrew root for dying is used in both statements, with the serpent negating the certainty of the warning. The contrast is direct.

**Textual finding:** The serpent contradicts the consequence God stated in Genesis 2:17.

The Witness must not merge these two statements or report them as equivalent interpretations. They are opposing claims.

### 3.4 The serpent attributes a motive to God — verse 5

The serpent continues:

> “For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.”

The serpent presents three connected claims:
- their eyes will be opened;
- they will be “as gods”;
- they will know good and evil.

He also attributes knowledge and motive to God: “For God doth know…”

Notice the movement in the conversation:
1. Question the wording of God's command.
2. Receive the woman's answer.
3. Deny the stated consequence.
4. Offer an alternative explanation of God's motive.
5. Present the prohibited act as a route to a promised condition.

At verse 5, these are still the serpent's assertions. The next verses must be examined to see what the narrative reports actually happens.

**Language question:** The Hebrew word *Elohim* can be rendered “God” or “gods” according to context. The KJV here says “as gods.” The precise force of the comparison and the phrase “knowing good and evil” deserves careful examination across Scripture; do not reduce it prematurely to one modern definition.

## 4. A significant connection: the closing words of Genesis 2 and the opening of Genesis 3

Genesis 2:25 says the man and woman were “naked” (*arummim*). Genesis 3:1 describes the serpent as “subtil” (*arum*).

The Hebrew words are closely related in sound and form. This creates a literary wordplay at the transition between the chapters.

This is a textual observation. It draws the reader's attention from the pair's stated condition to the serpent's craftiness. It does not, by itself, establish a complete doctrine about nakedness, innocence, or the serpent's nature.

The contrast is worth keeping in view as the narrative continues.

## 6. Creation investigation

No separate scientific comparison is needed to establish the central point of this passage. The subject here is the conversation, the representation of God's command, the contradiction of God's stated consequence, and the serpent's claim about God's motive.

The unified-Creation foundation remains in view: the serpent is described as a creature made by the LORD God. The text's central issue in these five verses, however, is not a biological classification of the serpent. It is the content and authority of the words spoken.

## 7. The Four Questions

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

How does GUA-W distinguish the Author's originating Word and command from a derivative voice that questions, reports, reframes, or contradicts it? What must a Witness do when two speakers make opposing claims about the same command?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The Author-to-Witness distinction helps keep the speakers and their claims separate. God's command is recorded in Genesis 2:16–17. The serpent questions its framing and then directly denies its stated consequence. The serpent's words do not become the Source merely because they are spoken.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The passage presents a progression in speech: a question that foregrounds restriction, the woman's reply, a direct contradiction of God's warning, and an alternative explanation attributed to God's motive. Each statement must be examined in its own wording and compared with the original command.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must preserve the source and context of a statement. It must distinguish quotation from paraphrase, question from assertion, and an attributed motive from what the named speaker actually said. A confident alternative claim is not established merely by being confidently spoken.

**How the four questions inform one another:** The passage makes accurate reception and reporting essential. The Witness must identify who speaks, compare the claim with the original command, and avoid promoting a derivative contradiction to the authority of the Author.

## 8. GUA-W relationship

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

- **Author:** The LORD God is identified as Creator and as the One who gave the command in Genesis 2:16–17.
- **Word:** God's permission, prohibition, and stated consequence are the original words under discussion.
- **Creation:** The Garden, its trees, the woman and man, and the serpent identified as a creature made by the LORD God.
- **Witness:** The woman reports the command; the serpent questions, contradicts, and attributes a motive to God. The reader must preserve the difference between each speaker's words.
- **Boundary:** The prohibition and consequence remain the subject of the conversation.

No GUA-W architecture is changed or added.

## 10. Triple Constraint review

**Biblical compliance:** The study compares Genesis 3:1–5 with the actual command in Genesis 2:16–17, distinguishes the speakers, and does not assign an unsupported motive to the woman. Later Scripture is used as cross-reference, not as a replacement for the immediate passage.

**Scientific coherence:** No scientific claim is required to establish the central textual findings. No forced scientific analogy is introduced.
