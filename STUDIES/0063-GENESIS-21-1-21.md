# Study 0063 — Genesis 21:1–21 (KJV)
## The birth of Isaac, fulfillment of the promise, Hagar and Ishmael, and God's provision in the wilderness

- **Primary passage:** Genesis 21:1–21 (KJV)
- **Previous study:** [Study 0062 — Genesis 20:1–18](0062-GENESIS-20-1-18.md)
- **Next passage:** Genesis 21:22–34 (KJV)
- **Central question:** What does this passage reveal about God's appointed time, the fulfillment of His Word, the distinction between the covenant promise and human arrangements, and God's hearing and provision in the wilderness?

## 1. Immediate context

Genesis 17 records God's covenant promise that Sarah will bear Abraham a son, and that the covenant will be established with Isaac. Genesis 18 repeats the promise and identifies its appointed time. Genesis 20 then records God's intervention to protect Sarah and preserve the circumstances in which the promised child is to be born.

Genesis 21 opens with the fulfillment: the LORD visits Sarah as He had said, and does unto Sarah as He had spoken. Sarah conceives and bears Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken. Abraham names the child Isaac and circumcises him on the eighth day, according to God's command.

Sarah rejoices that God has made her laugh and that others who hear will laugh with her. The child grows and is weaned. At the feast on the day Isaac is weaned, Sarah sees Ishmael, Hagar's son, mocking. She tells Abraham to cast out the bondwoman and her son, because the son of the bondwoman is not to be heir with Isaac.

The matter is grievous to Abraham because of his son. God tells Abraham not to let the matter be grievous in his sight because of the lad and the bondwoman, and to listen to Sarah's voice concerning the covenant line: in Isaac shall Abraham's seed be called. God also says that He will make a nation of the son of the bondwoman because he is Abraham's seed.

Abraham rises early, gives Hagar bread and a bottle of water, sends her away with the child, and she wanders in the wilderness of Beersheba. When the water is spent, she places the child under a shrub and sits at a distance, unwilling to see him die. She weeps. God hears the voice of the lad; the angel of God calls to Hagar, tells her not to fear, and says God has heard the lad where he is. Hagar is told to lift the lad up and hold him in her hand, and God opens her eyes to a well of water. She gives the lad drink.

God is with the lad as he grows. He becomes an archer and dwells in the wilderness of Paran. His mother takes him a wife out of the land of Egypt.

The passage must preserve two truths together: Isaac is the appointed son through whom the covenant line is named, and God also hears, preserves, and provides for Ishmael. The distinction in covenant role is not a statement that Ishmael is unseen or abandoned by God.

## 3. Verses 1–2 — The LORD does as He had spoken

The passage begins by naming the LORD as the One who visits Sarah as He had said and does unto Sarah as He had spoken. Sarah conceives and bears Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him.

### Direct observations

- The LORD is the acting subject of the opening statement.
- The event occurs “as he had said” and “as he had spoken.”
- Sarah conceives and bears a son.
- Abraham is in his old age.
- The birth occurs at the set time God had spoken of.

The repeated connection between God's speech and the event is central. The narrator does not describe Isaac's birth as an accidental outcome or as the product of Abraham and Sarah independently bringing God's promise into existence.

Genesis 18:10, 14 had already spoken of the appointed time. Genesis 21:2 now records the fulfillment according to that word.

### The distinction to preserve

The appointed time belongs to what God had spoken. The passage does not say that Abraham or Sarah determined the time or caused the promise to become true by their own power.

This does not require a claim about every biological detail of conception. The text's stated emphasis is the LORD's faithfulness to His spoken promise and the birth at the time He had appointed.

## 4. Verses 3–5 — Isaac is named and circumcised

Abraham calls the name of his son Isaac, whom Sarah bare unto him. Abraham circumcises Isaac on the eighth day, as God had commanded him. Abraham is one hundred years old when Isaac is born.

### What the text establishes

- The child is Abraham's son by Sarah.
- Abraham names him Isaac.
- Abraham circumcises him on the eighth day.
- This act follows God's command.
- Abraham is one hundred years old at Isaac's birth.

The naming is not improvised in this chapter. Genesis 17:19 had already specified the name Isaac. The eighth-day circumcision follows the covenant instruction given in Genesis 17:12.

The passage joins promise and obedience without confusing them. God gives the promise and establishes the covenant sign; Abraham carries out the command concerning the child.

### Preserve the order

God's word and promise precede Isaac's birth. Abraham's obedience concerning the name and circumcision follows the child's birth. The human act does not author the promise; it responds to what God has commanded.

## 5. Verses 6–7 — Sarah's laughter

Sarah says that God has made her to laugh and that all who hear will laugh with her. She asks who would have said to Abraham that Sarah should have given children suck, and declares that she has borne him a son in his old age.

### Direct observations

- Sarah attributes her laughter to God.
- She anticipates that others who hear will share in the laughter.
- She recognizes the extraordinary character of bearing a son in Abraham's old age.
- The text describes her nursing the child.

The laughter in this passage should be read in the context of Genesis 18:9–15. There Sarah had laughed when she heard the announcement because of her age and the apparent impossibility of the promise. In Genesis 21, her words express the wonder of the promise fulfilled.

The study should not flatten every occurrence of laughter into the same emotional state. The earlier response and the later celebration occur at different points in the narrative.

Sarah's question, “Who would have said unto Abraham, that Sarah should have given children suck?” is an expression of astonishment. The narrator has already told the reader who said it: the LORD had spoken.

## 6. Verse 8 — Isaac grows and is weaned

The child grows and is weaned, and Abraham makes a great feast on the day that Isaac is weaned.

### What is explicit

- Isaac grows.
- He reaches the point of weaning.
- Abraham makes a great feast.
- The feast is associated with the day of weaning.

The text does not specify Isaac's exact age at weaning. No precise age should be inserted as though Genesis states it.

The feast marks a family event and provides the immediate setting for the next scene. The study must not assume that the feast itself caused the conflict. The narrator next records Sarah seeing Ishmael mocking.

## 7. Verses 9–10 — Sarah sees Ishmael mocking

Sarah sees the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne unto Abraham, mocking. Sarah tells Abraham to cast out the bondwoman and her son, because the son of the bondwoman is not to be heir with Isaac.

### Direct observations

- Sarah sees Ishmael.
- The KJV describes Ishmael's action with the word “mocking.”
- Sarah identifies him as Hagar's son.
- Sarah demands that Hagar and her son be cast out.
- Sarah's stated concern is inheritance: the son of the bondwoman is not to be heir with Isaac.

The text does not provide a detailed description of the mocking or quote Ishmael's words. The precise action and tone should not be filled in with invented dialogue.

Galatians 4:29 later refers to the child born after the flesh persecuting the child born after the Spirit. That later apostolic interpretation is relevant to the canonical reading, but it should not be used to pretend Genesis 21 itself gives a fuller description than it does.

### Sarah's demand and the covenant distinction

Sarah's words concern the inheritance relationship between Ishmael and Isaac. The following verses show that God addresses Abraham directly and identifies Isaac as the line in which Abraham's seed will be called.

Sarah's demand is not itself the source of the covenant distinction. God's word in verse 12 establishes the relevant line.

## 8. Verses 11–13 — Abraham's grief and God's instruction

The matter is very grievous in Abraham's sight because of his son. God tells Abraham not to let the matter be grievous in his sight because of the lad and the bondwoman, and to listen to Sarah's voice in the matter. God states that in Isaac shall Abraham's seed be called.

God also says that He will make a nation of the son of the bondwoman because he is Abraham's seed.

### Two statements that must remain together

**Concerning Isaac:**
- God identifies Isaac as the line in which Abraham's seed will be called.
- This is the covenant-line distinction in the passage.

**Concerning Ishmael:**
- God promises to make a nation of him.
- The reason given is that he is Abraham's seed.
- Ishmael is not excluded from God's knowledge or provision.

The text does not say that Ishmael is the covenant heir in the same sense as Isaac. It also does not say that God abandons Ishmael.

### The distinction between promise and human arrangement

Genesis 16 records the earlier human arrangement involving Hagar and the birth of Ishmael. Genesis 17:18–21 distinguishes God's promise concerning Isaac from His word concerning Ishmael. Genesis 21 now carries that distinction into the family events.

The human arrangement does not replace the specific promise God made concerning Sarah and Isaac. Yet God's word concerning Ishmael also stands: God will make him a nation.

### Abraham's grief

Abraham's grief is explicitly recorded. The text does not say that he feels no love for Ishmael or that he is indifferent to Hagar. God addresses the grief and gives Abraham direction.

The study must preserve Abraham's emotional response rather than treating the departure as a merely administrative change in household membership.

## 9. Verse 14 — Hagar and Ishmael depart

Abraham rises early in the morning, takes bread and a bottle of water, gives them to Hagar, puts them on her shoulder, and sends her away with the child. She departs and wanders in the wilderness of Beersheba.

### Direct observations

- Abraham acts early in the morning.
- He provides bread and water.
- Hagar and Ishmael are sent away.
- They travel into the wilderness of Beersheba.
- Hagar wanders there.

The verse records the provisions Abraham gives. It does not specify their weight, quantity, or how long they were expected to last.

The wording concerning the child and Hagar's carrying should be read carefully in the KJV and its Hebrew context. Genesis 21:15–18 calls Ishmael a lad and describes him as being under a shrub. Genesis 16:16 and 17:25 establish that Ishmael was born before Isaac's birth; Genesis 21 does not give Isaac's exact weaning age. Do not invent a precise age for Ishmael from this passage.

The text does not say that Abraham personally accompanies them into the wilderness. It says he sends them away and they depart.

## 10. Verses 15–16 — The water is spent and Hagar weeps

When the water in the bottle is spent, Hagar casts the child under one of the shrubs. She goes and sits down over against him a good way off, as it were a bowshot, because she says she will not see the death of the child. She sits over against him, lifts up her voice, and weeps.

### What the passage states

- The water is exhausted.
- Hagar places the lad under a shrub.
- She moves a distance away.
- The distance is described as about a bowshot.
- She fears the child will die.
- She weeps.

The text presents an acute wilderness crisis. Hagar believes the child is near death and cannot bear to watch.

The phrase “as it were a bowshot” gives a distance comparison; it does not establish an exact modern measurement.

The narrator does not describe the child's medical condition, the temperature, the duration without water, or the precise distance in metres. Those details must not be invented.

The scene is not the end of the account. The next verse identifies God's response.

## 11. Verses 17–18 — God hears the lad

God hears the voice of the lad, and the angel of God calls to Hagar out of heaven. He asks what is troubling her, tells her not to fear, and says God has heard the voice of the lad where he is.

The angel tells Hagar to arise, lift up the lad, and hold him in her hand, because God will make him a great nation.

### Direct observations

- God hears the lad's voice.
- The angel of God calls to Hagar from heaven.
- Hagar is told not to fear.
- The message explicitly says God has heard the lad where he is.
- Hagar is instructed to arise, lift up the lad, and hold him.
- God's promise to make Ishmael a great nation is reaffirmed.

The text explicitly identifies the lad's voice as heard by God. Hagar's weeping is also recorded in verse 16, but verse 17 specifically says God hears the lad.

The promise concerning Ishmael in verse 18 connects back to verse 13. God's care in the wilderness is consistent with the earlier word that Ishmael would become a nation.

### A distinction concerning the angel

The narrative says the angel of God calls to Hagar, while the message speaks of what God has heard and what God will do. The text presents God's communication and action; the study should not speculate beyond the passage about the mechanics of the heavenly messenger.

## 12. Verse 19 — God opens Hagar's eyes to a well

God opens Hagar's eyes, and she sees a well of water. She goes, fills the bottle with water, and gives the lad drink.

### What is explicit

- God opens Hagar's eyes.
- Hagar sees a well of water.
- She goes to the well.
- She fills the bottle.
- She gives the lad drink.

The text attributes the opening of Hagar's eyes to God. It does not say that Hagar created the well or that the well suddenly came into existence at that moment.

This distinction is important. The narrative says God opened her eyes to see the provision. It does not specify whether the well had been physically hidden, obscured by the terrain, or simply not perceived in the crisis.

The study must not transform “God opened her eyes” into a claim about the physical creation of the well. Nor should it reduce the statement to a purely psychological metaphor when the passage records an actual well from which Hagar draws water.

### Provision and response

God's action and Hagar's action are both recorded:
- God opens her eyes.
- Hagar sees.
- Hagar goes and draws water.
- Hagar gives the lad drink.

The human response does not replace the divine provision; it follows the provision made visible to her.

## 13. Verses 20–21 — God is with Ishmael

God is with the lad, and he grows. He dwells in the wilderness and becomes an archer. He dwells in the wilderness of Paran, and his mother takes him a wife out of the land of Egypt.

### What is explicit

- God is with Ishmael.
- Ishmael grows.
- He becomes an archer.
- He dwells in the wilderness of Paran.
- His mother takes him a wife from Egypt.

The statement “God was with the lad” is direct and should not be minimized. Ishmael is not the covenant-line son identified in verse 12, but the passage explicitly records God's presence with him.

The text does not give the name of Ishmael's wife or the exact date of the marriage.

The chapter therefore ends this unit not with Ishmael's death or disappearance, but with his continued life, growth, and family future.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

I am witnessing the LORD fulfill what He had spoken to Sarah at the appointed time. Isaac is born, named, and circumcised according to God's command. Sarah rejoices. Later, a conflict concerning inheritance arises between the sons of Sarah and Hagar. God identifies Isaac as the covenant line while also promising a nation through Ishmael.

I am then witnessing Hagar and Ishmael in the wilderness, the exhaustion of their water, Hagar's grief, God's hearing of the lad, the message from the angel of God, the opening of Hagar's eyes to a well, and God's continued presence with Ishmael.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must not claim that the human actors produced the promise. The LORD visits Sarah and does as He had spoken. The appointed time is connected to God's word.

A Witness must also avoid collapsing two different statements: Isaac is the son through whom the covenant line is called, and Ishmael is a son whom God hears, preserves, and promises to make a nation.

A Witness must record the difficult family conflict without inventing the precise details of Ishmael's mocking or assigning motives not stated. It must also record God's care for Hagar and Ishmael rather than allowing the covenant distinction to erase that care.

### How the questions inform one another

The passage begins with God's word fulfilled at the appointed time and moves through the consequences of the distinction between the promised son and the son born through the earlier household arrangement. The covenant line is identified by God's word, not by human preference alone. Yet God's care extends to Ishmael in the wilderness. The same passage records both a particular covenant promise and God's provision beyond that line.

## 18. Triple Constraint review

### Biblical compliance — Pass, with both sons and both divine statements preserved

The study follows Genesis 21:1–21 in sequence. It preserves the fulfillment of God's word to Sarah at the appointed time, Isaac's naming and circumcision, Sarah's laughter, the weaning feast, Ishmael's mocking as the text describes it, Sarah's demand, Abraham's grief, God's instruction concerning Isaac and Ishmael, Hagar's departure, the wilderness crisis, God's hearing, the well, and God's presence with Ishmael.

It does not invent the precise nature of the mocking, an exact age for Isaac at weaning, an exact quantity of water, or a medical explanation for the wilderness crisis. It does not confuse Isaac's covenant role with God's care for Ishmael.

### Scientific coherence — No unsupported physical mechanism asserted

The passage reports conception, birth, growth, weaning, thirst, a well, and the provision of water. It does not supply medical measurements, a precise chronology for weaning, or the physical mechanism by which Hagar first failed to see the well and then saw it.

The study reports the narrative as written and does not invent scientific details. Any separate investigation of historical geography, ancient water storage, human hydration, or the biology of reproduction would require independent evidence and must remain distinct from the text's theological claims.

### GUA-W consistency — Pass

The study preserves Author → Word → Creation → Witness. The LORD speaks and fulfills His promise at the appointed time. Abraham responds to God's command. Isaac is identified as the covenant-line son. God also hears Ishmael and provides for him.

The study does not transfer the Author's role to Sarah's demand, Abraham's household arrangements, or Hagar's actions. It does not introduce a new GUA-W layer, operator, or law.

**Result:** Admissible for this study. The particular covenant line and God's care for Ishmael are both retained without contradiction or conflation.
