# Study 0050 — Genesis 12:1–9 (KJV)
## The LORD calls Abram: Promise, departure, land, and worship

**Study status:** Investigated and recorded. The passage's main sequence is clear: divine command and promise, Abram's departure, arrival in Canaan, the LORD's appearance and land promise, and Abram's altars and invocation. The precise chronology of the initial call in relation to Ur and Haran is considered with Acts 7:2–4. The translation of Genesis 12:3b, the identity/status of the people acquired in Haran, and the exact locations of some named sites remain matters for careful qualification.

**Primary passage:** Genesis 12:1–9 (KJV)  
**Previous study:** [Study 0049 — Genesis 11:27–32](0049-GENESIS-11-27-32.md)  
**Next passage:** Genesis 12:10–20

**Central question:** What does God initiate, what does Abram receive, and what does Abram do with what he has received?

## 2. Immediate context

Genesis 11:27–32 introduces Terah's household and records the family's movement from Ur toward Canaan, followed by its settlement in Haran. Genesis 12:1–9 continues that movement, but gives the journey a further and decisive dimension: the LORD's command and promises to Abram.

The preceding Babel account (Genesis 11:1–9) records a people seeking to make a name for themselves and avoid being scattered. Genesis 12:2 now records God's promise: “I will ... make thy name great.” This is a meaningful literary contrast in the final form of the narrative. It does not mean that every detail of the Babel account is a direct prediction of Abram, but it places human self-exaltation beside a name bestowed by God.

The movement is also striking:
- Babel: people seek to make their own name great.
- Abram: God promises to make Abram's name great.
- Babel: the families of humanity are scattered.
- Abram: all families of the earth are included in the promise of blessing.

The text moves from the nations to one called man and his household, while the stated horizon of blessing remains all the families of the earth.

8. Abram departs as the LORD had spoken; Lot accompanies him.
9. Abram is 75 years old when he departs from Haran.

13. The Canaanite is then in the land.
14. The LORD appears to Abram and promises the land to his seed.

The passage distinguishes God's promises from their fulfillment. Abram reaches Canaan, but the Canaanite is already in the land; the land is promised to Abram's seed, not recorded as an immediate possession by Abram. The text records altars, but does not explicitly say that Abram offers an animal sacrifice at either altar in this passage.

## 4. The call and the promise — Genesis 12:1–3

### 4.1 “Get thee out”

The command names three relational/geographical spheres:
- “thy country”;
- “thy kindred”;
- “thy father's house.”

The command therefore involves departure from homeland, extended kinship, and the immediate paternal household. The text does not say Abram must cease caring for family or that all family members are forbidden to accompany him: Lot goes with him in verse 4, and Sarai is named in verse 5.

The destination is initially left unspecified: “unto a land that I will shew thee.” The promise of divine direction accompanies the command. Abram is not given a route or a complete itinerary in this verse.

### 4.2 *Lekh-lekha* — “Get thee out”

The Hebrew expression is לֶךְ־לְךָ (*lekh-lekha*), formed from the imperative “go” and a second-person suffix. It is often rendered “go,” “go forth,” or “go for yourself.” The expression is emphatic and has attracted interpretive discussion. It should not be made to carry a hidden doctrine or a precise psychological meaning that the syntax alone cannot establish.

The following phrase, “I will shew thee,” places the showing of the destination with God. The text gives the command and the promised direction; it does not disclose every detail in advance.

### 4.3 The promises in verses 2–3

The promises are expressed as God's own future actions:
- “I will make of thee a great nation.”
- “I will bless thee.”
- “I will make thy name great.”
- “I will bless them that bless thee.”
- “I will curse him that curseth thee.”
- “In thee shall all families of the earth be blessed.”

Between the promises, Abram receives the imperative: “and thou shalt be a blessing.” The Hebrew form of “be” is an imperative. Thus the verse includes both what God promises to do and what Abram is told to be. The precise nuance of “be a blessing” is discussed below; it should not be reduced to passive receipt alone.

The promise is not presented as a human plan that Abram originates. God speaks in the first person: “I will.” Abram's action follows in verse 4.

### 4.4 The name contrast with Babel

Genesis 11:4 records the builders saying, “let us make us a name.” Genesis 12:2 records God saying to Abram, “I ... will ... make thy name great.”

This is a strong literary contrast:
- Babel's name-making is a human project.
- Abram's greatness of name is promised by God.

The contrast concerns the source of the name-making, not a claim that having a recognized name is itself wrong. Genesis 12:2 does not condemn all human reputation; it records God's promise to Abram.

### 4.5 “In thee shall all families of the earth be blessed” — translation question

The KJV rendering is preserved as the study text. The Hebrew verb in Genesis 12:3b is a Niphal form of ברך (*barakh*, bless). The Niphal can be discussed as passive, middle, or reflexive/reciprocal in this construction. Consequently, translations and scholars differ over whether the sense is:
- “shall be blessed” through/in Abram (passive);
- “shall find blessing” through association with Abram (middle);
- “shall bless themselves” or use Abram as a model/formula of blessing (reflexive).

The NET note outlines the passive and reflexive/reciprocal possibilities. Chee-Chiew Lee's Journal for the Study of the Old Testament article argues for a passive nuance in the Niphal and a distinct indirect-reflexive nuance in the Hithpael forms elsewhere in the Abrahamic promise tradition. Other scholarship argues for a reflexive or middle reading. The grammar is debated; do not report one proposed nuance as universally settled.

The New Testament explicitly receives the promise in a blessing-to-the-nations sense. Acts 3:25 quotes the promise in connection with the covenant to Abraham; Galatians 3:8 says Scripture foresaw that God would justify the heathen through faith and preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham. Paul's use is canonical evidence for the promise's reach to the nations. It does not erase the need to report the Hebrew translation discussion accurately.

The scope is not merely Abram's immediate household. The text names “all families of the earth.” Genesis 18:18 and 22:18, 26:4, and 28:14 repeat and develop the promise, including later references to the seed.

## 5. Abram's response and household — Genesis 12:4–5

### 5.1 “So Abram departed”

Verse 4 explicitly connects Abram's departure to what the LORD had spoken: “as the LORD had spoken unto him.” The order in the passage is divine speech and promise, followed by Abram's departure.

Abram is 75 when he departs from Haran. This links directly to the chronology examined in Study 0049. Genesis 11:32 gives Terah's death at 205 in the KJV/MT; Acts 7:4 says Abram left Haran after his father died. The age and birth-order question remains open and is not solved by Genesis 12:4 alone.

### 5.2 Who goes?

Verse 4 names Lot as accompanying Abram. Verse 5 names:
- Sarai, Abram's wife;
- Lot, his brother's son;
- their gathered substance;
- “the souls that they had gotten in Haran.”

The household is not described as Abram traveling alone. The departure affects a household and its dependents/property.

### 5.3 “The souls that they had gotten in Haran”

The Hebrew expression is *hann nefesh asher asu* — literally, “the persons/lives that they had made/acquired in Haran.” *Nephesh* can refer to a living person or persons; in this context it does not by itself establish a later philosophical definition of an immaterial soul.

Interpretations include:
- household servants or dependents acquired in Haran;
- people born into the household there;
- converts or adherents associated with Abram and Sarai, a traditional Jewish interpretive reading.

Rashi records the convert interpretation from Genesis Rabbah but also says the plain sense refers to male and female servants acquired. Other commentators likewise favor servants/dependents in the immediate property-and-household context. The verse does not specify the exact status or number of these people. Therefore the study records “people/persons acquired or gathered in Haran” as the cautious sense and leaves their exact status open. Do not assert that they were all converts, or that the verse gives a precise legal status.

### 5.4 Arrival in Canaan

Verse 5 states that they went forth to go into Canaan and “into the land of Canaan they came.” Unlike Genesis 11:31, which records the family's settlement in Haran while Canaan is the intended destination, Genesis 12:5 records arrival in Canaan.

This does not mean Abram immediately possesses the land. The next verses distinguish arrival, movement through the land, the presence of Canaanites, and a promise to Abram's seed.

## 6. The land and its inhabitants — Genesis 12:6–7

### 6.1 Shechem and the oak/plain of Moreh

The KJV calls the place “Sichem” and the “plain of Moreh.” The place is commonly identified with Shechem in the central hill country. The Hebrew term for the tree/site at Moreh is commonly rendered oak or terebinth in modern translations. The NET note says “Moreh” means “teacher” and raises the possibility that the tree/site was associated with a Canaanite shrine; this is a proposal, not an explicit statement of Genesis.

Shechem occupied a strategically important position in the central hill country, in the pass between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim. Its location connected routes through the region. These geographical observations help orient the narrative but do not establish the exact location of the oak of Moreh or prove every detail of Abram's route.

### 6.2 “The Canaanite was then in the land”

This parenthetical observation matters. Abram has arrived in the land that God will promise to his seed, but the text states that the Canaanite is already there.

The promise is therefore not described as immediate possession or an empty territory awaiting Abram. It is a promise whose realization lies beyond the present moment. Genesis later develops the land promise through Genesis 13:14–17; 15:18–21; 17:8; and 26:3–4.

The phrase also anticipates the later history of the land and its inhabitants. It should not be turned into a modern political claim without the broader canonical, historical, and ethical context.

### 6.3 “Unto thy seed will I give this land”

The Hebrew word *zeraʿ* can mean seed or offspring/descendants. It is grammatically singular in form but often functions collectively for descendants. Genesis 12:7 in context promises the land to Abram's offspring; the verse does not specify the full later sequence of descendants.

Later passages expand the promise:
- Genesis 13:14–17 — land and offspring;
- Genesis 15:5–7, 18–21 — descendants and covenantal land promise;
- Genesis 17:7–8 — covenant with Abraham and his seed, and the land;
- Genesis 22:17–18 — seed multiplied and blessing to the nations;
- Genesis 26:3–4 — promise repeated to Isaac;
- Genesis 28:13–14 — promise repeated to Jacob.

The New Testament gives a Christological reading of the seed promise in Galatians 3:16. That canonical interpretation should be reported as Paul's reading, not as a claim that the Hebrew noun *zeraʿ* can only mean one individual in every Genesis occurrence.

### 6.4 The LORD appeared

Genesis 12:7 explicitly says that the LORD appeared to Abram and spoke the land promise. The passage does not describe the physical form or mechanics of the appearance. Do not supply a visual or physical description that the text does not provide.

Abram's response is to build an altar to the LORD who appeared to him. The text says that an altar is built; it does not narrate an offering at this altar in verses 1–9.

## 7. The tent, the second altar, and calling upon the LORD — Genesis 12:8–9

Abram moves to a mountain/elevated area east of Bethel, pitches his tent, and locates Bethel to the west and Hai/Ai to the east. He builds another altar and calls upon the name of the LORD.

The passage presents two forms of location and movement:
- the tent: a dwelling arrangement associated with continued travel;
- the altar: a marked place of worship/invocation.

The text does not say that Abram has built a permanent city or taken possession of the territory. Hebrews 11:9 later describes Abraham as dwelling in the land of promise as in a foreign country, in tents, with Isaac and Jacob. Hebrews 11:10 points to his expectation of a city with foundations whose builder and maker is God.

“Called upon the name of the LORD” also appears in Genesis 4:26 and recurs in Genesis 13:4 and 21:33. In Genesis 12:8 the phrase is associated with Abram's altar. It indicates invocation/worship, though the verse does not provide the words of a prayer or liturgy.

Verse 9 says Abram continues toward the south. The Hebrew geographical term is associated with the Negeb/south country. The verse records continued movement, not a final settlement.

## 8. The KJV “had said” and the chronology of the call

Genesis 12:1 in the KJV reads, “Now the LORD had said unto Abram.” The Hebrew opening is *wayyomer YHWH* (“and the LORD said”). The Hebrew form is a standard narrative wayyiqtol form, normally translated “and he said.” The KJV's “had said” reflects a chronological reading of the narrative in relation to Terah's journey and the later departure from Haran.

Acts 7:2–4 is essential to this question:
- Acts 7:2 says the God of glory appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia before he lived in Haran.
- Acts 7:3 records the command to leave his country and kindred.
- Acts 7:4 says Abraham then left the land of the Chaldeans and lived in Haran; after his father died, he moved into the land in which the audience now lived.

This provides canonical evidence that the appearance and call occurred in Mesopotamia before Abraham's residence in Haran. Genesis 12:1–4 then narrates the command and Abram's departure from Haran at age 75, while Genesis 11:31–32 has already narrated Terah's household journey and death.

The KJV's “had said” is compatible with reading the call as previously given, but the Hebrew verb form by itself does not encode the English pluperfect “had said.” The chronology must be read with Acts 7 and the prior Genesis account, not decided by one English tense alone.

**Unresolved chronology carried forward:** Genesis 11:26 does not explicitly establish Abram's birth order. Genesis 11:32 gives Terah's age as 205 in the KJV/MT, while the Samaritan Pentateuch has a 145-year reading. Genesis 12:4 gives Abram's age as 75 at departure from Haran. Acts 7:4 says this departure followed Terah's death. The competing reconstructions remain open; this study does not silently harmonize or alter the KJV text.

## 10. Creation and historical investigation

### Question

What can geographical and archaeological evidence contribute to understanding Abram's movement through Canaan, and what does it leave unresolved?

### Relevant fields

Historical geography and archaeology are relevant to the named locations Shechem, Bethel, and Ai. This is contextual investigation rather than a claim that archaeology verifies Abram personally.

### What can be responsibly stated

Shechem is commonly located in the central hill country between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim. Its position at a pass and near important routes helps explain why it appears repeatedly in later biblical narratives. The oak/terebinth of Moreh is a textual landmark; its exact identification is not established by the passage.

The text's geography describes movement through a populated land. The Canaanites are explicitly present. The land promise is therefore not represented as an immediate empty-land possession.

### What the evidence does not establish

- Archaeology does not independently identify Abram, Sarai, Lot, or the altar described here.
- The exact oak or terebinth of Moreh has not been securely identified from this text.
- The exact route and duration of Abram's travel are not given.
- The precise identification of Ai in this period is debated.
- Archaeological remains at a site cannot by themselves prove the biblical event or identify its participants.

No scientific mechanism is required to explain the call, promise, divine appearance, or blessing. No artificial scientific analogy is added. The historical-geographical evidence is kept in its proper role as contextual witness.

## 11. The Four GUA-W Questions

The four questions are recursive and mutually informative, not four sequential stages.

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- How do we distinguish the Author's initiating Word from the Witness's response?
- How should the map represent a promise whose fulfillment is not immediate?
- How do we distinguish command, promise, response, and later manifestation?
- How do we record a translation debate without replacing the KJV text or declaring a disputed grammatical option settled?
- How do we distinguish the biblical word *nephesh* in context from later philosophical definitions of “soul”?
- What is the proper relation between a textual place-name and archaeological identification?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The established Source-to-Witness order helps keep the passage's actions distinct:
- God speaks and promises.
- Abram receives the command and departs.
- God appears and repeats the land promise.
- Abram responds with altars and invocation.

The map can register these relationships, but it cannot become the Author or add an explanation the text does not provide. The promise is not the same thing as its completed manifestation.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The passage records divine initiative, a command to leave, promises concerning nation/name/blessing, Abram's response at age 75, the household's arrival in Canaan, the presence of Canaanites, the promise of land to Abram's seed, and worship at two altars.

The promise's horizon includes all families of the earth. Later Scripture develops the promise in relation to Abraham's descendants and, in Paul's interpretation, Christ and the inclusion of the Gentiles.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must:
- distinguish a command from the response to it;
- distinguish promise from fulfillment;
- distinguish a land promised to descendants from immediate possession by Abram;
- distinguish the KJV's rendering from a debated Hebrew construction;
- distinguish an altar from an explicitly narrated sacrifice;
- distinguish a people/persons term from an assumed spiritual or legal status;
- distinguish a geographical landmark from an archaeologically proven identification.

### How the questions inform one another

The text reveals an order that the Witness must preserve: God speaks and promises; Abram departs; God appears and reiterates the land promise; Abram worships. The map helps organize the order but cannot replace the passage. Cross-references clarify the wider scope of the promise while also requiring care with chronology and interpretation. The study leaves the unresolved matters visible for the next investigation.

### Supported observations and interpretations

- Genesis 11:4 and 12:2 create a literary contrast between humanity's attempt to make a name and God's promise to make Abram's name great.
- The Babel account's scattering of peoples is followed by a promise whose stated horizon includes all families of the earth.
- God's command and promise precede Abram's recorded departure.
- Abram's arrival in Canaan is not the same as possession of the land.
- The two altar scenes mark the LORD's appearance/promise and Abram's invocation.
- The promise continues through later Genesis narratives and receives New Testament interpretation in Acts and Galatians.

### Unresolved questions carried forward

1. What is the full nuance of *lekh-lekha* in Genesis 12:1?
2. Does “be thou a blessing” emphasize Abram as a channel of blessing, a model/example of blessing, or both?
3. How should the Niphal of *barakh* in Genesis 12:3b be translated: passive, middle, or reflexive/reciprocal?
4. What is the exact status of the people acquired in Haran in Genesis 12:5?
5. What was the precise location of the oak/terebinth of Moreh?
6. What is the most defensible identification of Ai/Hai in the setting of Genesis 12:8?
7. How should the KJV “had said” in Genesis 12:1 be related to the Hebrew *wayyomer* and Acts 7:2–4?
8. How do the ages and birth-order possibilities in Genesis 11:26, 11:32, 12:4, and Acts 7:4 fit together?
9. How does the land promise unfold through the later covenant passages without collapsing promise, inheritance, and possession into one moment?
10. How should Paul's interpretation of the seed in Galatians 3:16 be related to the collective use of *zeraʿ* in Genesis?

### What must not be claimed

- That Abram originated the promise or earned the LORD's call.
- That Abram's departure itself fulfilled every promise.
- That all families of the earth were already blessed in their completed historical sense by the end of verse 9.
- That one debated grammatical reading of Genesis 12:3b is beyond scholarly discussion.
- That the people acquired in Haran are certainly converts or certainly enslaved persons.
- That the altar scenes necessarily include sacrifices not narrated in the passage.
- That Canaan was unoccupied when Abram arrived.
- That archaeology has identified Abram, his household, or his altars.
- That Genesis 12:1's English pluperfect alone settles the call's chronology.
- That the promise of land means Abram personally takes possession in this passage.

## 16. Continuity record

- **Current conclusion:** Genesis 12:1–9 records the LORD's command and promises to Abram, Abram's departure from Haran at 75, the household's arrival in Canaan, the LORD's appearance and land promise, and Abram's worship through two altars and invocation of the LORD.
- **Major literary relationship:** Babel's “make us a name” (Genesis 11:4) is placed beside God's “make thy name great” (Genesis 12:2); the promise to Abram is not limited to his household but reaches all families of the earth.
- **Important GUA-W distinction:** The Author initiates and speaks; Abram receives and responds. The Witness records the order without becoming the Source.
- **Unresolved questions carried forward:** Hebrew nuance of *lekh-lekha*; “be a blessing”; Niphal of *barakh*; people acquired in Haran; Moreh/Ai locations; Genesis 12:1 and Acts 7 chronology; Terah/Abram ages; development of land and seed promises.
- **Next passage:** Genesis 12:10–20 (KJV), famine in the land, Abram's journey into Egypt, Sarai's presentation as his sister, Pharaoh's household, and the LORD's intervention.
- **Study remains open.**
