## 2. Immediate context

Genesis 3:6–7 records the woman and man eating the fruit, the opening of their eyes, their recognition that they were naked, and their making coverings from fig leaves.

Genesis 3:8–13 continues without changing the subject. The pair hear the voice of the LORD God, hide, and are addressed by God. The man answers; God asks further questions; the man speaks about the woman; God addresses the woman; and she speaks about the serpent.

The passage gives the first recorded human responses after the eating:
- Adam reports hearing, fear, nakedness, and hiding.
- Adam says the woman gave him the fruit and acknowledges, “I did eat.”
- The woman says the serpent beguiled her and acknowledges, “I did eat.”

The text records explanations as spoken by the people. Their explanations must be distinguished from the narrator's account in verses 6–7.

## 3. Observe the sequence before interpreting it

### 3.1 They hear and hide — verse 8

The verse begins, “And they heard the voice of the LORD God…”

The KJV describes the LORD God as “walking in the garden in the cool of the day.” The Hebrew expression rendered “in the cool of the day” includes *ruach* (wind, breath, or spirit, according to context) and *yom* (day). The traditional KJV rendering is “cool of the day”; translations differ in how they express the phrase. The wording should be noted without claiming that the Hebrew phrase settles the precise weather, time of day, or physical mechanism.

The verse then says Adam and his wife hid themselves “from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden.”

Notice the contrast with Genesis 2:25. Before this event, the man and woman were naked and not ashamed. After eating and recognizing their nakedness, they have made coverings; now they hide among the Garden's trees when they hear the LORD God's voice.

The narrative gives a change in their response to God's presence. It does not yet give a full explanation of every dimension of that change.

**Textual observations:**
- They hear the voice.
- They hide themselves.
- The hiding is described as being from the presence of the LORD God.
- The trees of the Garden are the place among which they hide.

Do not infer that the trees successfully conceal them from God. The text reports their attempt to hide, not that God lost sight or knowledge of them.

### 3.2 God calls to Adam — verse 9

> “Where art thou?”

God calls to Adam, not first to the woman. The question is direct and personal.

The wording is a question about Adam's location or state of presence. The verse does not say God lacks knowledge of Adam's location. In the context, Adam has hidden, and God calls him into the conversation.

It is reasonable to understand the question as summoning Adam to answer, but that is an interpretation of the narrative function of the question, not an explicit statement that the verse itself defines God's purpose in asking.

**Important distinction:** A question asked by the Author does not imply that the Author is dependent upon the respondent for information. We must not assign ignorance to God simply because the text records Him asking a question.

### 3.3 Adam reports hearing, fear, nakedness, and hiding — verse 10

Adam answers:

> “I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.”

His answer gives a stated sequence:
1. He heard God's voice.
2. He was afraid.
3. He gives nakedness as the reason for his fear.
4. He hid himself.

Compare this with verse 8. The narrator says they hid among the trees. Adam now gives his own account of why he hid: fear associated with nakedness.

The pair had already recognized their nakedness in verse 7 and made coverings. Yet Adam now says he was afraid because he was naked. The fig-leaf coverings did not prevent the reported fear or hiding. The passage does not yet explain the full relation between nakedness, fear, guilt, and the coverings.

**Observation:** The man's own explanation connects fear with nakedness. The narrator has already connected the recognition of nakedness with the eating and opened eyes.

### 3.4 God's two questions — verse 11

God asks:

> “Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?”

These are two questions and should not be collapsed into one.

**First question: “Who told thee that thou wast naked?”**

This returns to Adam's stated reason in verse 10. Adam said he was afraid because he was naked. God asks who told him this.

The question directs attention to the source of Adam's new recognition. The passage does not yet provide Adam's answer to this first question as a separate sentence. Adam's next statement refers to the woman and the fruit, but does not explicitly say, “She told me I was naked.”

**Second question: “Hast thou eaten of the tree…?”**

God brings the conversation directly to the command previously given in Genesis 2:16–17. The command is identified as God's own command to Adam.

This question places Adam's action in relation to the original boundary. It is not merely a question about the fruit as an object; it recalls the command and asks whether Adam ate from the prohibited tree.

**Observation:** God's questions move from Adam's reported knowledge to the specific act in relation to the command.

### 3.5 Adam's answer — verse 12

Adam says:

> “The woman whom thou gavest to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I did eat.”

The answer contains three parts:
1. He identifies the woman.
2. He says God gave her to be with him.
3. He says she gave him fruit from the tree.
4. He then explicitly acknowledges: “and I did eat.”

The wording places the woman and God's gift of her into Adam's explanation before his admission. The text records what Adam says; it does not require the reader to accept his framing as a complete or adequate account of responsibility.

The narrator already said in Genesis 3:6 that the woman gave the fruit and the man ate. Adam's statement confirms that he ate, while emphasizing the woman's role in giving it to him.

**Important distinction:** Adam does not deny eating. His sentence includes an explicit admission. At the same time, he foregrounds the woman and refers to her as the one God gave him. The text allows us to observe that emphasis; it does not authorize us to claim every unspoken motive behind his wording.

### 3.6 God's question to the woman and her answer — verse 13

God asks the woman:

> “What is this that thou hast done?”

The question follows Adam's answer and turns to the woman's action. It does not erase the man's action or make the woman the only participant.

The woman answers:

> “The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat.”

Her answer also has two parts:
1. She identifies the serpent's beguiling as part of what happened.
2. She acknowledges, “and I did eat.”

Compare the answers:
- Adam: the woman gave me the fruit, “and I did eat.”
- Woman: the serpent beguiled me, “and I did eat.”

Both identify another participant in the sequence, and both explicitly acknowledge eating. The narrator's earlier account remains the controlling record of the actions: the woman took and ate, then gave to her husband, and he ate.

The woman's word “beguiled” is important. It connects with the serpent's speech in Genesis 3:1–5. Later Scripture also refers to Eve being beguiled (2 Corinthians 11:3; 1 Timothy 2:14). The woman's report is consistent with the narrative's prior description of the serpent's questioning and contradiction, but the report does not make her eating disappear.

**Observation:** Both people acknowledge their own eating while identifying another participant in the events.

## 4. Language and contextual observations

### 4.1 “Voice” — *qol*

The Hebrew word *qol* can refer to a voice, sound, or noise, depending on context. Here the narrative identifies it as the voice of the LORD God, which the pair hear in the Garden. The passage does not describe the sound's physical properties or provide a scientific account of how it was produced.

### 4.2 “Walking” — *halak*

The Hebrew verb translated “walking” is from *halak*, a common verb for going or walking. The verse presents the LORD God as walking in the Garden. This is the narrative's description. The study should neither erase the wording nor pretend that this one phrase answers every theological question about divine presence and manifestation.

### 4.3 “Where art thou?” — *'ayyekka*

The Hebrew expression is a direct question to Adam: “Where are you?” Its narrative setting is the man's hiding. The question brings Adam into the open exchange. It does not establish that God needed Adam to supply information that God did not possess.

### 4.4 “Afraid” — *yare*

Adam uses a Hebrew term from the *yare* word family, commonly associated with fear or being afraid. His own answer supplies the stated connection: he was afraid because he was naked.

The text reports Adam's stated reason. It does not yet provide a complete psychological analysis of his condition.

### 4.5 “Beguiled” — *nasha*

The woman's word translated “beguiled” is from the Hebrew *nasha*, associated with deceiving or misleading. It links her answer to the serpent's earlier conduct in the narrative. The word does not, by itself, settle every question about the woman's agency, knowledge, or responsibility. Her answer ends with her own admission: “and I did eat.”

## 7. The Four Questions

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

How does the map preserve the distinction between the Author's knowledge and a creature's report? How should a Witness distinguish a question that summons an answer from a question that requests missing information? How do we register a person's explanation without adopting that explanation as the complete account of events?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The Source-to-Witness distinction keeps the original command, the people's actions, God's questions, and the people's answers separate. The Author's question does not make the respondent the source of truth. The people's statements are witnesses to their own perspective and admissions, but they do not replace the narrator's account of what occurred.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The passage records:
- the pair hearing the LORD God's voice and hiding;
- the LORD God calling Adam;
- Adam reporting hearing, fear, nakedness, and hiding;
- God asking who told Adam he was naked and whether he ate from the prohibited tree;
- Adam identifying the woman and admitting he ate;
- God asking the woman what she had done;
- the woman identifying the serpent's beguiling and admitting she ate.

Both human answers include “I did eat.” Both identify another participant, but neither admission is absent from the record.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must distinguish:
- an event from a participant's explanation of the event;
- a question from an expression of ignorance;
- an admission from an attribution of another person's role;
- the narrator's account from a speaker's selective emphasis;
- and what is explicitly stated from motives that are not stated.

A careful Witness does not erase the admission because another participant is mentioned. Nor does it claim to know the inner motive behind a person's wording when the text does not disclose it.

**How the four questions inform one another:** The Author's questions bring the human participants into an account of what happened. Their answers provide testimony, but the narrator's earlier record remains essential for evaluating those answers. The Witness must report both without confusing them.
