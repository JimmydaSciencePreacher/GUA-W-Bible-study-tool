## The blessing, dominion, blood, life, and the image of God

## 1. Read the passage — KJV

**Genesis 9:1**

> And God blessed Noah and his sons, and said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth.

**Genesis 9:2**

> And the fear of you and the dread of you shall be upon every beast of the earth, and upon every fowl of the air, upon all that moveth upon the earth, and upon all the fishes of the sea; into your hand are they delivered.

**Genesis 9:3**

> Every moving thing that liveth shall be meat for you; even as the green herb have I given you all things.

**Genesis 9:4**

> But flesh with the life thereof, which is the blood thereof, shall ye not eat.

**Genesis 9:5**

> And surely your blood of your lives will I require; at the hand of every beast will I require it, and at the hand of man; at the hand of every man's brother will I require the life of man.

**Genesis 9:6**

> Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed: for in the image of God made he man.

**Genesis 9:7**

> And you, be ye fruitful, and multiply; bring forth abundantly in the earth, and multiply therein.

## 2. Immediate observations

The passage has five movements:

1. **Blessing and multiplication (v. 1):** God blesses Noah and his sons and commands fruitfulness.
2. **Dominion and relationship (v. 2):** fear and dread of humanity are placed upon living creatures; they are delivered into human hands.
3. **Provision and limitation (vv. 3–4):** moving creatures are given as food, but flesh with its life/blood is prohibited.
4. **Accountability for life (vv. 5–6):** God requires an accounting for human blood and identifies the image of God as the reason for the special treatment of human life.
5. **Repeated multiplication (v. 7):** the command to be fruitful and multiply is repeated and intensified with *sharats*, teeming or abounding.

The passage grants provision and authority while placing limits on human conduct. Permission to use living creatures as food is not an unlimited permission to disregard life.

The repetition of the fruitfulness command at the beginning and end frames the instructions about animals, food, blood, and human accountability.

## 3. Hebrew investigation

### 3.1 “God blessed” — *barak* (בָּרַךְ)

Genesis 9:1 uses *barak*, “to bless.” God is the subject; Noah and his sons are the recipients.

The blessing is immediately accompanied by the command:
- *peru* — be fruitful;
- *revu* — multiply;
- *millu* — fill/replenish the earth.

The same fruitfulness formula appears in Genesis 1:28. Genesis 8:17 had already directed the living creatures toward fruitfulness and multiplication. Genesis 9:1 now addresses Noah and his sons.

**Disposition:** The blessing and command come from God. Noah's family receives the instruction; it does not originate it.

### 3.2 “Replenish the earth” — *male* (מָלֵא)

The verb *male* means to fill or be full. The KJV's “replenish” here expresses filling the earth. The English word should not be used to smuggle in a theory that the earth had previously been populated and then emptied. The Hebrew verb itself means fill.

The same root is used in Genesis 1:28 and in other contexts for filling or being full.

**Disposition:** The immediate sense is to fill the earth through the commanded multiplication. The word alone does not establish a pre-Adamic population or an earlier destruction.

### 3.3 “Fear” and “dread” — *mora* (מוֹרָא) and *chat* (חַת)

Verse 2 uses two terms for fear or dread. The text describes a changed relationship between humanity and other creatures: fear and dread of humans will be upon the animals, birds, and creatures moving on the earth and in the sea.

The verse then says they are delivered into humanity's hand. “Hand” is an idiom for power, control, or authority.

The passage does not give a zoological explanation for how every animal responds, nor does it say every individual animal will always flee from every human. It describes the human-animal relationship in broad covenantal/narrative terms.

**Disposition:** The text states human authority and an associated fear/dread relationship. It does not provide a universal behavioral law for every animal encounter.

### 3.4 “Into your hand are they delivered” — *nathan* (נָתַן)

The passive form of *nathan*, “give,” indicates that the creatures are given into human hands. The source of the grant is the divine speaker.

Compare Genesis 1:26–28, where humanity receives dominion over living creatures. Genesis 9:2 restates human authority after the Flood, now with the fear/dread language.

**Disposition:** Human authority is received, not self-originated. The passage does not identify humanity as the ultimate owner or author of animal life.

### 3.5 “Every moving thing that liveth” — *remes* (רֶמֶשׂ) and *chay* (חַי)

Verse 3 grants as food “every moving thing that liveth.” *Remes* refers to a moving or creeping creature; *chay* means living/alive.

The scope is broad in the wording. Verse 4 immediately supplies a restriction: flesh is not to be eaten with its life, identified with its blood.

The verse therefore contains both a grant and a boundary. It is not sound to quote verse 3 while ignoring verse 4.

**Disposition:** Animal food is permitted in the passage, but the permission is expressly qualified.

### 3.6 “The life thereof, which is the blood thereof” — *nephesh* (נֶפֶשׁ) and *dam* (דָּם)

The Hebrew of verse 4 is compact:

*basar benaphsho damo lo tokhelu* — approximately, “flesh with its life, its blood, you shall not eat.”

The noun *nephesh* can mean life, living being, self, person, or soul according to context. Here it is associated directly with *dam*, blood. The construction is commonly understood as apposition: “its life, that is, its blood” or “its lifeblood.”

The KJV expresses the relationship as “the life thereof, which is the blood thereof.”

This is not a statement that blood and *nephesh* are interchangeable in every biblical context, nor does it settle every theological question about the soul. It identifies blood as the life-bearing or life-associated element in this prohibition.

The same relationship is developed in Leviticus 17:11, 14, where blood is associated with the life of the flesh. Deuteronomy 12:23 also says that blood is the life and prohibits eating the life with the flesh.

**Disposition:** In this food prohibition, blood represents or is identified with the creature's life. Do not turn this contextual statement into a complete metaphysical definition of *nephesh*.

### 3.7 “Your blood of your lives will I require” — *darash* (דָּרַשׁ)

Verse 5 repeats the verb *darash*, meaning seek, require, demand, or call to account. God says He will require an accounting for human blood/life.

The expression “from the hand of” indicates the party from whom an accounting is required. The verse names every living creature and humanity; it then focuses on a human being's responsibility for the life of another human being, described as his “brother.”

The repeated *darash* emphasizes accountability. The exact way the animal clause is to be understood—whether as an assertion of animal accountability in the narrative or as a statement that God will require the life taken by an animal—is discussed by interpreters. The text does not provide a procedure for adjudicating animal cases.

**Disposition:** God claims the right to require an accounting for human life. The text does not give a complete judicial procedure in verse 5.

### 3.8 “Whoso sheddeth man's blood” — *shaphakh dam* (שָׁפַךְ דָּם)

The expression means to shed blood and is used for the taking of life, especially violent killing. Verse 6 states a reciprocal consequence: the one who sheds human blood will have his blood shed “by man.”

The phrase “by man” identifies human agency in the response. Interpretations differ on whether the wording directly establishes a human judicial authority or states a general principle of retributive justice. Later biblical law develops judicial procedures and restrictions, but those details should not simply be read back into this short statement.

The verse must not be reduced to private vengeance. Nor should it be treated as a complete criminal code on its own. The wider canonical treatment of murder, witnesses, courts, intent, and punishment must be considered before drawing legal conclusions.

**Disposition:** The verse establishes the gravity of shedding human blood and a humanly mediated response, grounded in the image of God. Its precise legal implementation requires broader textual investigation.

### 3.9 “In the image of God” — *tselem Elohim* (צֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים)

Verse 6 gives the reason: humanity was made in God's image.

This directly recalls Genesis 1:26–27. The image-of-God language appears before the Fall and is invoked here after the Flood. The passage does not say that the Flood erased or replaced humanity's image-bearing status.

This is a major textual observation: the value and accountability attached to human life are grounded in God's creative act, not in a human achievement, social rank, strength, usefulness, or self-assigned worth.

The verse applies the image rationale to human bloodshed. It does not explicitly provide a complete philosophical definition of the image of God—whether it is constituted by rationality, relationship, vocation, representation, or another account. Those questions require a wider canonical study.

**Disposition:** The image of God is explicitly the reason given for the special accountability attached to human bloodshed. The exact ontology of the image is not fully defined in these verses.

### 3.10 “Bring forth abundantly” — *sharats* (שָׁרַץ)

Verse 7 uses *sharats*, the same root encountered in Genesis 1:20–21 and Genesis 8:17. It conveys swarming, teeming, or abounding.

The verse repeats *parah* (be fruitful) and *rabah* (multiply), then adds the teeming/abounding language. This reinforces the opening command in verse 1.

**Disposition:** The passage closes by returning to the purpose of fruitfulness and multiplication. The Creation vocabulary continues across Genesis 1, 8, and 9.

## 4. Blood and life: scientific investigation

### 4.1 What biology can establish about blood

Modern physiology describes blood as a circulating tissue with several components and functions:
- plasma transports cells, nutrients, proteins, hormones, and waste;
- red blood cells transport oxygen and contribute to carbon-dioxide transport;
- white blood cells participate in immune defense;
- platelets and clotting proteins help limit blood loss after injury;
- circulation supports delivery and removal processes throughout the body.

These are established physiological functions. Blood loss can be life-threatening because it disrupts circulation, oxygen delivery, and the body's ability to maintain essential functions.

Sources from MedlinePlus, the American Society of Hematology, and Johns Hopkins Medicine describe these functions.

**Boundary:** Modern physiology explains why blood is essential to the functioning of many animals, including humans. It does not prove that the Hebrew word *nephesh* means “circulatory system,” nor does it settle the theological meaning of life or soul.

### 4.2 Does science show that blood is life?

Science can show that blood performs functions necessary for normal life in humans and many animals. It can describe oxygen transport, nutrient transport, immune defense, clotting, and waste removal.

But the biblical statement is not a modern biochemical definition. Genesis 9:4 uses the language of life (*nephesh*) and blood (*dam*) in a prohibition concerning eating flesh with its blood.

Therefore distinguish:
1. **Biblical claim:** the life of the flesh is identified with its blood in this textual and ritual context.
2. **Scientific observation:** blood is a vital transport and regulatory tissue in many organisms.
3. **Unresolved theological question:** the full relationship between *nephesh*, bodily life, personhood, and soul across Scripture.

Do not claim that modern hematology has scientifically proved a doctrine of the soul. Do not claim that the Hebrew verse is contradicted because biology describes life at cellular and organismal levels. These are different kinds of claims and require careful comparison.

### 4.3 Blood is not the only condition of life

Biology does not define life as “blood alone.” Some living organisms do not have blood in the vertebrate sense; some use hemolymph or other transport systems, and many organisms have no circulatory system comparable to humans.

Genesis 9:4 is addressed within a human dietary and animal-life context. It should not be converted into a universal biological claim that every living organism has blood.

**Disposition:** Blood is a major life-supporting tissue in humans and many animals. It is not a universal biological definition of life.

## 5. Dominion, provision, and restraint

Genesis 9:2–4 gives a connected sequence:
- living creatures are delivered into human hands;
- moving creatures are given as food;
- flesh with its life/blood is prohibited.

The grant of dominion is therefore accompanied by a restriction. The human recipient is not given permission to treat life as meaningless or to consume the lifeblood as though it were ordinary food.

Genesis 1:26–30 and Genesis 9:2–4 should be read together. Genesis 1 describes the original human vocation and plant-food provision; Genesis 9 records a post-Flood food permission that includes moving creatures, with the blood restriction.

The text does not say that animal life and human life have identical status. Verses 5–6 distinguish human bloodshed by grounding its gravity in the image of God. Nor does the passage say that animal life is worthless: verse 4 places a limit on how animal flesh is consumed.

**Finding:** Authority, provision, and restraint coexist. Dominion is a delegated responsibility under the Author, not autonomous ownership.

## 6. Human life and the image of God

Genesis 9:5–6 places a special accountability on the taking of human life. The stated reason is not human strength, intelligence, social usefulness, or membership in a particular nation. It is that God made humanity in His image.

Compare:
- Genesis 1:26–27 — humanity created in God's image.
- Genesis 5:1–3 — image/likeness language in the genealogy after the Fall.
- Genesis 9:6 — the image rationale grounds the accountability for human bloodshed.
- James 3:9 — human beings are described as made after God's likeness, in a warning against cursing people.

The image language persists after Genesis 3 and after the Flood. This passage therefore does not present the image as erased by human sin or by the Flood.

**Important boundary:** Genesis 9:6 establishes a reason for the gravity of human bloodshed. It does not answer every question about the image's precise nature or every modern bioethical application without further study.

## 12. Findings and open questions

### Established by the text
- God blesses Noah and his sons.
- They are commanded to be fruitful, multiply, and fill the earth.
- Fear and dread of humanity are placed upon living creatures.
- Living creatures are delivered into human hands.
- Moving creatures are given as food.
- Flesh with its life/blood is not to be eaten.
- God requires an accounting for human blood.
- Verse 6 grounds the gravity of human bloodshed in humanity's creation in God's image.
- The fruitfulness command is repeated in verse 7.

### Supported interpretations
- Human authority is received from God, not self-originated.
- Dominion is bounded; permission does not mean unrestricted autonomy.
- Blood is identified with the life of the flesh in the context of the dietary prohibition.
- Blood's physiological importance is scientifically observable, but that does not reduce *nephesh* to a biological mechanism.
- Human life receives special accountability in the passage because humanity bears God's image.
- The image-of-God language persists after the Fall and Flood in the biblical narrative.

### Unresolved questions
1. What is the full scope of “every moving thing” in verse 3?
2. How does the post-Flood food permission relate to the plant-food provision of Genesis 1:29–30?
3. What is the exact contextual force of *nephesh* in Genesis 9:4–5?
4. How should the phrase “which is the blood thereof” be understood grammatically and theologically?
5. How does the animal clause in verse 5 function?
6. Does verse 6 directly establish a judicial institution, state a general principle, or both?
7. How do later biblical laws concerning witnesses, intent, manslaughter, and murder develop the principle in verse 6?
8. What is the precise nature of the image of God?
9. How does image-bearing relate to human dignity, responsibility, and accountability?
10. How should the biblical concept of *nephesh* be compared with modern biological descriptions of life without confusing categories?
11. How does the blood restriction relate to later sacrificial law and the wider canonical treatment of blood?
12. What does the repeated use of *sharats* contribute to the Creation continuity across Genesis 1, 8, and 9?

### What must not be claimed
- That *nephesh* always means an immaterial soul.
- That blood is the complete scientific definition of life.
- That modern physiology proves or disproves the biblical doctrine of the soul.
- That every living organism has blood in the vertebrate sense.
- That dominion grants unrestricted exploitation.
- That Genesis 9:6 alone supplies a complete modern criminal code or settles every question of capital punishment.
- That the image of God is fully defined in these seven verses.
- That human worth is based on intelligence, strength, utility, social status, or scientific measurement.
- That the post-Flood food permission cancels the restriction in verse 4.

## 13. Continuity record

- **Current conclusion:** Genesis 9:1–7 records God's blessing and multiplication command, humanity's delegated authority over living creatures, permission to eat moving creatures with a restriction against eating flesh with its blood, divine accountability for human blood, and the image-of-God rationale for the gravity of human bloodshed.
- **Important Hebrew distinctions:** *Nephesh* has a contextual range including life, living being, self, and person; in 9:4 it is closely associated with *dam* (blood). *Darash* means seek, require, or demand an accounting. *Tselem Elohim* recalls the image language of Genesis 1:26–27. *Sharats* in 9:7 recalls Genesis 1:20–21 and 8:17.
- **Important scientific boundary:** Blood performs vital transport, immune, regulatory, and clotting functions in humans and many animals. Biology does not define the full theological meaning of *nephesh*, and not all living organisms possess blood in the vertebrate sense.
- **Unresolved questions carried forward:** The scope of animal-food permission; *nephesh* and blood; the animal clause in verse 5; the legal force of verse 6; the ontology of the image of God; and the relation to later blood and sacrificial laws.
