## 1. Passage text — KJV

> And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.

## 2. Textual observations

The verse presents three linked elements:

1. **Speaker:** “And God said”
2. **Content of the speech:** “Let there be light”
3. **Recorded result:** “and there was light”

The verse does not merely report light as an isolated occurrence. It explicitly relates God's speech to the result that follows.

The sentence does not name an intermediary agent, a competing will, resistance, or a delay. Those details are not part of the account given in this verse.

## 3. Hebrew language and grammar

The Masoretic Hebrew text:

> וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר וַיְהִי־אוֹר

Transliteration:

> Wayyōmer ʾĕlōhīm, yəhî ʾôr, wayəhî-ʾôr.

| Hebrew | Transliteration | KJV |
|---|---|---|
| וַיֹּאמֶר | wayyōmer | And said |
| אֱלֹהִים | ʾĕlōhīm | God |
| יְהִי | yəhî | Let there be |
| אוֹר | ʾôr | Light |
| וַיְהִי | wayəhî | And there was |
| אוֹר | ʾôr | Light |

### The repeated verb

Both **yəhî** and **wayəhî** derive from the Hebrew root **היה (hayah)**, whose range includes being, becoming, or happening.

- **יְהִי (yəhî):** a short jussive form, third-person masculine singular, commonly rendered “Let there be.” In this context it expresses the divine directive.
- **וַיְהִי (wayəhî):** a narrative wayyiqtol form, reporting what happened: “and there was.”

The same root appears in both clauses, while the grammatical form and narrative function differ. The NET Bible notes that the pairing expresses the divine declaration and its fulfillment. The precise theological force is drawn from the grammar in context, not from a claim that the verb itself is a technical scientific operator.

### Important distinction

“Let there be” is an English rendering of a Hebrew jussive. It is not simply an ordinary human request. The speaker and context matter. In this narrative, God is the Creator and the verse immediately records the result.

**Observation:** The form of the speech and the form of the result are deliberately related.

## 4. The speech-to-result sequence

The verse gives this sequence:

**God said → “Let there be light” → “and there was light.”**

This is the first explicit instance in the account where the divine speech is immediately followed by the corresponding result.

It is reasonable to describe this as a **speech-to-realization relationship** in the narrative.

It would go beyond the verse to claim that we have thereby identified a physical mechanism, a field equation, a vibration, a frequency, or a modern information-processing operation. None of those is named here.

### A structural observation for GUA-W

The verse provides a clear textual distinction between:
- the originating speaker;
- the expressed declaration;
- the result that comes to be.

This is consistent with investigating the relationship **Author → Word → Creation**. It does not authorize us to treat those labels as a replacement for the verse or as proof that every later GUA-W architectural category is already fully specified here.

## 5. The word “light” — אור (ʾôr)

The Hebrew noun **ʾôr** means light. Its meaning in any particular passage must be determined by context.

In Genesis 1:3, the immediate context is the darkness described in Genesis 1:2. The light/darkness contrast becomes explicit in Genesis 1:4, where God sees that the light is good and separates the light from the darkness. Genesis 1:5 then records the naming of the light as “Day” and the darkness as “Night.”

The text has not yet identified the physical source, composition, wavelength, or mechanism of this light.

### A question already present in the sequence

The sun, moon, and stars are described later, in Genesis 1:14–19. Therefore, the narrative places the appearance of light in verse 3 before its account of the luminaries on day four.

This is a real textual feature and must not be ignored.

But the text does **not** say in verse 3:
- that the sun already existed;
- that the light was sunlight;
- that the light had no source;
- that the light was electromagnetic radiation in the modern technical sense;
- or what physical process produced it.

The identity and physical source of the light remain open questions for the continuing study.

## 6. Scripture cross-references

### Psalm 33:6, 9

Psalm 33:6 connects the heavens with the word of the LORD; verse 9 says that He spoke and it was done, and commanded and it stood fast.

**Connection:** This is a direct canonical witness to the relationship between divine speech and created result. It strengthens the importance of the speech/result pattern without supplying a physical mechanism.

### John 1:1–5

John begins with the Word, identifies the Word's relation to God, says all things were made by Him, and then speaks of life and light shining in darkness.

**Connection:** John places creation, the Word, life, and light in a larger theological frame. John 1 should be read in its own context; its use of “light” includes theological meaning and should not automatically be reduced to physical illumination.

### 2 Corinthians 4:6

Paul refers to the God who commanded light to shine out of darkness and applies that language to the shining of God's knowledge in the heart.

**Connection:** Paul explicitly draws on creation-language to describe divine illumination. This is a canonical theological application, not a claim that conversion and visible light are the same physical event.

### Isaiah 45:7

The LORD declares that He forms light and creates darkness.

**Connection:** This passage attributes both light and darkness to God's sovereign activity. Its immediate context concerns the LORD's sovereignty and should not be detached from that setting.

### Job 38:19

God asks Job where the way to the dwelling of light is.

**Connection:** Light is treated as part of the created order whose way and dwelling are beyond Job's mastery. This is relevant to the limits of the creature's knowledge, not a scientific definition.

### Other references for continued comparison

## 7. Creation and scientific investigation

The study is open to examining light as a feature of Creation. That requires distinguishing the biblical word from the categories and measurements used in modern physics.

### What modern science investigates

In physics and astronomy, light is commonly discussed in terms of electromagnetic radiation. Photons, wavelength, frequency, energy, propagation, emission, absorption, and scattering are among the relevant concepts. Visible light is only part of the electromagnetic spectrum; radiation outside the visible range includes infrared, microwaves, radio waves, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays.

The U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Science describes the cosmic microwave background (CMB) as radiation released when the early universe became transparent, approximately 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Before that transition, photons repeatedly interacted with charged particles, so radiation could not travel freely over long distances. As the universe expanded, the radiation's wavelengths stretched; what is now observed as the CMB lies in the microwave region.

This provides a concrete example of how scientists investigate light/radiation in the physical universe: through measurable signals, wavelengths, polarization, and models tested against observations.

### What this does and does not establish for Genesis 1:3

**It establishes, within the scientific model and observations described by the source:**
- Radiation is a measurable feature of the physical universe.
- The early universe's transparency and the free propagation of radiation are subjects of observational cosmology.
- The word “light” in everyday speech can cover phenomena that modern physics distinguishes by wavelength and energy.

**It does not establish:**
- That the CMB is the light of Genesis 1:3.
- That the Big Bang timeline is the chronology intended by Genesis 1.
- That Genesis 1:3 is describing recombination, photons, or any particular cosmological event.
- That modern physics has identified the mechanism described by the biblical sentence.

The CMB is a scientific observation interpreted within a cosmological model. It is not a direct identification of the biblical “light.” Equating them would be an unsupported inference.

### A useful investigation question

Since Genesis places light before the luminaries in verses 14–19, what does the text mean by light in verse 3, and what can physical observation responsibly contribute to exploring that question?

Potential lines of investigation include:
1. The distinction between light as a physical phenomenon and the luminaries named later in the chapter.
2. The difference between the existence of radiation and the visibility of a particular source from a particular location.
3. How the text itself distinguishes light, darkness, day, night, and the later heavenly lights.
4. What astronomy can measure about radiation and luminous sources, without assuming that the scientific timeline is identical to the literary sequence.

These are research questions, not conclusions.

## 11. Findings and unresolved questions

### Established from the text
- God speaks.
- The content of the speech is “Let there be light.”
- The narrative records “and there was light.”
- The same Hebrew root, *hayah*, occurs in the directive and the result forms.
- The light appears in the narrative before the luminaries are described in Genesis 1:14–19.

### Supported observations
- The grammar creates a close correspondence between divine directive and narrative fulfillment.
- Psalm 33:6, 9 and John 1:1–5 provide important canonical connections between the Word and Creation.
- 2 Corinthians 4:6 explicitly applies creation-light language to divine illumination.
- Modern science investigates radiation using measurable physical properties, but the scientific category cannot simply be substituted for the Hebrew word *ʾôr*.

### Unresolved questions
1. What is the referent of “light” in Genesis 1:3?
2. How does this light relate to the separation of light and darkness in verse 4 and the naming of day and night in verse 5?
3. How does the light in verses 3–5 relate to the luminaries appointed in verses 14–19?
4. Does the text distinguish the existence of light from the later appointment of the luminaries? If so, how?
5. What can astronomy and physics contribute to these questions without imposing their chronology or technical vocabulary onto the text?
6. How should the relationship between divine speech and realized result be mapped in GUA-W without inventing a new architectural law?

### What must not be claimed yet
- That the light of Genesis 1:3 is the Sun.
- That it is the CMB or a specific early-universe event.
- That it is a photon, field, frequency, or vibration in the technical modern sense.
- That science has demonstrated the event described by Genesis 1:3.
- That every later GUA-W architectural layer is explicitly contained in this verse.
