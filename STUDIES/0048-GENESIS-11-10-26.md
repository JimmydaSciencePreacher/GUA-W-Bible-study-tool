## 2. Immediate context and observations

Genesis 10 records the descendants of Noah's sons and the differentiation of peoples. Genesis 11:1–9 narrates Babel and dispersion. Genesis 11:10–26 returns to Shem's line and narrows the genealogy toward Terah and his sons. Genesis 11:27 begins the next *toledot* section, “Now these are the generations of Terah.”

The passage follows a repeated pattern:

1. A named ancestor reaches a stated age.
2. He begets the next named descendant.
3. The text records the years he lives after that event.
4. The text records that he has sons and daughters.

The pattern is not identical to Genesis 5. Genesis 5 repeatedly concludes with a total lifespan and “and he died.” Genesis 11:10–26 does not repeat that death formula for each person. It emphasizes the age at the named begetting event, the remaining years, and the continuation of the family.

The sequence in the KJV is:

**Shem → Arphaxad → Salah → Eber → Peleg → Reu → Serug → Nahor → Terah → Abram, Nahor, and Haran.**

The genealogy does not give a complete list of every child or every descendant. It selects a line and records additional sons and daughters without naming them.

### 3.1 A caution about “begat”

The KJV repeatedly uses “begat.” The underlying genealogical formula identifies a begetting relationship, but the presence of a name in a genealogy does not, by itself, prove that every list is a complete modern-style register of immediate biological parent-child links.

Some English translations use “became the father of” or “fathered.” In genealogical contexts, “father” can also refer to an ancestor, depending on the passage and the structure of the list. This possibility must be tested case by case, not assumed whenever a chronological difficulty appears.

The text gives a selected lineage. It does not say, “these are every generation without omission.”

## 4. The *toledot* structure

Genesis 11:10 begins:

> These are the generations of Shem

The Hebrew term is *toledot* (תּוֹלְדוֹת), referring to generations, descendants, or the account associated with what proceeds from a person.

This is a recurring structural expression in Genesis:
- Genesis 2:4 — the generations/account of the heavens and the earth.
- Genesis 5:1 — the book of the generations of Adam.
- Genesis 6:9 — the generations of Noah.
- Genesis 10:1 — the generations of Noah's sons.
- Genesis 11:10 — the generations of Shem.
- Genesis 11:27 — the generations of Terah.

The phrase does more than introduce a list of names. It marks a new unit in the book's organization.

Bill T. Arnold observes that Genesis 11:10–26 is framed by the Shem heading and the Terah heading in verse 27. It faces backward toward the primeval history and forward toward the ancestral narratives. This is a literary observation, not a claim that the genealogy itself changes the historical events it records.

## 5. The line from Shem to Abram

### 5.1 Shem

Shem is one of Noah's three sons. Genesis 10:21 connects Shem with the children of Eber. Genesis 11:10 now follows the selected line through Shem.

The passage says Shem was one hundred years old when he begat Arphaxad, two years after the Flood, and lived another five hundred years after that.

### 5.2 Arphaxad and Salah

Arphaxad is the next named descendant. The KJV/MT sequence proceeds directly from Arphaxad to Salah.

A significant textual issue appears here: the Greek Septuagint tradition includes an additional name, Cainan, between Arphaxad and Salah. Luke 3:36 also includes Cainan in its genealogy. This variant is addressed separately below; it must not be silently inserted into the KJV text.

### 5.3 Eber

Eber is named as Salah's son. Genesis 10:21 calls Shem the father of all the children of Eber. Eber is therefore already a significant name in the Table of Nations before Genesis 11 follows the selected line toward Abram.

The association between Eber and the later term “Hebrew” is commonly discussed. The biblical text establishes Eber as an ancestor in the genealogy; the precise linguistic and historical relationship between the name Eber and the designation Hebrew should not be reduced to an unsupported wordplay.

### 5.4 Peleg

Peleg was introduced in Genesis 10:25, where the text says that in his days the earth was divided. Genesis 11:16–17 places Peleg in the line from Eber and records his age when he begat Reu.

The genealogy does not explain the division in this passage. The question carried from Genesis 10 remains open.

### 5.5 Reu, Serug, and Nahor

These names continue the selected line. The text records each named begetting age and subsequent years, but gives no separate narrative about their actions, occupations, locations, or beliefs.

Do not fill those silences with later traditions or speculative biographies.

### 5.6 Terah and his sons

Verse 26 names Abram, Nahor, and Haran. This is the first point in the sequence where three sons are named together rather than one selected descendant.

The order in which names are listed does not necessarily establish birth order. Genesis 11:27–32 supplies further family information: Haran is Lot's father and dies before Terah; Abram marries Sarai; Nahor marries Milcah; and Sarai is barren. These details will be examined directly in the next study.

The immediate narrative focus moves from the line of Shem to the household of Terah, then to Abram.

## 6. A major textual question: Masoretic Text, Samaritan Pentateuch, and Septuagint

The KJV follows the Hebrew Masoretic textual tradition for this passage. Other ancient textual traditions preserve different readings, especially in the numbers and in the presence or absence of Cainan.

These are not merely translation-style differences. They are textual variants in the underlying witnesses.

### 6.1 Cainan between Arphaxad and Salah

- **KJV / Masoretic Text:** Arphaxad → Salah → Eber.
- **Septuagint reading in Genesis 11:** Arphaxad → Cainan → Salah → Eber.
- **Luke 3:36:** includes Cainan in the genealogy.

The NET textual note records the Septuagint's additional Cainan and observes that this reading also appears behind Luke 3:35–36. Andrew Steinmann argues that Cainan is a later addition in the Greek Genesis tradition, possibly associated with harmonization to Luke; other text-critical discussions have proposed an insertion from the Genesis 5 genealogy or other explanations.

**Disposition:** The existence of the variant is established. Its origin and direction of transmission remain a textual-critical question. Do not claim that the KJV contains Cainan in Genesis 11:12; it does not.

### 6.2 Differences in the ages

The MT, SP, and LXX differ in several ages. A simplified comparison from Steinmann's published table is:

| Ancestor | MT age at begetting | SP | LXX |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Shem | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| Arphaxad | 35 | 135 | 135 |
| Salah | 30 | 130 | 130 |
| Eber | 34 | 134 | 134 |
| Peleg | 30 | 130 | 130 |
| Reu | 32 | 132 | 132 |
| Serug | 30 | 130 | 130 |
| Nahor | 29 | 79 | 79 |
| Terah | 70 | 70 | 70 |

The remaining-years figures also differ. For example, the table gives Arphaxad as 403 years in MT, 303 in SP, and 430 in LXX; Eber as 430 in MT, 270 in SP, and 370 in LXX; and Nahor as 119 in MT, 69 in SP, and 129 in LXX.

This table is not a declaration that one tradition is automatically correct. It documents the differences that must be evaluated.

### 6.3 Why the numbers matter

The begetting ages are often added to construct a chronology from the Flood toward Abram. That calculation depends on assumptions:
- That each named descendant is the immediate biological son of the preceding name.
- That no generations are omitted.
- That the numbers in the chosen textual tradition are preserved accurately.
- That the age figure marks the birth of the specific named descendant, rather than the beginning of a sequence of sons or a genealogical relation.

Those assumptions must be examined rather than hidden inside the arithmetic.

Steinmann's 2021 study compares 860 variants among MT, SP, and LXX across Genesis and examines the genealogies in Genesis 5 and 11 as a case study. He argues that SP and LXX often show systematic harmonizing changes, while MT is more conservative overall, though he also identifies a possible numerical error in the MT figure for Eber's remaining years. His conclusions are a scholarly argument, not a substitute for examining the witnesses or a universally agreed verdict.

### 6.4 Textual witness versus translation

The KJV is an English translation based on a Hebrew textual tradition. The Septuagint is an ancient Greek translation with its own textual history. The Samaritan Pentateuch is a Hebrew textual tradition.

The task is not to treat one language as automatically authoritative because it is older or newer. The task is to compare readings, manuscript histories, scribal tendencies, context, and the evidence for each variant.

**GUA-W boundary:** A Witness must report what each textual witness actually contains. It must not silently combine variants into a newly constructed text and then call that composite the KJV.

## 7. The Terah–Abram chronology question

Genesis 11:26 says Terah lived seventy years and begat Abram, Nahor, and Haran. Genesis 11:32 later says Terah lived 205 years and died. Genesis 12:4 says Abram was seventy-five years old when he departed from Haran. Acts 7:4 says Abram departed from the land of the Chaldeans after his father died.

These statements have generated different chronological readings.

### 7.1 A common harmonizing calculation

If Abram was born when Terah was 70, then at Abram's departure at age 75, Terah would be 145. If Terah died at 205, he would live another 60 years after Abram's departure.

This reading preserves the MT/KJV figures of 70, 75, and 205, but it does not read Acts 7:4 as placing Abram's departure from Haran after Terah's death.

### 7.2 A different birth-order reading

Genesis 11:26 does not explicitly say that Abram was Terah's firstborn or that all three sons were born in the same year. The list may name sons in an order of narrative importance rather than birth order.

On this reading, Terah began fathering the named sons at age 70, but Abram may have been born later. If Abram was born when Terah was 130, then Terah would be 205 when Abram was 75, allowing Acts 7:4 and Genesis 12:4 to be read together chronologically.

This is an inference that depends on the sequence and the interpretation of the genealogy. It is not explicitly stated in Genesis 11:26.

### 7.3 The Samaritan Pentateuch reading

The SP gives Terah's age at death as 145 rather than 205 in Genesis 11:32. This aligns Terah's death with Abram's departure at age 75 if Abram was born when Terah was 70.

Some interpreters regard this as a harmonizing adjustment; others explore how the variant relates to the chronology in Acts. The textual variant must be acknowledged rather than hidden.

### 7.4 Scholarly disagreement

Bill T. Arnold reads Genesis 11:26 as Terah beginning to father his sons at seventy and treats the later death notice as a literary conclusion to the genealogy, not necessarily the next event chronologically. In his reading, Abram leaves while Terah is still alive, and Terah dies later.

Andrew Steinmann argues that the age of seventy need not mark Abram's own birth and that the 205-year figure makes it unlikely that Abram was born when Terah was seventy.

Other interpreters attempt to harmonize Genesis and Acts differently.

**Current disposition: Unresolved.** Do not declare one chronology settled without evaluating Genesis 11:26, 11:32, 12:4, Acts 7:2–4, and the textual witnesses together. The next passage will provide the immediate family context, but the question may require a separate chronology study.

## 8. Creation and scientific investigation

### Question being investigated

Can modern population science independently reconstruct the exact family sequence or ages recorded in this genealogy?

### Relevant disciplines

- Population genetics.
- Historical demography.
- Human reproductive biology.
- Archaeogenetics.
- Textual criticism (a distinct discipline concerned with reconstructing and comparing textual witnesses, not a biological science).

### What evidence establishes

Modern population genetics can investigate patterns of shared ancestry, genetic variation, population bottlenecks, migration, and admixture in living and ancient populations when suitable samples exist.

Historical demography can model population change when birth rates, mortality, migration, and time intervals are available.

### What evidence does not establish

The Genesis genealogy does not provide:
- Complete population counts.
- A complete register of every birth.
- A verified archaeological identification for each named person.
- Genetic samples linked to the named individuals.
- A scientific mechanism for the extraordinary lifespans recorded in the passage.

Modern science therefore cannot independently verify the specific ages or identify the genetic descendants of each named patriarch from this text alone.

Nor should current human life-expectancy data be used to rewrite the KJV numbers. The scientific question and the textual question are different.

**Relationship to Scripture:** No direct scientific mechanism is established by this passage. Scientific coherence requires restraint: do not invent demographic or genetic evidence where the necessary data do not exist.

## 14. Findings and open questions

### Established by the KJV text
1. The genealogy is introduced as the generations of Shem.
2. Shem is one hundred years old when he begets Arphaxad, two years after the Flood.
3. The named line proceeds through Arphaxad, Salah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, Nahor, and Terah.
4. The text repeatedly records years lived after the named begetting event.
5. The text repeatedly records that the men had sons and daughters.
6. Terah's three named sons are Abram, Nahor, and Haran.
7. The passage directs the narrative toward Terah's family and Abram.

### Supported interpretations
- The genealogy is a selected line, not a complete list of every descendant.
- Its repeated formula links it structurally with Genesis 5.
- It bridges the primeval history and the ancestral narratives.
- The line through Shem is selected from the wider Table of Nations.
- The listing of Terah's sons may be ordered by narrative significance rather than birth order.

### Unresolved questions
1. Does the genealogy contain omitted generations?
2. What is the exact relationship between Eber and the later term “Hebrew”?
3. What happened during Peleg's days when the earth was divided?
4. How should the Cainan variant in the LXX and Luke 3:36 be evaluated?
5. What explains the numerical differences among MT, SP, and LXX?
6. Does Genesis 11:26 identify Abram as the firstborn, or merely list the sons in a selected order?
7. How should Genesis 11:26, 11:32, 12:4, and Acts 7:2–4 be read together?
8. Does the genealogy provide a continuous chronology or a selected genealogical sequence?
9. What does the reduction in recorded lifespan figures mean within the text, and what remains beyond scientific reconstruction?

### What must not be claimed
- That all textual traditions contain identical names and ages.
- That the LXX Cainan reading is either original or secondary without presenting the argument and evidence.
- That the KJV includes Cainan in Genesis 11:12.
- That the name order in verse 26 proves Abram was Terah's firstborn.
- That every genealogical link necessarily represents an immediate father-to-son connection.
- That arithmetic alone proves a complete chronology.
- That modern population genetics identifies the named patriarchs.
- That modern human life expectancy can independently confirm or disprove the ages in the biblical text.

## 15. Continuity record

- **Current conclusion:** Genesis 11:10–26 follows a selected line from Shem through Terah and names Abram, Nahor, and Haran. The passage records ages, begetting events, and the continuation of families. It bridges the primeval history and the ancestral narratives.
- **Important Hebrew term:** *Toledot* = generations, descendants, or the account associated with a person/family.
- **Important textual distinction:** KJV/MT moves from Arphaxad to Salah; the LXX tradition includes Cainan, a reading also found in Luke 3:36. Numerical variants among MT, SP, and LXX must be preserved and evaluated, not silently combined.
- **Important chronology distinction:** Genesis 11:26 does not explicitly establish that Abram was Terah's firstborn. The relationship among Terah's age, his death, Abram's age at departure, and Acts 7:4 remains open.
- **Unresolved questions carried forward:** Genealogical compression; Cainan; MT/SP/LXX numerical variants; Terah/Abram chronology; Peleg's division; Eber/Hebrew relationship.
