# Study 0056 — Genesis 17:1–14 (KJV)
## El Shaddai, Abraham's new name, the everlasting covenant, and its sign

- **Primary passage:** Genesis 17:1–14 (KJV)
- **Previous study:** [Study 0055 — Genesis 16:1–16](0055-GENESIS-16-1-16.md)
- **Next passage:** Genesis 17:15–27 (KJV)
- **Central question:** What changes when God establishes the covenant sign, and what does not change about the identity of the Author and the recipient?

## 1. Immediate context

Genesis 15 records God's promise of an heir from Abram's own body, Abram's belief, and the covenant concerning the land. Genesis 16 records Sarai's proposal involving Hagar, Abram's response, Hagar's affliction, the birth of Ishmael, and God's attention to Hagar. Genesis 17 resumes with God appearing to Abram when he is ninety-nine years old. Ishmael is already alive; Genesis 16:16 gives Abram's age as eighty-six at Ishmael's birth.

Genesis 17:1–14 contains God's self-identification as El Shaddai, a command to walk before Him and be *tamim*, a covenant declaration, the renaming of Abram as Abraham, promises concerning nations, kings, seed, and land, and the requirement of circumcision as a covenant sign.

The study is limited to verses 1–14. Verses 15–27 must be read next to examine Sarah's renaming, Isaac's promised birth, Ishmael's blessing, and the actual circumcision of the household.

## 3. Verse 1 — El Shaddai, walking before God, and *tamim*

God appears to Abram when he is ninety-nine years old and says, “I am the Almighty God; walk before me, and be thou perfect.”

The divine name/title in Hebrew is *El Shaddai* (אֵל שַׁדַּי), traditionally rendered “God Almighty.” The precise historical etymology of *Shaddai* is debated; the translation “Almighty” is traditional, but the word's derivation should not be presented as settled merely because the English rendering is familiar.

The command is *hithallekh lefanai* (“walk before me”), followed by *wehyeh tamim* (“and be thou tamim”). *Tamim* can mean whole, complete, sound, or blameless, with the best English rendering depending on context. “Perfect” in the KJV should not automatically be read through the modern English sense of absolute flawlessness. The wording connects integrity/completeness with walking before God.

Compare:
- Genesis 6:9, where Noah is described as just and *tamim* in his generations.
- Deuteronomy 18:13, where Israel is commanded to be *tamim* with the LORD.
- Job 1:1, where Job is described as perfect and upright.

The passage gives the command; it does not provide a philosophical definition of perfection. The context supports integrity and wholeness in one's walk before God, but further lexical and canonical study is appropriate.

**Order matters:** God identifies Himself before commanding Abram. The authority behind the command belongs to the One speaking, not to Abram's independent status.

## 4. Verses 2–5 — Covenant declaration and Abraham's renaming

God says, “I will make my covenant between me and thee, and will multiply thee exceedingly.” The initiative in the declaration is God's. Abram falls on his face and receives what God says.

In verse 4, God declares that Abram will be a father of many nations. In verse 5, God changes his name from Abram to Abraham and explains the change: “for a father of many nations have I made thee.”

The Hebrew explanation uses a wordplay with *'av hamon goyim* (“father of a multitude of nations”). The text's own explanation is clear as narrative meaning. The precise historical etymology of the name Abraham is debated; scholars distinguish the explanation given in Genesis 17:5 from the name's earlier linguistic formation. Do not present the popular etymology as a settled historical derivation.

The renaming functions within the narrative as a reminder of God's declared purpose. Abraham does not originate the promise by renaming himself. God declares the name and its stated significance.

The grammar of verse 5 includes a perfect form in a context of divine intention and promise. The NET notes discuss its rhetorical force. The study need not resolve every aspect of Hebrew verbal aspect to observe that the verse is a divine declaration of what God intends to bring about.

## 5. Verses 6–8 — Fruitfulness, nations, kings, seed, land, and God

The promises include:
- Abraham will be exceedingly fruitful.
- Nations will come from him.
- Kings will come from him.
- God will establish His covenant with Abraham and his seed after him.
- God will be God to Abraham and his seed.
- The land of Canaan, where Abraham is a sojourner, is promised as an everlasting possession.

These are related but distinguishable elements. Descendants, nations, kings, covenant relationship, and land should not be collapsed into a single undifferentiated promise.

Verse 7 uses the Hebrew verb *qum* in a causative form in the expression often translated “to establish” or “confirm” the covenant. The NET notes describe this as carrying out or giving effect to a covenant or oath. Genesis 15:18 had already narrated God making/cutting a covenant with Abram. Genesis 17 supplies further covenant declarations and obligations. The relationship between these passages should be examined canonically rather than assuming that one cancels or replaces the other.

Verse 8 calls Canaan the land of Abraham's sojournings. The promise is spoken while Abraham is still a resident alien rather than an established possessor of the whole land. The contrast between present residence and promised possession is part of the narrative.

## 6. Verses 9–14 — Covenant obligation and the physical sign

Verse 9 turns to Abraham's responsibility: “Thou shalt keep my covenant, therefore, thou, and thy seed after thee in their generations.”

Verse 10 specifies the obligation: every male among them is to be circumcised. Verse 11 calls circumcision a “token” of the covenant. The Hebrew word is *'ot* (אוֹת), meaning sign or token. Verse 13 describes the covenant as being in their flesh and calls it everlasting. Verse 14 states the consequence for the uncircumcised male: he has broken the covenant and is to be cut off from his people.

The passage therefore distinguishes:
1. God's covenant declaration and promises.
2. The obligation placed upon Abraham and his male household.
3. The physical sign specified by God.
4. The stated consequence of not observing the sign.

Do not reduce the sign to a mere human badge or assume that the physical act independently originates the covenant. The text identifies it as the sign of God's covenant and simultaneously makes its observance a commanded obligation. The sign is not presented as optional.

The passage includes Abraham's seed, household-born males, and males purchased with money from a stranger. It is not limited to a biological father and his infant sons. The exact social and legal status of every person included in the household is not fully explained in these verses.

### Eight days

Verse 12 specifies that a male child is to be circumcised on the eighth day. The timing is part of the command. Do not replace it with a generic statement that circumcision could happen at any age. Genesis 17 itself also includes adult household members; verses 23–27, outside the present study unit, will record Abraham's obedience and Ishmael's circumcision at thirteen.

### Ancient Near Eastern context

Circumcision was known among several peoples in the ancient Near East; it was not an entirely unknown physical practice. Historical discussions describe differing contexts and timings among neighboring cultures. Genesis 17 gives the practice a particular covenantal function and specifies its timing for male infants on the eighth day. Scholarly accounts differ on the origins, distribution, and social functions of circumcision in antiquity; those debates should not be compressed into a single certain origin story.

The fact that a physical practice existed in surrounding cultures does not establish that those cultures assigned it the same covenant meaning as Genesis 17. The meaning assigned in this passage comes from the biblical text.

## 7. Ishmael and the sequence from Genesis 16

Ishmael has already been born before Genesis 17 begins. Genesis 16:16 records Abram's age as eighty-six at Ishmael's birth; Genesis 17:1 gives his age as ninety-nine. The passage therefore takes place after a substantial interval.

Genesis 17:1–14 does not yet state the full distinction between Ishmael and Isaac. That distinction appears in verses 15–21. Ishmael is Abraham's son and will be included in the circumcision of the household, as verses 23–27 later record. Genesis 17:20 promises that Ishmael will be blessed, fruitful, multiplied, and become a great nation. Genesis 17:19 and 21 identify Isaac as the son through whom God will establish the covenant.

Therefore distinguish:
- Being Abraham's son.
- Receiving circumcision as a household member.
- Receiving God's promise of blessing.
- Being the specified line through whom this covenant is established.

These are related but not identical categories. Do not infer that blessing and covenant-line status mean the same thing.

## 8. Creation and historical investigation — circumcision as embodied sign

### Question investigated

What can historical and biological observation say about circumcision as a physical practice, and what does that evidence not determine about its covenant meaning?

### Historical evidence

Circumcision is documented among multiple ancient Near Eastern peoples. The practice's origin and social functions are debated. Some historical reconstructions associate it with puberty, marriage, or other rites in particular societies; the evidence does not establish one uniform practice across all peoples or periods. Genesis 17 specifies a distinct covenant context and an eight-day timing for male infants.

### Biological observation

Circumcision is a physical surgical procedure involving removal of the foreskin. It alters bodily tissue and is not merely a verbal declaration or an invisible mental state. This limited biological description is sufficient for the comparison here; the study is not offering medical advice or evaluating the medical risks and benefits of the procedure.

### What this establishes

- The covenant sign described in Genesis 17 is embodied and physically observable.
- The practice was known in parts of the ancient Near East.
- The text specifies the covenant function and the timing required within Abraham's household.
- A physical mark can serve as a socially recognizable sign, though the exact social implications vary by context.

### What this does not establish

- It does not prove the divine origin or authority of the covenant.
- It does not show that neighboring cultures understood circumcision as Israel's covenant sign.
- It does not make the physical act the originating cause of the covenant.
- It does not establish that bodily alteration automatically produces inward faith, moral integrity, or spiritual status.
- It does not settle later theological debates about circumcision and the New Covenant.

### Relationship to Scripture

The scientific and historical observations describe a physical practice and its cultural contexts. Genesis 17 supplies the covenant meaning assigned within this narrative. The evidence streams can be compared, but historical prevalence cannot determine the theological meaning of the sign.

## 10. The Four GUA-W Questions

The four questions are recursive and inform one another.

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- Who originates the covenant?
- Who receives it?
- What is declared, what is promised, and what is commanded?
- What is the relationship between covenant promise and covenant obligation?
- What function does the physical sign serve in the text?
- How do we distinguish the historical existence of a practice from the covenant meaning assigned to it?
- How does the inclusion of household members relate to the later distinction between Ishmael and Isaac?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The established Source-to-Witness order helps preserve distinct roles:
- God identifies Himself and declares the covenant.
- God gives Abraham a new name and explains its significance.
- Abraham receives the declaration and is commanded to keep the covenant.
- Circumcision is assigned as the physical sign.
- The household is included in the prescribed observance.

GUA-W can organize these relationships without becoming the Author or changing the text. No new GUA-W law or architecture is proposed.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

I am witnessing divine declaration, promise, renaming, command, household obligation, and a physical sign. These are distinguishable features within one passage. The physical sign does not originate the covenant; it is designated by God as its token.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

Abraham receives a new name and a covenant sign. His identity in the narrative is shaped by a declaration that comes from God, not by self-authorship. The witness records both God's initiative and Abraham's required response. A faithful account must preserve the obligation as well as the promise; it must not report only the benefits while omitting the commanded sign.

### How the questions inform one another

The passage makes the difference between Source, declaration, recipient, obligation, and sign especially visible. The witness must neither turn the sign into the Author nor reduce the covenant to an abstract promise without the commanded response. Both belong in the record, with their roles kept distinct.

## 13. Triple Constraint review

### Biblical compliance — Pass, with lexical and canonical questions preserved

The study follows Genesis 17:1–14 and distinguishes God's declaration, Abraham's reception, covenant promises, household obligations, and circumcision as the sign. It does not collapse the promises of land, descendants, and covenant relationship. It does not confuse Ishmael's blessing with the later specified covenant line through Isaac. The precise etymology of *Shaddai*, the historical etymology of Abraham, and the full semantic range of *tamim* remain qualified.

### Scientific coherence — Contextual historical and biological observations

The physical description of circumcision and the historical observation that the practice was known among other ancient peoples are coherent with the cited historical sources. The study does not infer theological meaning from prevalence, nor claim that biology proves the covenant.

### GUA-W consistency — Pass

The study preserves Author → Word → Creation → Witness. God originates the covenant declaration; Abraham receives it and its obligations; circumcision is a sign appointed within the covenant. The derivative sign does not alter or originate the Source.

**Result:** Admissible for this study. Historical origins, the full lexical range of key Hebrew terms, and later canonical questions remain open.

### Supported observations and interpretations

- *Tamim* carries a range including whole, complete, sound, and blameless; “perfect” should be read contextually.
- The covenant declaration originates with God; Abraham is its recipient and is also given obligations.
- The name Abraham is explained in Genesis 17:5 through the wordplay “father of a multitude of nations”; historical etymology is debated.
- Genesis 17 develops the covenant relationship already narrated in Genesis 15.
- Circumcision was practiced in other ancient Near Eastern settings, but the covenant function and timing in Genesis 17 are specified by this text.
- The sign is physical and embodied, but the physical practice does not independently originate the covenant.
- Ishmael is already part of Abraham's household; the next verses will distinguish blessing Ishmael from the covenant line through Isaac.

### Unresolved questions carried forward

1. What is the most precise lexical and historical derivation of *Shaddai*?
2. What nuance of *tamim* best captures God's command in Genesis 17:1?
3. How does “walk before me” relate to the command to be *tamim*?
4. What is the precise relationship between the covenant-making language of Genesis 15:18 and covenant-establishing language in Genesis 17:7?
5. What is the historical etymology of Abraham, distinct from the biblical wordplay in Genesis 17:5?
6. How should the everlasting land promise in Genesis 17:8 be traced through later Scripture?
7. What are the social and legal implications of including household-born and purchased males in the covenant sign?
8. What historical evidence best establishes the timing and functions of circumcision among neighboring peoples?
9. Why is the eighth day specified, and what does the text itself establish about the timing?
10. How do the physical sign and later biblical language of circumcision of the heart relate without being confused?
11. How do Ishmael's circumcision, blessing, and status relate to the covenant through Isaac?
12. How do later New Testament treatments of circumcision develop the subject without erasing the Genesis narrative's own context?

### What must not be claimed

- That *Shaddai* has one certain etymology simply because the KJV translates it “Almighty.”
- That *tamim* necessarily means absolute flawlessness in the modern English sense.
- That Abraham's name has a settled historical etymology identical to the explanatory wordplay in Genesis 17:5.
- That Abraham originates the covenant.
- That circumcision originates or independently creates the covenant.
- That circumcision was unknown among other ancient Near Eastern peoples.
- That historical prevalence proves the covenant meaning assigned in Genesis.
- That the physical sign automatically establishes inward faith or moral integrity.
- That Ishmael's blessing and Isaac's covenant role are identical.
- That science or historical reconstruction proves the divine authority of the covenant.
- That GUA-W may be redesigned or extended from this passage without the user's authorization.
