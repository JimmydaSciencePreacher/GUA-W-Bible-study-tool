## 1. Passage text — KJV

**Genesis 3:20**

> And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living.

**Genesis 3:21**

> Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.

**Genesis 3:22**

> And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:

> Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken.

**Genesis 3:24**

> So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.

## 2. Immediate context and sequence

Genesis 3:14–19 records God's declarations concerning the serpent, the woman, Adam, and the ground. Genesis 3:20–24 follows those declarations with naming, God's provision of clothing, God's statement concerning the tree of life, the man's departure from Eden, and the guarding of the way to the tree.

The sequence is:
1. Adam names his wife Eve (verse 20).
2. The LORD God makes coats of skins and clothes Adam and his wife (verse 21).
3. The LORD God speaks concerning the man's knowledge of good and evil and the tree of life (verse 22).
4. The LORD God sends the man out of the Garden to till the ground (verse 23).
5. The man is driven out, and the way to the tree of life is guarded (verse 24).

The text does not state how much time elapsed between these actions. Preserve the narrated order without inventing a detailed timetable.

The passage closes the Garden account with a boundary: the man is outside the Garden, and the way to the tree of life is guarded.

## 3. Observe the text before interpreting it

### 3.1 Adam names his wife Eve — verse 20

Adam calls his wife's name **Eve**, and the verse gives the reason: **“because she was the mother of all living.”**

The naming is an action by Adam. The explanation is supplied by the narrator. The name is commonly connected with the Hebrew root associated with life/living (*ḥayah*), while the precise historical etymology of the name should not be made to carry more than the verse itself says.

The description “mother of all living” places Eve in relation to the human life that will proceed through her. It does not mean that she is the source or author of life. The narrative has already identified God as the giver of life: Genesis 2:7 says God breathed into man's nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living soul. Genesis 1:27–28 attributes humanity's creation and blessing to God.

There is a noteworthy placement: Adam names her after the declarations of Genesis 3:16 concerning childbirth and before the account of the LORD God clothing them and sending the man out. The verse does not explain Adam's inner reasoning beyond the stated reason for the name. Do not claim that the naming itself proves a particular emotional state or a complete theological understanding by Adam.

Genesis 4:1 later records Eve's words concerning the birth of Cain. Genesis 4:25 records the birth of Seth. These passages continue the account of human life outside Eden; they do not change the stated source of life from God to humanity.

### 3.2 God makes coats of skins and clothes them — verse 21

The subject of the action is explicit: **“the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.”** Both Adam and his wife are included.

This follows Genesis 3:7, where the man and woman sew fig leaves together and make themselves aprons after their eyes are opened and they know they are naked.

The contrast in the narrative is observable:
- Genesis 3:7: the man and woman make coverings for themselves from fig leaves.
- Genesis 3:21: the LORD God makes coats of skins and clothes them.

The text records God's provision. It does not state that the human coverings were accepted as sufficient, nor does it explicitly state why the skin garments were selected. It does show that God acts to clothe both of them.

**Important boundary:** Coats of skins imply garments made from animal hide. The verse does not narrate the taking of an animal's life, identify an animal, describe an altar, mention blood, or call the event a sacrifice. A sacrifice/atonement interpretation may be proposed through wider theological reasoning, but it is not an explicit statement of Genesis 3:21. Do not convert a possible canonical association into a narrated fact.

Nor should we infer that clothing itself is evil or that the garments remove the consequences declared in verses 14–19. The text says they are clothed; the expulsion and guarded way still follow.

### 3.3 “As one of us” and the knowledge of good and evil — verse 22

The LORD God says:

> “Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil…”

This language recalls:
- the serpent's claim in Genesis 3:5, “ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil”;
- the woman's observation in Genesis 3:6;
- the opening of the eyes and awareness of nakedness in Genesis 3:7.

The text now records God Himself speaking of the man as having become “as one of us” in relation to knowing good and evil. That does not mean the serpent's entire account was true, nor does it say the man became God, became equal to God in every respect, or acquired omniscience.

The phrase “as one of us” is plural language. Genesis 1:26 also records God saying, “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness.” These passages invite canonical theological examination. The plural wording should be acknowledged, but Genesis 3:22 by itself does not explain the identity or number of the referents in a way that settles every later theological debate. Do not use the phrase alone as a complete proof of a particular doctrine, and do not erase its plural form.

The phrase “to know good and evil” also needs care. In Genesis 2:16–17, God gave a command concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. In Genesis 3:5, the serpent promised knowledge. In Genesis 3:22, God acknowledges that the man has become as one of “us” in this respect. The text does not say that Adam and Eve now possess God's perfect wisdom, moral purity, or complete knowledge.

The distinction between knowing good and evil and possessing the authority to define good and evil is important. Genesis 3:22 says the man has become as one of us “to know good and evil”; it does not say that the man has become the Author, the source of moral truth, or the authority over God's command. That distinction is an inference from the wider narrative and must be labeled as such, not presented as a quotation from this verse.

### 3.4 The tree of life and the stated restriction — verse 22

The LORD God continues:

> “and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:”

The text names a possible sequence:
1. the man might put forth his hand;
2. take also of the tree of life;
3. eat;
4. live for ever.

The stated concern is access to the tree of life and the outcome of living for ever. The word **“also”** links this to the earlier eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The tree of life was already named in Genesis 2:9, among the trees planted in the Garden. Genesis 2:16–17 gave the prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; the text did not state that the tree of life was forbidden to the man at that time.

Now, after the transgression, God declares that the man must not take and eat from the tree of life and live for ever. The next verses enact the restriction by sending the man out and guarding the way.

The passage does not say that the tree of life is merely symbolic, nor does it explain its biological or metaphysical mechanism. It also does not explicitly state that the tree would make the man morally restored or undo the transgression. The stated result is “live for ever.” Preserve that wording.

The restriction may be understood as judgment, mercy, protection, or a combination in later theological interpretation. The text explicitly states the reason in terms of preventing the man from taking and eating and living for ever; it does not explicitly label the motive “mercy.” Do not make an interpretation erase the stated consequence.

### 3.5 Sent out to till the ground — verse 23

The LORD God **“sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken.”**

The wording recalls Genesis 2:7, where God formed man of the dust of the ground, and Genesis 3:17–19, where Adam is told that he will eat through sorrowful labor and return to the ground.

The man had work before the transgression: Genesis 2:15 says the LORD God put him in the Garden to dress it and to keep it. Therefore, this verse does not introduce work as a new human activity. It places the man outside the Garden and assigns the tilling of the ground from which he was taken.

The phrase “from whence he was taken” connects human origin and post-expulsion labor. The ground is both the material source from which Adam was formed and the place from which he must now obtain food.

The verse says “sent him forth.” Verse 24 then says “he drove out the man.” Both expressions belong in the record. Do not soften verse 24 by quoting only the less forceful phrase in verse 23.

### 3.6 Driven out; the way guarded — verse 24

The verse says:
- “So he drove out the man”;
- God placed cherubims at the east of the Garden of Eden;
- a flaming sword turned every way;
- the purpose was “to keep the way of the tree of life.”

The verb translated “drove out” is forceful expulsion language. The narrative therefore emphasizes that the man's departure is not merely a voluntary relocation. God sends him forth, and the text then describes him as driven out.

The cherubims are placed at the east of the Garden. The verse does not describe their appearance, number beyond the plural form, or exact physical arrangement. The text identifies them as cherubims and assigns them a guarding function in relation to the way to the tree of life.

The phrase “a flaming sword which turned every way” describes the sword as an active barrier. The precise mechanics of the image are not explained. Do not invent a machine-like mechanism or a map of the Garden's entrance.

The stated purpose is clear: **“to keep the way of the tree of life.”** The word “keep” is related to the Hebrew *shamar*, a word used elsewhere for keeping, guarding, or watching. It is notable that Genesis 2:15 had said Adam was placed in the Garden “to dress it and to keep it.” Now, after the transgression, cherubims guard the way to the tree of life. The repeated guarding language invites comparison, but the roles are not identical: Adam's assigned keeping of the Garden and the cherubims' guarding of the way are distinct actions.

The Garden's way is now guarded. The text does not say that the tree of life ceased to exist. It says access to its way is kept.

## 6. Creation and scientific investigation

This passage is primarily concerned with naming, divine provision, moral knowledge, the tree of life, expulsion, and guarded sacred space. It does not give a measurable physical mechanism for the cherubim, the flaming sword, or the tree of life.

A disciplined unified investigation should not manufacture a scientific parallel where the text does not present a scientific question that current evidence can address.

### 6.1 Animal skins and clothing

**Question:** What does the material reference establish, and what does it leave unstated?

The text establishes that the garments were made of skins. In ordinary material terms, animal hide is animal-derived material. But Genesis 3:21 does not identify an animal, describe how the skins were obtained, or state that an animal was offered as a sacrifice.

Archaeology and anthropology can investigate the history and use of animal hides for clothing and other purposes in human societies. Such evidence may describe human material practices, but it cannot establish the specific event narrated in Genesis 3:21 or prove that the garments were produced by a sacrificial rite.

**Disposition:** Record the material stated in the text. Do not claim a sacrifice or atonement mechanism from this verse alone.

### 6.2 The tree of life and biological longevity

**Question:** Can present biology establish the nature of the tree of life or the meaning of “live for ever”?

Biology can investigate lifespan, aging, cellular maintenance, disease, and longevity in organisms. Those investigations do not identify the Eden tree of life or establish that a known biological process accounts for the text's statement. The passage does not provide a botanical description, species, biochemical pathway, or experimental condition for the tree.

**Disposition:** No scientific identification is warranted. The text's statement about eating and living for ever remains a textual/theological claim. Do not identify the tree with a modern plant, compound, or longevity technology.

### 6.3 Cherubim and the flaming sword

**Question:** Does the passage provide an observable physical description that would permit a scientific classification?

No. It names cherubim and a flaming sword and states their guarding purpose. It does not give a specimen, anatomy, dimensions, material composition, or repeatable observation. Other biblical passages provide additional theological and visionary descriptions of cherubim, but those are not scientific measurements of the beings in Genesis 3:24.

**Disposition:** No scientific classification is made. The passage is not a license to label cherubim as animals, human beings, machines, or a known physical phenomenon.

### 6.4 Naming and human language

**Question:** Can linguistics and anthropology describe the human practice of naming, and what would that establish here?

Human language research can investigate naming practices, kinship terms, and the way communities connect names with meanings. That can provide general context for the act of naming. It cannot establish Adam's unrecorded mental process or replace the reason the narrator explicitly supplies: Eve was named because she was the mother of all living.

**Disposition:** General language research may describe naming as a human practice, but it does not add a hidden motive to the narrative.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The passage makes several source/derivative distinctions visible:
- Eve is mother of human life in the narrated family line, but God is the Creator and giver of life.
- Adam and his wife make coverings in verse 7; God makes and provides clothing in verse 21.
- The man has become “as one of us” in the stated respect of knowing good and evil, but the text does not make him the Author or source of moral truth.
- The tree of life remains named, but access is restricted.
- The man is sent out and driven out; the cherubim and flaming sword guard the way.

GUA-W helps organize these distinctions. It must not be used to invent a mechanism for the tree, the clothing, or the cherubim.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The passage records the transition from the Garden to life outside it. Adam names his wife Eve; God clothes both; God states that the man must not take from the tree of life and live for ever; God sends the man out to till the ground; and God guards the way back to the tree.

The text does not say that the tree disappears. It says that its way is guarded. It does not say that the garments are a sacrifice. It says God made coats of skins and clothed them. It does not say the man became omniscient. It says he became as one of “us” to know good and evil.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must report the difference between:
- a mother and the Creator/source of life;
- a garment and an explicitly narrated sacrifice;
- knowledge and authorship or authority;
- a tree's continued existence and access to that tree;
- being sent forth and being driven out;
- a named heavenly being and an imagined physical classification;
- a canonical connection and a detail directly stated in Genesis.

A faithful Witness does not fill textual silence with a preferred mechanism. It records what is present, investigates what can be investigated, and marks the rest unresolved.

**How the four questions inform one another:** The text shows God providing clothing and guarding the way to the tree of life. GUA-W helps preserve the Source, provision, boundary, and witness distinctions. Scientific inquiry can describe material hides or human naming practices, but it cannot turn those observations into an explanation of the divine action or the guarded way.

## 10. Triple Constraint review

**Biblical compliance:** The study follows the narrated order, preserves the explicit naming reason, distinguishes human-made coverings from God's clothing, keeps the plural wording “one of us,” and records both “sent him forth” and “drove out.” It does not claim that the tree is destroyed, that the skins are a narrated sacrifice, or that Adam becomes omniscient.

**Scientific coherence:** The limited material and language questions are bounded. Present-day archaeology, anthropology, biology, and linguistics cannot reconstruct the specific divine actions or identify the tree of life or cherubim. No unsupported scientific identification is admitted.

**GUA-W consistency:** God remains the Source and giver of life. Human motherhood, knowledge, clothing, and observation remain derivative. The guarded way is recorded as a boundary declared and enacted by the Author. No GUA-W architecture is changed.

**Disposition:** The direct textual findings are admissible. The precise theological significance of “as one of us,” the full nature of the tree of life, the reason for the garments beyond the stated act of clothing, and the full nature of the cherubim and flaming sword remain open where the passage does not explain them.

2. The LORD God makes coats of skins and clothes Adam and his wife.
3. God says the man has become as one of “us” to know good and evil.
4. God states that the man must not take from the tree of life, eat, and live for ever.

6. The text then says God drove the man out.
7. Cherubim and a flaming sword turning every way guard the way to the tree of life.

### Supported observations
- God's clothing follows the self-made fig-leaf coverings in Genesis 3:7.
- Eve's name is linked in the text to human life proceeding through her, while God remains Creator and giver of life.
- The tree of life is present in Genesis 2:9 and its access is guarded in Genesis 3:22–24.
- Genesis 3:23 returns to the ground from which Adam was formed in Genesis 2:7.
- The word “keep/guard” creates a textual connection between Adam's earlier task in Genesis 2:15 and the guarding of the way in Genesis 3:24, though the roles differ.
- The Garden narrative ends with exclusion from access to the tree of life, not with a statement that the tree itself has been destroyed.

### Unresolved
1. What is the full meaning of the name *Ḥavvah* and its relation to the Hebrew word family for life?
2. What is the full significance of “as one of us” in Genesis 3:22, read alongside Genesis 1:26 and the rest of Scripture?
3. What exactly is the nature and function of the tree of life, beyond the stated connection between eating and living for ever?
4. Why does God choose coats of skins as the covering? The text states the action but does not explain the full rationale.
5. Does the provision of skins have a later canonical connection to sacrifice or atonement? Genesis 3:21 alone does not establish it.
6. What is the nature and appearance of the cherubim in Genesis 3:24? The verse does not describe them.
7. What is the precise imagery or operation of the flaming sword “which turned every way”?
8. How should the guarding of the way to the tree of life relate to later biblical promises concerning the tree of life in Revelation 2 and 22?
9. What is the relationship between the Garden's guarded way and the later biblical imagery of God's presence, sanctuary, and cherubim?

### Do not claim yet
- That Eve is the original source of life.
- That Genesis 3:21 explicitly narrates a sacrifice or blood atonement.
- That the man became God, omniscient, or equal to God in every respect.
- That the tree of life was destroyed.
- That cherubim are ordinary animals, humans, machines, or a known scientific phenomenon.
