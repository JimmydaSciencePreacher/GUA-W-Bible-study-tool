# Study 0020 — Genesis 2:4–7
## The generations of the heavens and earth, the ground, and the formation of man

**Study status:** Passage investigated and recorded as a unified Scripture-and-Creation study. The passage introduces the *toledoth* heading for the heavens and earth, describes conditions before the field vegetation specified in the passage, mentions water rising from the earth, and narrates the forming of man from the dust of the ground and God's breathing the breath of life into his nostrils. The relation between the vegetation terms in Genesis 2:5 and the vegetation created in Genesis 1:11–12, the precise meaning of *'ed* in verse 6, and the relation between Genesis 1:26–27 and 2:7 remain open for careful examination.

## 1. Passage text — KJV

> **Genesis 2:4**  
> These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,
>
> **Genesis 2:5**  
> And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.
>
> **Genesis 2:6**  
> But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground.
>
> **Genesis 2:7**  
> And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

The passage moves through several connected statements:
1. A heading identifies the generations/account of the heavens and earth when they were created (v. 4).
2. The narrative identifies conditions concerning the plant of the field and herb of the field (v. 5).
3. It gives two stated conditions: no rain had yet been caused by the LORD God, and there was no man to till the ground (v. 5).
4. A mist or water-related phenomenon rises from the earth and waters the face of the ground (v. 6).
5. The LORD God forms man from the dust of the ground (v. 7).
6. God breathes into man's nostrils the breath of life (v. 7).
7. Man becomes a living soul (v. 7).

The text explicitly names the LORD God as the acting subject in the forming and breathing. Man is the formed recipient; he does not originate his own life.

## 2. Immediate literary and canonical context

### 2.1 The transition from Genesis 1:31 and 2:1–3

Genesis 1 closes the six-day account with the declaration that everything God made was “very good” (1:31). Genesis 2:1–3 declares the heavens and earth finished and the seventh day blessed and sanctified. Genesis 2:4 then begins a new literary section with the formula “These are the generations…”

The chapter division is not itself a reason to detach 2:4 from the preceding account. The text moves from the completed heavens and earth to an account focused on the earth, the ground, the Garden, and the man.

### 2.2 Genesis 1:26–27 and Genesis 2:7

Genesis 1:26–27 states that God created mankind in His image and likeness, and that He created them male and female. Genesis 2:7 describes the LORD God forming the man from the dust of the ground, breathing into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man becoming a living soul.

These passages emphasize different features:
- Genesis 1:26–27: humanity's creation in God's image and likeness; male and female; the context of humanity's place and vocation within the created order.
- Genesis 2:7: the man's material formation from the ground; God's breathing of life's breath; the result that man became a living soul.

The two passages can be read as complementary perspectives within the same canonical account. That is a coherent reading, but the exact literary relation and sequence should be examined rather than asserted without argument. Genesis 2:7 does not repeat the image phrase, and Genesis 1:27 does not describe the dust-forming or breath-giving detail. Neither omission cancels the statement made in the other passage.

### 2.3 The vegetation language in Genesis 2:5

Genesis 1:11–12 already records God's command and the earth's bringing forth of vegetation, including grass, herb yielding seed, and fruit tree yielding fruit after its kind. Genesis 2:5 refers to “the plant of the field” and “the herb of the field,” in a setting where rain and a man to till the ground are also mentioned.

The wording therefore requires care. It is not safe to assume that Genesis 2:5 means no vegetation of any kind existed anywhere, because Genesis 1:11–12 has already described vegetation. One interpretation is that Genesis 2:5 focuses on field growth or cultivated vegetation in relation to rain and human cultivation. The precise identification of the plant categories and the force of the “before” clauses remain subjects for lexical and contextual study.

**Do not flatten the two passages:** Genesis 1:11–12 and Genesis 2:5 must both be allowed to say what their wording and context support.

### 2.4 The order of “earth and heavens”

Genesis 2:4 uses “the earth and the heavens,” whereas Genesis 1:1 says “the heaven and the earth.” The order is reversed. The reversal is observable; its significance should not be exaggerated. In Genesis 2:4 the narrative focus is turning toward the earth and the human setting. That may help explain the order, but the text does not explicitly state that this is the reason.

### A. “Generations” — *toledoth*

The word *toledoth* is related to the idea of begetting or bringing forth and is used in biblical headings that introduce generations, descendants, or an account of what proceeds from a person or subject. In Genesis, “These are the generations of…” functions as a recurring literary formula.

In Genesis 2:4 the heading is attached to “the heavens and of the earth.” It introduces the account that follows concerning the created world and its human setting. Translating it simply as “generations” preserves the KJV wording, but readers should recognize that in this construction it can introduce an account or history, not only a list of biological descendants.

**Textual conclusion:** Genesis 2:4 marks a new section by means of the *toledoth* formula.  
**Not established by the heading alone:** A complete theory of the literary structure of Genesis or a claim that the heavens and earth literally reproduce descendants.

### B. “In the day” — *beyom*

The phrase translated “in the day that the LORD God made…” uses *yom* (“day”) with a preposition and an infinitive construction. “In the day” can function as a temporal expression meaning when or at the time that an action occurred. The phrase should not be treated as though it must always denote one isolated twenty-four-hour interval regardless of syntax and context.

In Genesis 2:4, the phrase introduces the time or occasion of the LORD God's making of earth and heavens. Its exact relation to the day sequence in Genesis 1 should be considered in the context of the full passage, not decided by this phrase alone.

**Open issue:** How does the temporal phrase function here in relation to the six-day structure already narrated?

### C. “Plant of the field” — *siach hassadeh*

The KJV “plant of the field” translates a phrase whose first noun, *siach*, has a limited and debated range in biblical usage. It is not automatically identical to every botanical category translated “plant” in modern English. The accompanying phrase “of the field” and the reference to rain and cultivation are important contextual controls.

### D. “Herb of the field” — *esev hassadeh*

*Esev* is a broad vegetation term often rendered herb or plant. The phrase in Genesis 2:5 is not necessarily identical in scope to every occurrence of *esev* elsewhere. The text's reference to the field and the absence of rain and a cultivator must be considered together.

**Important boundary:** Do not claim that all plant life was absent from the earth. Genesis 1:11–12 has already stated that God caused vegetation to appear. Whether Genesis 2:5 refers to uncultivated field growth, cultivated crops, particular shrubs/herbs, or another narrower category remains unresolved in this pass.

### E. “To till” — *avad*

The verb *avad* can mean work, serve, labor, or cultivate depending on context. Here the KJV renders it “till,” in reference to the ground. The passage connects the absence of a man to work the ground with the stated conditions concerning field vegetation.

The word's broader meaning should not be imported into this verse without contextual support. Its agricultural sense is appropriate here.

### F. “Ground” — *adamah*

The word *adamah* means ground, soil, or land. It is closely related in sound and wordplay to *adam*, man. Genesis 2:7 makes the relationship especially visible: the man (*adam*) is formed from the dust (*afar*) of the ground (*adamah*).

The wordplay is part of the passage's presentation. It emphasizes man's relation to the ground from which he is formed. It does not mean that the man is merely soil or that the text reduces human identity to chemistry.

### G. “Mist” — *'ed*

The Hebrew noun *'ed* is rare, and its precise meaning in Genesis 2:6 is disputed. The KJV renders it “mist.” Other translations and interpreters have proposed a stream, flow, or other water-related phenomenon rising from the earth. The verb that follows is associated with watering or making the ground drink.

The verse clearly presents water rising from the earth and watering the face of the ground in the KJV rendering. The exact physical phenomenon should not be claimed as settled from this rare noun alone.

**Open issue:** Is the verse describing mist/vapor, a spring or flow, groundwater discharge, or another form of irrigation? Further Hebrew usage, ancient translation evidence, and geological/hydrological comparison are needed.

### H. “Formed” — *yatsar*

The verb *yatsar* means to form or fashion. It is used of shaping or forming, and can evoke the work of a potter. Genesis 2:7 presents God as the one who forms the man from the dust of the ground.

This is a direct statement of divine action in the text. The potter image may help a reader understand the verb's force, but the verse does not provide a modern anatomical or biochemical description of the process.

### I. “Dust” — *afar*

*'Afar* means dust or loose earth. The man is formed from the dust of the *adamah*. This is the textual basis for speaking of man's earthly material origin in this passage.

Scientific analysis of the human body's elemental composition may offer a limited comparison with the material constituents found in the earth. Such comparison does not reconstruct the original formation event or explain God's action. It must not be presented as though the text were a modern chemistry statement.

### J. “Breathed” — *naphach*; “breath of life” — *nishmath chayyim*

The verb *naphach* means to breathe or blow. The noun *neshamah* refers to breath, and *chayyim* to life. The KJV renders the phrase “the breath of life.” The text identifies God as the giver and the man's life as the result.

The wording presents a direct act of God. It should not be reduced to the ordinary mechanics of pulmonary ventilation. Nor does this verse by itself explain every theological question concerning spirit, soul, consciousness, or the relation between body and life.

### K. “Living soul” — *nephesh chayyah*

The phrase *nephesh chayyah* is rendered “living soul.” The same phrase or construction appears in Genesis 1 for living creatures (Genesis 1:20, 24), and is used again in Genesis 2:19 for the living creatures brought to Adam.

This shared language matters. It cautions against assuming that *nephesh* always means an immaterial, separable entity in every occurrence. In context it can refer to a living being or creature. Genesis 2:7 says that the man became a living *nephesh* after God breathed life's breath into him.

This shared expression does not erase humanity's distinct image-bearing status stated in Genesis 1:26–27. The text can use a shared life-term while also distinguishing humanity by image, vocation, and other statements.

**Textual conclusion:** Man becomes a living being through God's life-giving action.  
**Not established by this phrase alone:** A complete doctrine of the soul, consciousness, or the intermediate state after death.

## 4. Creation investigation

### 4.1 Investigation question

What can present-day observations of soil, water movement, plant growth, human material composition, and respiration help us understand about the created conditions described in Genesis 2:5–7? Which connections are direct observations, which are comparisons, and which questions remain outside the reach of present measurement?

### 4.2 Soil and the human body

Modern biology and chemistry identify many elements in the human body that also occur in Earth's crust, soil, water, and food systems. The human body is materially dependent on the wider created environment: water and nutrients are obtained through food and drink, and many elements cycle through geological and biological systems.

This is a limited material comparison with the passage's statement that man was formed from the dust of the ground. It does not establish that Genesis 2:7 is a chemical formula, identify the precise composition of the original dust, or experimentally reproduce God's forming of the first man.

A source-level investigation should distinguish:
- Elements present in the human body.
- Their biological roles and typical ranges.
- Their sources and pathways through food, water, and environment.
- The difference between elemental similarity and a demonstrated causal account of human origins.

### 4.3 Soil, water, and plant growth

Plant growth depends on interacting conditions that include water, light, carbon dioxide, mineral nutrients, temperature, and the plant's biological organization. Soil can provide physical support, water storage, aeration, and mineral nutrients, while water movement through soil depends on texture, structure, porosity, gravity, and hydraulic gradients.

Genesis 2:5–6 links field vegetation with the stated absence of rain and a man to till the ground, then describes water rising from the earth and watering the ground. Present-day hydrology can investigate mechanisms by which water moves upward or emerges at the surface, including capillary movement and groundwater discharge. These are possible comparison domains, not proof of the exact phenomenon described by *'ed*.

Do not assume that the present global water cycle or a particular modern irrigation process is a complete reconstruction of the conditions in the passage.

### 4.4 Rain, groundwater, and the rare term *'ed*

Hydrology distinguishes precipitation from groundwater, soil moisture, surface flow, capillary rise, and evapotranspiration. These processes can all affect the water available to plants, but they are not interchangeable.

The Hebrew *'ed* is too rare and disputed to identify confidently with one modern hydrological mechanism on lexical grounds alone. The KJV's “mist” should be recorded faithfully, while alternate interpretations remain visible.

A later source-level pass should compare:
- Hebrew lexicons and the term's other proposed biblical occurrence(s).
- Ancient versions and translation history.
- The syntax of Genesis 2:6.
- Geological and hydrological mechanisms that could produce the described effects.
- The limits of reconstructing ancient or primordial conditions from modern observations.

### 4.5 Human respiration and the breath of life

Modern physiology describes respiration at several levels: ventilation (movement of air into and out of the lungs), gas exchange, transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide, and cellular respiration. These are measurable biological processes in living humans.

Genesis 2:7 describes God breathing into the man's nostrils the breath of life. The verse is not a physiology textbook and does not specify the modern mechanisms just listed. The relationship between divine life-giving action and creaturely respiration is therefore not established as an identical mechanism by this passage.

The passage's primary claim is theological and ontological: man's life is received from God. Scientific study can describe present human respiratory and metabolic processes; it cannot measure or experimentally reproduce the divine act narrated here.

### 4.6 Growth, cultivation, and human vocation

Genesis 2:5 notes that there was not yet a man to till the ground. Genesis 1:28 had already stated humanity's given vocation concerning the earth and living creatures. Genesis 2:15 will later place the man in the Garden to dress and keep it.

These passages make human work within Creation a significant theme. Agricultural science, soil science, and ecology can investigate how human cultivation affects soil structure, nutrient cycling, plant communities, water use, and ecosystem function. These studies can inform responsible descriptions of cultivation, but they must not be read as though modern agriculture were identical to the first man's task.
