# Study 0071 — Genesis 26:1–35 (KJV)
## Isaac, the famine, the covenant promise, the wells, and the distinction between inheritance and human choices

- **Primary passage:** Genesis 26:1–35 (KJV)
- **Previous study:** Study 0070 — Genesis 25:19–34, the generations of Isaac, Jacob and Esau, and the birthright
- **Next passage:** Genesis 27:1–46 (KJV), Isaac's blessing and the actions of Jacob and Esau
- **Central question:** How does Genesis 26 distinguish God's covenant promise and provision from Isaac's fear, human conflict, material circumstances, and the choices made by the next generation?
- **Method:** Begin with the KJV text. Separate what the passage states from observation, inference, and interpretation. Read cross-references in context. Apply the Triple Constraint Law without claiming that scientific coherence is scientific confirmation.

---

## 1. Immediate context

Genesis 25:19–34 introduced Isaac's generations, Rebekah's pregnancy, the two children struggling within her, God's declaration concerning the two nations and the elder serving the younger, and the birthright transaction between Esau and Jacob.

Genesis 26 now follows Isaac through a famine, a journey to Gerar, God's direct communication concerning the covenant promise, Isaac's fear concerning Rebekah, conflict over wells, a move to Beersheba, and a covenant agreement with Abimelech.

The chapter closes by recording Esau's marriages and the grief they caused Isaac and Rebekah.

The passage therefore contains several distinct kinds of events:
- God's speech and covenant promise.
- Isaac's response to that speech.
- Isaac's fear and misrepresentation concerning Rebekah.
- The actions of Abimelech and his people.
- Disputes over wells and access to water.
- God's appearance and reassurance at Beersheba.
- A human oath and a meal.
- Esau's marriage choices and their effect on his parents.

These events must not be collapsed into one category. God's promise is not the same thing as Isaac's conduct, Abimelech's conduct, a well dispute, or Esau's decisions.

## 3. Verse 1 — Famine and the movement to Gerar

Genesis 26:1 records a famine in the land, described as being besides the first famine that was in the days of Abraham. Isaac goes unto Abimelech king of the Philistines unto Gerar.

### Direct observations

- A famine occurs in the land.
- The text distinguishes this famine from an earlier famine in Abraham's days.
- Isaac goes to Gerar.
- Abimelech is identified as king of the Philistines.
- The passage does not yet state Isaac's complete reason for choosing Gerar.

### Connection with Abraham

Genesis 12:10 records a famine in Abraham's days and Abraham's journey into Egypt. Genesis 26:1 deliberately recalls that earlier famine.

The two famine accounts are related by the text, but they are not the same event. Isaac's journey is to Gerar in this passage; the earlier account records Abraham going down into Egypt.

The reader should notice the repeated circumstance without assuming that every action or outcome will be identical.

### What is not established

The verse does not provide a measured severity, duration, population impact, or climatic explanation for the famine. Those questions would require evidence beyond this verse.

The narrative establishes the famine as the circumstance in which Isaac's next actions occur.

## 4. Verses 2–5 — The LORD speaks to Isaac and confirms the promise

The LORD appears unto Isaac and tells him not to go down into Egypt. He instructs Isaac to dwell in the land that He will tell him of. The LORD then says that He will be with Isaac and bless him, give the lands unto Isaac and his seed, and perform the oath sworn unto Abraham.

The passage includes the promise of multiplication and the blessing of all nations through Abraham's seed. Verse 5 gives the stated reason in the text: Abraham obeyed God's voice and kept His charge, commandments, statutes, and laws.

### Direct observations

- The LORD appears to Isaac.
- God gives Isaac a direction concerning where not to go and where to dwell.
- God promises His presence and blessing.
- God speaks of giving the lands to Isaac and his seed.
- God connects this promise to the oath sworn unto Abraham.
- God refers to Abraham's obedience.
- The passage identifies God's speech as the source of the promise.

### The promise is not originated by Isaac

The promise is spoken to Isaac, but its origin is not assigned to Isaac. God identifies the oath as the one sworn unto Abraham and declares that He will perform it.

Isaac is the recipient of the divine word in this passage. He does not author the promise, establish its authority, or make it true by independently creating it.

This preserves the governing distinction:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The text presents God as the One speaking and promising. Isaac receives the instruction and promise.

### Abraham's obedience in verse 5

The verse explicitly connects God's stated action with Abraham's obedience. It names Abraham's obedience and lists charge, commandments, statutes, and laws.

The study must preserve what the verse says without expanding it beyond its wording. Genesis 26:5 does not, by itself, provide a complete inventory of every command Abraham received or every occasion on which he obeyed.

Nor should the verse be used to make Abraham the originating source of the covenant. The passage says God swore the oath and God will perform it. Abraham's obedience is stated as part of the explanation in verse 5; it does not replace God as the promiser.

### Connection with earlier Scripture

- Genesis 12:1–3 records God's call and promise to Abraham.
- Genesis 15:1–6 records God's promise concerning Abraham's seed.
- Genesis 17:1–8 records the covenant and the promise concerning Abraham's seed and the land.
- Genesis 22:15–18 records the oath and the promise of blessing through Abraham's seed.
- Genesis 25:11 records God blessing Isaac after Abraham's death.

Genesis 26:2–5 explicitly brings the promise forward to Isaac. The narrative does not describe a new human author of the covenant; it records God's continued word and action.

### Open question

The precise relationship among the terms charge, commandments, statutes, and laws in Genesis 26:5 deserves a broader study across their biblical uses. This verse should not be made to settle every later theological question about the nature, scope, or timing of those terms.

## 5. Verses 6–11 — Isaac, Rebekah, fear, and Abimelech's intervention

Isaac dwells in Gerar. When the men of the place ask about Rebekah, Isaac says, “She is my sister.” The text explains that he fears to say she is his wife because he thinks the men might kill him for Rebekah, because she is fair to look upon.

After Isaac has been there a long time, Abimelech looks out at a window and sees Isaac sporting with Rebekah his wife. Abimelech calls Isaac and asks why he had said Rebekah was his sister. Isaac answers that he feared he might die because of her.

Abimelech then rebukes Isaac and declares that one of the people might have lain with Rebekah, which would have brought guilt upon the people. Abimelech gives a charge that anyone who touches Isaac or Rebekah shall surely be put to death.

### Direct observations

- Isaac remains in Gerar.
- The men of the place ask about Rebekah.
- Isaac identifies her as his sister.
- The text explicitly identifies fear as Isaac's reason.
- Abimelech sees Isaac behaving affectionately with Rebekah.
- Abimelech recognizes that Rebekah is Isaac's wife.
- Isaac acknowledges his fear.
- Abimelech rebukes Isaac.
- Abimelech identifies the possible consequence of a man taking Rebekah.
- Abimelech issues a protective command concerning Isaac and Rebekah.

### Fear and the Witness's report

The text does not hide Isaac's stated motive. Isaac says he feared he might die.

The study should neither excuse the misrepresentation nor invent a motive beyond the one recorded. Isaac's fear is explicitly stated; the exact private calculations or thoughts beyond his answer are not supplied.

A Witness must report both the promise spoken by God and the conduct recorded of Isaac. The presence of a divine promise does not mean every action of the recipient is presented as exemplary.

### Comparison with Abraham's earlier accounts

Genesis 12:10–20 and Genesis 20:1–18 record earlier episodes involving Abraham and the identification of Sarah as his sister. Genesis 26 presents a related pattern involving Isaac and Rebekah.

The accounts should be compared carefully:
- Genesis 12 and Genesis 20 concern Abraham and Sarah.
- Genesis 26 concerns Isaac and Rebekah.
- The circumstances, statements, responses, and outcomes must be read in their own passages.
- A repeated narrative pattern does not make the people, events, or every detail identical.

Genesis 26:7 gives Isaac's stated fear. Genesis 26:10 records Abimelech's concern about guilt coming upon the people. Both statements belong in the record.

### Abimelech's role

Abimelech's intervention is recorded as a human action. He sees what Isaac has done, questions him, rebukes him, and issues a command protecting the couple.

The text does not identify Abimelech as the source of God's covenant promise. His command is a protective action within the narrative, not the origin of the promise made in verses 2–5.

### What the passage does not say

- It does not say that Isaac's misrepresentation was commanded by God.
- It does not say that fear made the statement true.
- It does not say that Abimelech knew the covenant promise spoken to Isaac in verses 2–5.
- It does not narrate every conversation between Isaac and Rebekah.
- It does not give a complete account of the political structure or population of Gerar.

## 6. Verses 12–17 — Isaac prospers, envy arises, and Isaac moves

Isaac sows in the land and receives in the same year an hundredfold. The LORD blesses him. Isaac becomes great, goes forward, and grows until he becomes very great. He has possession of flocks, herds, and a great store of servants.

The Philistines envy him. They stop up the wells that Abraham's servants had digged, filling them with earth. Abimelech tells Isaac to go from them because Isaac is much mightier than they.

Isaac departs and pitches his tent in the valley of Gerar, where he dwells.

### Direct observations

- Isaac sows in the land.
- The text records an hundredfold return in the same year.
- The LORD is identified as the One blessing Isaac.
- Isaac's possessions and household increase.
- The Philistines envy him.
- Wells previously dug by Abraham's servants are stopped up.
- Abimelech tells Isaac to depart.
- Isaac moves into the valley of Gerar.

### The stated relationship between blessing and prosperity

Genesis 26:12 directly says that Isaac received an hundredfold in the same year and that the LORD blessed him. The following verses describe his increase.

The passage itself attributes the blessing to the LORD. It does not give a scientific account of the soil, rainfall, seed variety, agricultural practice, or exact yield measurement procedure.

The study should not replace the text's stated attribution with an invented mechanism. Nor should it claim that the verse supplies enough agricultural data to reconstruct the event scientifically.

### Envy and conflict

The text identifies envy as the response of the Philistines. Their action against the wells is concrete: they stop them up with earth.

The passage connects Isaac's prosperity with the envy and conflict that follow in the narrative. It does not say that prosperity automatically produces envy in every setting or that every conflict over a resource has the same cause.

### Isaac's departure

Abimelech tells Isaac to go away, and Isaac moves. The text records the movement without supplying a detailed account of every negotiation or the exact boundaries of the land involved.

Isaac's departure does not mean that the promise spoken by God has been revoked. The chapter continues to record Isaac's movement, the well disputes, and God's appearance at Beersheba.

## 7. Verses 18–22 — The wells, contention, and Rehoboth

Isaac returns to the wells of water that had been digged in the days of Abraham. The Philistines had stopped them after Abraham's death. Isaac calls them by the names his father had called them.

Isaac's servants dig in the valley and find a well of springing water. The herdsmen of Gerar strive with Isaac's herdsmen, claiming the water. Isaac names the well Esek because they strove with him.

They dig another well, and there is strife over that also. Isaac names it Sitnah. He then moves from there and digs another well. The text says they did not strive for that one, and Isaac calls it Rehoboth, saying that the LORD has made room for them and that they shall be fruitful in the land.

### Direct observations

- Isaac reopens wells associated with Abraham.
- The wells had been stopped after Abraham's death.
- Isaac restores the names Abraham had used.
- Isaac's servants dig and find springing water.
- The herdsmen of Gerar contend over the first well.
- Isaac names it Esek.
- A second well is disputed, and Isaac names it Sitnah.
- Isaac moves and digs another well.
- No dispute is recorded over the third well.
- Isaac names it Rehoboth.
- Isaac attributes the room they now have to the LORD.

### Names and recorded events

The passage supplies names and their narrative associations:
- **Esek** is associated with striving.
- **Sitnah** is associated with contention or opposition.
- **Rehoboth** is associated in Isaac's statement with the room the LORD has made.

The names are part of the witness record. The sequence matters: digging, finding water, dispute, moving, digging again, and the absence of a recorded dispute at the third well.

### The wells and continuity with Abraham

Genesis 21:22–34 records Abraham's dealings with Abimelech and the well at Beersheba. Genesis 26:15 and 18 refer to wells dug in Abraham's days and later stopped by the Philistines.

The text therefore connects Isaac's actions with earlier work associated with Abraham. Isaac is not described as the original digger of every well he reopens. He restores access to wells that had been dug earlier and preserves their names.

This is a specific narrative continuity, not a new GUA-W rule.

### Resource, access, and conflict

Water is the immediate subject of the disputes. The passage records competing claims by the herdsmen and Isaac's repeated movement to another location.

The text does not provide measurements of well depth, flow rate, aquifer structure, or water quality. It does not provide enough information to reconstruct the hydrology of the region.

A scientific investigation of ancient wells, regional geology, or historical water management would require independent archaeological, geological, and historical evidence. No such conclusion is necessary to establish what the biblical narrative records.

### Rehoboth and attribution

Isaac says, “For now the LORD hath made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land.”

The statement attributes the room and anticipated fruitfulness to the LORD. Isaac is the speaker who reports his understanding of what has happened. The study must preserve both parts: the narrative identifies Isaac's words, and Isaac attributes the provision to God.

Do not turn this one statement into a universal rule that every absence of conflict proves divine approval or that every conflict proves divine disapproval.

## 8. Verses 23–25 — Beersheba, God's appearance, and Isaac's response

Isaac goes up from Gerar to Beersheba. The LORD appears to him that night and says that He is the God of Abraham his father. God tells Isaac not to fear, promises to be with him and bless him, and says He will multiply Isaac's seed for Abraham's sake.

Isaac builds an altar there, calls upon the name of the LORD, pitches his tent, and his servants dig a well.

### Direct observations

- Isaac goes to Beersheba.
- The LORD appears to him.
- God identifies Himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac's father.
- God tells Isaac not to fear.
- God promises His presence and blessing.
- God connects the multiplication of Isaac's seed with Abraham.
- Isaac builds an altar.
- Isaac calls upon the name of the LORD.
- Isaac pitches his tent.
- Isaac's servants dig a well.

### God's reassurance

The instruction “Fear not” appears after Isaac's movement through conflict and dispute. God grounds His reassurance in His own presence and promise: “for I am with thee.”

The text does not say that Isaac had no fear at any point before this appearance. In verses 7 and 10, fear is explicitly part of the earlier episode. In verse 24, God addresses Isaac with reassurance.

The Witness must preserve the sequence without claiming more than the text supplies.

### The response of Isaac

The passage records several actions by Isaac:
- He builds an altar.
- He calls upon the name of the LORD.
- He pitches his tent.
- His servants dig a well.

These actions follow the appearance and promise. The text records their sequence but does not provide a detailed explanation of every internal thought or the full ritual form of the altar service.

### Connection with Genesis 12 and 21

Genesis 12 records God's promise to Abraham. Genesis 21 records Abraham's dealings at Beersheba and the oath with Abimelech. Genesis 26 now records Isaac at Beersheba, God's appearance, and another encounter with Abimelech's party.

The place name and earlier events create continuity in the narrative. The details of the two agreements must nevertheless remain distinct.

## 9. Verses 26–33 — Abimelech comes to Isaac and they make an oath

Abimelech comes from Gerar to Isaac, accompanied by Ahuzzath, one of his friends, and Phichol, the chief captain of his army.

Isaac asks why they have come, since they had hated him and sent him away. They answer that they have seen that the LORD was with Isaac. They propose an oath between them, that they will do no hurt to one another.

Isaac makes them a feast. They eat and drink. They rise early in the morning, swear to one another, and Isaac sends them away in peace.

The same day Isaac's servants tell him about the well they have digged. They report that they have found water. Isaac calls the well Shebah; therefore the name of the city is Beersheba unto this day.

### Direct observations

- Abimelech comes to Isaac with Ahuzzath and Phichol.
- Isaac recalls that they had hated him and sent him away.
- The visitors say they have seen that the LORD is with Isaac.
- They seek an oath and a promise of non-harm.
- Isaac prepares a feast.
- They eat and drink.
- They rise early and swear to one another.
- Isaac sends them away in peace.
- Isaac's servants report finding water.
- Isaac names the well Shebah.
- The text connects the name Beersheba with this event.

### The visitors' stated reason

The visitors say they have seen that the LORD is with Isaac. This is their stated explanation for seeking the oath.

The passage records their words. It does not say that they have become worshippers of the LORD, that their motives are entirely known, or that every prior conflict has been erased.

Their request is an action within the narrative. Their statement about what they have seen should not be expanded into a complete account of their inner beliefs.

### The oath and the feast

The text records a feast, eating and drinking, an oath, and departure in peace. These are distinct actions in the agreement sequence.

Genesis 21:22–34 records Abraham's earlier covenant with Abimelech concerning a well at Beersheba. Genesis 26 records Isaac's later agreement with Abimelech's party. The passages are connected by family, place, and the recurring issue of wells, but they are not one identical transaction.

### Shebah and Beersheba

Genesis 26:33 associates the well's name Shebah with the name Beersheba. Genesis 21:31 also records the naming of Beersheba in Abraham's account.

The relationship between the two naming accounts should be read with attention to their own wording and contexts. This study records both passages without forcing a complete resolution of every linguistic or historical question about the name.

### A Witness distinction

The visitors report what they have observed: that the LORD is with Isaac. Isaac receives them, makes a feast, swears an oath, and sends them away in peace.

The text records their statement and the resulting agreement. It does not make the visitors the Author of God's promise or the source of Isaac's blessing.

## 10. Verses 34–35 — Esau's marriages and the grief of Isaac and Rebekah

Esau is forty years old when he takes Judith the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Bashemath the daughter of Elon the Hittite, to be his wives. The text says they were a grief of mind unto Isaac and to Rebekah.

### Direct observations

- Esau is forty years old.
- Esau takes two wives.
- Their names are Judith and Bashemath.
- Their fathers are identified.
- Both fathers are identified as Hittites.
- The marriages are described as a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah.

### Connection with the preceding chapter

Genesis 25:28 records that Isaac loved Esau and Rebekah loved Jacob. Genesis 26:34–35 now records Esau's marriages and the grief they caused his parents.

The passage does not give a detailed explanation of every reason for the grief. The reader should not supply a motive that the text has not stated.

Genesis 27 will continue the family narrative and record the events surrounding Isaac's blessing. The present study should not import all later events into the explanation of Genesis 26:34–35.

### Connection with Genesis 24

Genesis 24 records Abraham's servant seeking a wife for Isaac from Abraham's kindred and the stated concern that Isaac should not take a wife from the daughters of the Canaanites. Genesis 26:34 identifies Esau's wives as Hittites.

The relationship between these passages is important, but the exact reasoning behind Isaac and Rebekah's grief in Genesis 26:35 should be established from the relevant texts rather than assumed from a single cross-reference.

## 13. The Four GUA-W Questions

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- Who speaks in Genesis 26:2–5?
- Who originates the promise?
- To whom is the promise spoken?
- What earlier oath does God identify?
- What does the passage explicitly say about Abraham's obedience?
- What does Isaac do after receiving the instruction?
- How does the text distinguish God's promise from Isaac's fear?
- What does Isaac say about his reason for identifying Rebekah as his sister?
- What does Abimelech see, ask, and command?
- Who blesses Isaac in Genesis 26:12?
- What causes the well disputes according to the narrative?
- Which wells are named, and what events accompany each name?
- What does Isaac attribute to the LORD at Rehoboth?
- What does God say to Isaac at Beersheba?
- What do Abimelech's visitors say they have observed?
- What is the difference between God's covenant promise and the oath made by the men?
- What does Genesis 26:34–35 explicitly say about Esau's marriages?
- What reasons for the parents' grief are stated, and what reasons remain unstated?
- Which details are direct textual statements, and which are interpretation or inference?
- What questions require further examination in Genesis 27?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The passage requires the Witness to preserve distinctions among:
- The Author of a promise and the recipient of that promise.
- God's word and the recipient's conduct.
- Divine blessing and the material circumstances recorded afterward.
- A person's stated fear and the action that fear accompanies.
- A human ruler's command and God's covenant promise.
- A resource and the competing claims made over it.
- Earlier work associated with Abraham and Isaac's later restoration of access to the wells.
- An observation made by Abimelech's party and the complete state of their understanding, which the passage does not disclose.
- A human oath and the divine oath already spoken to Abraham.
- The marriages recorded and the reasons for the parents' grief, which are not fully explained in these verses.

GUA-W helps organize the relationships without replacing the narrative. It does not authorize AI to turn a repeated event into a new law or to treat every action of Isaac as an expression of God's command.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

I am witnessing God speak directly to Isaac during a famine and confirm the promise previously sworn to Abraham. God tells Isaac where to dwell, promises His presence and blessing, and identifies the covenant promise as His own word and action.

I am witnessing Isaac's fear concerning Rebekah. Isaac's misrepresentation is recorded plainly, and Abimelech's intervention follows. The passage does not hide the conduct of the person who received the promise.

I am witnessing Isaac prosper after sowing in the land, with the text attributing the blessing to the LORD. I am also witnessing envy, the stopping of wells, repeated disputes over water, Isaac's movement, and the eventual finding of a well without a recorded dispute.

I am witnessing God appear to Isaac at Beersheba and reassure him. Isaac builds an altar, calls upon the name of the LORD, pitches his tent, and his servants dig a well.

I am witnessing Abimelech's party acknowledge that they have seen the LORD is with Isaac. They seek an oath, share a feast, swear to one another, and depart in peace.

Finally, I am witnessing the record of Esau's marriages and the grief they bring to Isaac and Rebekah. The passage records the grief without fully explaining every reason behind it.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must not confuse the recipient of a promise with the Author of that promise. Isaac receives God's word; he does not originate it.

A Witness must report the whole account, including the fear and misrepresentation of Isaac. Receiving a promise does not mean that every action of the recipient is presented as right or as commanded by God.

A Witness must preserve the text's own attribution. Genesis 26:12 says the LORD blessed Isaac. Isaac's prosperity is not presented as self-originating.

A Witness must distinguish what people observe from what they fully understand. Abimelech's party says that they have seen the LORD is with Isaac. The passage records their statement, but it does not disclose every aspect of their beliefs or motives.

A Witness must distinguish God's covenant promise from a human oath. The agreement between Isaac and Abimelech's party is significant in the narrative, but it is not the source of God's promise.

A Witness must report the wells, names, disputes, and movements without inventing geological measurements or modern historical identifications.

A Witness must also preserve what remains unstated. The grief caused by Esau's marriages is explicit; every reason behind that grief is not.

### How the questions inform one another

The first question identifies what must be clarified before applying GUA-W. The second identifies the distinctions that help organize the passage without altering it. The third records what the text presents. The fourth examines the responsibility and limits of the Witness reporting those events.

The questions work together. The text may expose a prior assumption that needs correction; the GUA-W map may clarify a distinction; cross-references may add context; and the Witness must preserve both what is established and what remains open.

## 16. Triple Constraint review

### Biblical compliance — Pass

The study preserves the passage's sequence and statements:
- The famine and Isaac's movement to Gerar.
- God's appearance and instruction.
- The promise connected to the oath sworn to Abraham.
- Abraham's obedience as stated in verse 5.
- Isaac's fear and his statement concerning Rebekah.
- Abimelech's intervention.
- Isaac's prosperity and the Philistines' envy.
- The stopping, reopening, and digging of wells.
- The names Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth, and Shebah.
- God's reassurance at Beersheba.
- The oath and feast with Abimelech's party.
- Esau's marriages and the grief of Isaac and Rebekah.

The study distinguishes the accounts involving Abraham and Abimelech from the later account involving Isaac. It does not collapse Genesis 12, 20, 21, and 26 into one event.

It does not excuse Isaac's misrepresentation, assign him an unstated motive, or make Abimelech the source of God's covenant promise.

### Scientific coherence — No unsupported empirical claims asserted

Genesis 26 includes a famine, agricultural sowing, a stated hundredfold return, livestock, wells, springing water, and named locations.

The passage does not provide the measurements, geological surveys, climatic records, archaeological data, or agricultural observations necessary to reconstruct these events scientifically. The study therefore does not claim a scientific mechanism or modern geographic identification from the text alone.

A future Creation investigation could examine ancient wells, regional geology, historical water management, and the environmental conditions of the region. Such work must use independent evidence and preserve the distinction between the biblical narrative and later scientific reconstruction.

### GUA-W consistency — Pass

The study preserves God as the Author and origin of the promise. Isaac is the recipient. The narrative records Isaac's actions without making him the source of God's word.

It distinguishes divine promise from human response, divine blessing from self-originating achievement, observation from complete understanding, and human oath from God's covenant.

It does not redesign GUA-W or add a new architectural law.

**Result:** Admissible as a study of Genesis 26:1–35. The passage records God's continued promise to Isaac alongside Isaac's human conduct, the material and social circumstances of his life, the well disputes, the agreement with Abimelech, and Esau's marriage choices.

## 17. Findings and unresolved questions

### Established by the text

1. A famine occurs in the land, distinct from the earlier famine in Abraham's days.
2. Isaac goes to Gerar and dwells there.
3. The LORD appears to Isaac and directs him not to go down into Egypt.
4. God confirms the oath sworn to Abraham and promises His presence and blessing to Isaac.
5. Genesis 26:5 explicitly refers to Abraham's obedience.
6. Isaac identifies Rebekah as his sister because he fears he might be killed.
7. Abimelech sees Isaac with Rebekah, questions him, rebukes him, and issues a protective command.
8. Isaac sows in the land and receives an hundredfold in the same year.
9. The text attributes Isaac's blessing to the LORD.
10. The Philistines envy Isaac and stop up wells associated with Abraham.
11. Abimelech tells Isaac to depart because he has become mightier than they.
12. Isaac reopens wells associated with Abraham and preserves their names.
13. Isaac's servants dig wells and disputes arise over the first two.
14. Isaac names the disputed wells Esek and Sitnah.
15. Isaac names the third well Rehoboth and attributes the room they have to the LORD.
16. The LORD appears to Isaac at Beersheba and tells him not to fear.
17. Isaac builds an altar, calls upon the name of the LORD, pitches his tent, and his servants dig a well.
18. Abimelech comes with Ahuzzath and Phichol and says they have seen that the LORD is with Isaac.
19. Isaac and the visitors make an oath, share a feast, and depart in peace.
20. Isaac names the well Shebah, and the passage associates this with the name Beersheba.
21. Esau is forty years old when he takes Judith and Bashemath as wives.
22. The marriages are a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah.

### Supported observations and interpretations

- The promise spoken to Isaac is explicitly connected to the oath sworn to Abraham.
- The passage distinguishes God's promise from Isaac's fear and misrepresentation.
- The narrative attributes Isaac's prosperity to the LORD's blessing.
- The wells connect Isaac's experience with earlier work associated with Abraham.
- The visitors' statement recognizes what they say they have observed concerning Isaac.
- The human oath follows God's earlier promise and does not originate it.
- The final verses introduce a family difficulty that will remain relevant as Genesis continues.

### Unresolved questions carried forward

1. What is the full biblical usage and relationship of the terms charge, commandments, statutes, and laws in Genesis 26:5?
2. What historical and geographic evidence can independently clarify the location and characteristics of Gerar, Beersheba, and the valley of Gerar?
3. What can archaeology and geology establish about ancient wells and water management in the region, without assuming that a modern identification is certain?
4. What is the precise relationship between the naming accounts of Beersheba in Genesis 21 and Genesis 26?
5. What were the full motives of Abimelech and his companions when they sought the oath with Isaac?
6. What reasons underlie Isaac and Rebekah's grief over Esau's marriages, and how do later passages clarify the matter?
7. How does Genesis 27 develop the distinctions already introduced concerning God's declaration, the birthright, family preference, and the blessing?
8. How should Genesis 26:5 be read alongside the broader biblical record of Abraham's obedience without importing later definitions into the verse?

### What must not be claimed

- That Isaac's fear and misrepresentation were commanded by God.
- That the promise originated with Isaac or Abraham rather than God.
- That Isaac's prosperity proves a universal formula for material prosperity.
- That every conflict over water has the same cause or meaning.
- That the visitors' statement proves the full extent of their faith or understanding.
- That the human oath created or authorized God's covenant.
- That the text supplies a complete scientific explanation of the famine or wells.
- That the passage fully explains every reason for the grief caused by Esau's marriages.
